I started fixing bicycles for the children on my street because I was good at it and they were not. That is the honest version. My father kept a box of tools under the stairs, and by fourteen I could true a wheel, patch a tube, and straighten a derailleur. The bikes on our lane were hand-me-downs with rusted chains and soft brake cables. I would take one, sit on the front step for twenty minutes, and hand it back working. They would say thanks and ride off. I liked how that felt.

I believed I was doing something generous. I also believed, without ever saying it out loud, that the fastest help is the best help, and that the point of knowing how to do something is to do it for people who cannot.

The girl who changed my mind was called Priya and she was nine. Her chain came off constantly because her rear derailleur was bent, and she brought the bike over maybe four times in one month. The fourth time I reached for it and she did not let go of the handlebars. She said she wanted to watch. I said it was quicker if I just did it. She said she knew, and kept holding on.

So we did it slowly. I showed her the bent hanger, crouching behind the wheel to sight along the cogs. I let her turn the pedals while I pushed the cable. She got grease across her forehead. It took fifty minutes instead of twelve, and at the end she asked me what the small screws were called, and I realised I did not know. I had been calling them the small screws for two years.

That is the part I keep coming back to. Not the moment of teaching her, but the moment immediately after, when I understood that being able to do something and being able to explain it are separate skills, and I had only ever bothered with one of them. My speed had been hiding something from me as well as from her.

After that I changed how I did it, though not immediately and not perfectly. I still fix things too fast when I am tired. But mostly now I ask first whether the person wants it fixed or wants to learn, and I have stopped assuming the answer. Priya can do her own chain. Two of the younger boys can patch a tube badly, which is still patching a tube. There is a shoebox on our step with tyre levers in it that anyone can take.

What I questioned was not really a belief about bicycles. It was a belief about what generosity looks like. I had thought it looked like competence arriving quickly. It turns out it more often looks like standing there with your hands in your pockets, watching somebody do it worse than you would have, and not saying anything.
