My mom started the garden in our backyard three years ago when the landscaper broke his leg and we couldn't afford to replace him immediately. She bought seeds from the dollar store and planted them in rows. Tomatoes, carrots, some kind of squash that she says came from her mother's garden thirty years ago. I helped because she asked me to, not because I particularly cared about vegetables.

For two years it worked. We'd get tomatoes in August that tasted like nothing from the grocery store could ever taste like. My dad would make salsa on a Saturday afternoon. It was fine. It was a nice thing but it wasn't essential. I wasn't someone who cared about gardening. That was my mom's thing.

Then the drought hit. I don't know if it was officially a drought or if that's just what people called it when it didn't rain for six weeks and the water department sent emails about conservation. The garden started dying. The tomato plants got brown and brittle. The carrots looked like someone had turned them to leather. My mom kept watering them anyway, dragging the hose out every evening even though it felt wasteful. She'd stand there in the heat and pour water on plants that looked like they were already dead. It seemed pointless.

I told her that. I said we should just let them go. We can buy vegetables from the store. It's not worth the water. We can replant next year when it rains again. She didn't argue. She just nodded like she'd heard this before, which she probably had, from my dad or my sister or her own brain telling her to give up. But she kept going out there anyway.

One afternoon in July I came home from work and she was kneeling in the dirt next to a tomato plant that looked absolutely dead. Like brown stick dead. And she was checking the soil and adjusting the drip line and saying something to the plant that I couldn't hear. I almost walked past her. Actually I did walk past her. But then I came back outside and asked what she was doing.

She said she read that during droughts, if you keep watering deeply but less frequently, the plants send their roots deeper to find water. They get stronger. And maybe they don't produce as much fruit. Maybe they're not beautiful. But they survive. And the next year when it rains again, they're better adapted to handle the dry spells. They're more resilient.

We didn't get tomatoes that year. We got like five sad ones that tasted slightly better than store-bought. But we kept almost everything alive. And the next year, when it did rain again, the garden came back differently. The plants were smaller but tougher. They produced more than they ever had before, even in normal conditions.

I didn't think much about this at the time. It was just something that happened in my yard. But I'm different now from the person who told my mom to give up. Not because I suddenly care about tomatoes, though I kind of do now. But because I learned something about persistence from watching her stand in the heat and tend to plants that looked like failures. About how sometimes you have to keep investing in things that seem pointless in the moment because you're not measuring for today. You're measuring for next year.
