Every evening at six, for as long as I can remember, my grandfather pulled his harmonium out from behind the couch and played for twenty minutes before dinner. Same time, almost every day, rain or heat or the one time the power went out and he played anyway by the light of his phone propped against the bellows. I used to sit cross-legged next to him doing homework, half listening, until the year I turned eleven and he handed me the bellows side and said, without much ceremony, that my hands were finally big enough to pump and press keys at the same time.

He never used sheet music. He taught the way he'd been taught, by ear, humming a line and waiting for me to find it on the keys, correcting me with a tap on my wrist when I landed a half-step off. The harmonium itself is older than my mother — a boxy wooden thing with a crack along the side that he glued back together twice, and a middle C-sharp reed that buzzes slightly no matter how many times it's been serviced, so that every song we play carries this one small imperfection in exactly the same place. I know that buzz better than I know most things about myself.

When his hands started shaking too much to manage the stops reliably, I didn't decide to keep the six o'clock habit going. It just kept going, the way habits do when nobody announces they're ending. I started playing while he sang, then playing the melody while he hummed along under his breath, then eventually playing alone in the next room while he listened from his chair, correcting me by voice instead of by touch. Twenty minutes a day, for seven years now, on an instrument most people my age have never heard of, let alone heard played.

I don't know how to explain what this means for a campus that doesn't already have anyone doing it, except to say that I've watched a two-hundred-year-old kind of music survive inside one small daily habit, passed hand to hand instead of downloaded or streamed, and I think that's a specific, useful thing to bring somewhere new. I'd bring the actual harmonium, cracked side and buzzing reed and all. I'd play in a common room some evening the way my grandfather played in our living room, not performing exactly, just doing the thing at six o'clock because that's when it happens, and I'd teach anyone curious enough to sit down next to me the way he taught me, by ear, correcting a half-step at a time. Whatever community forms around that, badly played at first and slowly less so, wouldn't exist there without me carrying it in.
