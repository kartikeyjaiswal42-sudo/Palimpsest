The camera was my uncle's, a Russian Zenit he'd carried through two decades and never once cleaned, and it had no light meter, which meant every exposure was a guess I made with my thumb and my gut and a rule of thumb he taught me over the phone called "sunny sixteen" that I never fully trusted. I joined the community darkroom at the rec center on Fifth Street because they let anyone use the enlargers for two dollars an hour, and because the woman who ran it, Odette, didn't care that I showed up with a camera that looked like it belonged in a museum.

The darkroom people were not like anyone at my school. There was a retired mail carrier who shot only stray cats. A nursing student who photographed her patients' hands, with permission, because she said hands told you more than faces did. A man everyone called Big Theo who had one working eye and somehow took the sharpest pictures in the room. I was the youngest by about thirty years, and for the first few months I mostly listened, developed my rolls badly, and tried to look like I belonged.

Odette asked me to shoot the center's spring fundraiser, the one event all year that actually paid for new chemicals and paper. I said yes before I thought it through. I told myself the sunny-sixteen rule would carry me through an evening event held half outside and half in a gymnasium with terrible fluorescent light, because it had carried me through a few backyard barbecues that spring. It did not carry me through this. I shot four rolls, thirty-six frames each, guessing my exposures in a room where the light changed every time someone opened the gym doors, and when I developed them two nights later, alone, at eleven at night because I couldn't wait, almost every frame from indoors came back either black or so overexposed the faces had dissolved into white.

I had one good roll, the outdoor one, taken in daylight where the rule actually worked. Everything that mattered to Odette — the dance floor, the silent auction table, the moment the center's founder cut the ribbon on the new roof — was gone, burned into nothing by my own overconfidence in a rule that only worked under the conditions it was built for.

I told her before she asked. I remember standing in the doorway of the darkroom with the contact sheets in my hand, watching her face go through disappointment fast and then something gentler, and she said, "Well, now you know what a meter's for," and put me on developing duty for the rest of the semester instead of shooting duty, which felt at the time like a demotion and now feels like the moment I actually became part of that room instead of a guest in it.

I still don't own a light meter. I use my phone for exposure readings now, which Big Theo calls cheating and Odette calls common sense, and the argument between the two of them is one of my favorite sounds in that building. I think about the ruined rolls more than I think about any photograph I've taken since, because they're the reason anyone there trusts me with a camera at all.
