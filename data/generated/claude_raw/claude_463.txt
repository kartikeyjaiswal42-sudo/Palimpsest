Andriy came to live with us in September of my sophomore year, seventeen years old like I am now, from a city outside Kharkiv that I had to look up on a map the week his host application landed on our kitchen table. He arrived eight months after the war started, quiet in a way that took me weeks to understand wasn't shyness. By December he was sleeping in the room next to mine, eating breakfast across from me every morning, correcting my pronunciation of his last name with a patience that never seemed to run out, and I had started thinking of him, without really deciding to, as something close to a brother.

The disagreement started small and never really got resolved, not by the time he flew home in June and not, I think, in the year and a half since. I wanted him to try out for the school's soccer team. He was good — good enough that our coach noticed him juggling a ball in the courtyard during lunch and asked me, twice, to bring him around to a practice. I pushed. I framed it as a way for him to make friends outside our house, a way to build a life here instead of just enduring one, because that's how I would have wanted someone to push me if our positions were reversed. What I did not understand, or refused to understand, was that for him, joining something that visible, that social, that photographed-for-the-yearbook, felt like agreeing to perform a kind of normalcy he did not have inside him yet.

He told me once, the only time he came close to explaining it directly, that playing on a team here would mean smiling in team photos while his cousin was somewhere he couldn't get updates from for days at a stretch, and that he didn't know how to do both of those things honestly at the same time. I told him nobody expected him to be constantly grieving, that living a normal life wasn't a betrayal of anyone, that my own grandfather had told me once that the dead would want you to keep living loudly, not quietly. I meant it as comfort. I think now it landed as a lecture from someone who had never had to test whether that idea actually holds up when it's your own family the sentence is about.

We had some version of this argument maybe six or seven times that year, always circling the same two positions, never quite landing anywhere new. He never joined the team. I never fully stopped bringing it up, though I got better, eventually, at noticing when to drop it — usually too late, usually after his face had already closed a little. Neither of us apologized for our side of it, exactly, because neither of us thought we were wrong, and both of us, I think, were partly right.

What I've learned from it, if I've learned anything, is less a clean lesson than a permanent unease, which might be the more honest kind of learning anyway. I used to think good advice was good advice regardless of who received it, that a true thing said kindly enough would eventually be accepted as true. Andriy taught me, without meaning to teach me anything, that some situations don't have a right answer sitting underneath them waiting to be found — that his caution and my push toward normal life could both be reasonable responses to something I will never have to live through myself, and that respecting him meant, at some point, letting the disagreement just sit there unfinished instead of needing to win it.

He still messages me sometimes, usually late at night his time, usually about nothing important — a song, a photo of his dog, a complaint about a professor. We have never gone back to the soccer conversation directly, though it sits underneath a lot of what we don't say. I don't know if I was right to push him or wrong to keep pushing after he'd made his answer clear. I know that loving someone doesn't automatically make you capable of understanding what they need, and that the two things — the love and the understanding — sometimes have to grow at very different speeds.
