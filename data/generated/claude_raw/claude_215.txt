They tore down the court on Bellmore Street in June, the one with the crooked rim that made every shot bank in from the left whether you meant it to or not, the one where I learned to play and later taught my younger cousin to play, replaced almost overnight by chain-link fencing and a permit notice zip-tied to the post where the backboard used to hang.

I went to the city council meeting that August mostly to be angry in public, honestly, expecting a room of bored adults and a five-minute public comment slot where I'd say my piece about a lost neighborhood landmark and go home having accomplished nothing except venting. I did not expect to sit through the entire two-hour meeting, but I did, because somewhere around the second agenda item, listening to a planning official explain the actual zoning process that had turned that court into a future six-unit building, something shifted in how I was listening.

I had walked in assuming the decision was some faceless act of neglect, a court nobody valued being erased by nobody in particular. What I heard instead was a genuinely complicated, almost fascinating account of density requirements, a housing shortage the city had been tracking for a decade, a parcel that had technically been zoned for redevelopment since before I was born, and a public comment period two years earlier that almost nobody from our block had shown up to, myself included, because none of us had known to look for it. My anger didn't fully disappear. It changed shape, though, from something aimed at an anonymous "they" into something closer to real curiosity about how these decisions actually get made, who gets a say, and why the people most affected are so often the last ones paying attention.

That's the moment I'd point to if I had to name one — sitting in a folding chair in a municipal building at nine at night, watching a zoning map projected on a screen, realizing I had spent seventeen years walking past decisions like this one without any idea they were decisions at all, made by real people in rooms exactly like this, following rules I'd never bothered to read. I went home and looked up our city's zoning code that same night and read for almost three hours, well past when I meant to stop, cross-referencing parcel numbers against a map until I understood roughly how our whole neighborhood had been carved up decades before any of us moved in.

I still don't know if I think the condo building is a bad idea. Some nights I do, mostly when I miss the specific sound the old rim made. Other nights I think about how badly this city needs housing and I'm not sure a basketball court, however beloved, should automatically win that argument. What I do know is that I now read city council agendas the way some people read box scores, and I've started going to the meetings regularly, not to be angry anymore but because I actually want to understand what's coming before the fence goes up around it.

I turn to the planning official from that first meeting now, a woman named Ms. Okonkwo who answers my emails patiently and let me shadow her for a day in the fall, and to the zoning code itself, dense and badly formatted, which I somehow now find more absorbing than most of my actual homework. I never got the court back. I got something else instead, a way of seeing the neighborhood that I don't think I can unsee now, for better or worse.
