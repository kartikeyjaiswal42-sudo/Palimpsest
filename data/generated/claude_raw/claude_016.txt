The bracket is still in my desk drawer. It is a piece of 3mm aluminium about the size of a playing card, with four holes drilled in it and one corner filed down where it used to catch on the servo horn. I keep it because it is the smallest object I own that proves I was wrong about something important.

For most of two years I believed that our robotics team's problem was a lack of ambition. We were a small school with a small budget, and every design meeting ended with someone saying we should keep it simple. I hated that phrase. Simple felt like a word people used when they had already decided to lose. So when I became build lead in Year 12, I pushed for a four-bar lifting arm with a rotating gripper, which meant more joints, more moving parts, and more brackets like this one holding it all together. Priya, who had been on the team longer than me, said we were building something we wouldn't have time to test. I told her testing was what the last week was for.

The bracket was the piece I made myself, on a Tuesday, in the workshop after everyone had gone home. I cut it, drilled it, filed the corner, and bolted it into the arm. It held the second joint. It worked in the workshop. It worked in the corridor outside the workshop, which we used as a practice field because it was the only space long enough. It worked eleven times in a row the night before we drove to Coventry.

On stage, in the second match, the arm rose about forty centimetres and then stopped. The bracket had not broken. That is the part I did not expect. It had bent — a soft, almost polite curve, maybe five degrees — because the competition floor had a lip at the edge of the field that our corridor did not have, and the extra jolt of going over it put a load on the joint at an angle I had never modelled. Five degrees was enough to bind the servo. We scored eleven points across three matches. Teams with two-motor scoop designs, the kind of thing I would have called unambitious, scored sixty.

What I had actually believed, I realised on the drive home, was not that complexity was good. I had believed that being cautious was a character flaw, and that the people around me who wanted fewer parts wanted less of everything, including less of themselves. That was an insult I had been carrying quietly for two years, and it was wrong. Priya wasn't unambitious. She had simply understood something I hadn't: that every part you add is another thing that must be true for the whole to work, and you only have so many hours to make things true.

I did not stop wanting to build difficult machines. What changed is that I now ask a different question first. Not "can this work" but "how many ways can this fail, and have I seen the floor it will fail on." This year we built a two-joint arm instead of four. It is less impressive on paper. It went eight matches without stopping.

I kept the bracket because it did not fail dramatically. It bent five degrees. Most of the things I get wrong will look like that: not a break, just a small quiet deviation from what I assumed, somewhere I never thought to check. The bracket sits in the drawer to remind me to go and look.
