We lost the semi-final by a single ballot. Two-one. I found out standing in a corridor at a school in Sheffield that smelled like floor polish and cheap deodorant, holding a paper cup of squash, and my first thought was not about the loss at all. My first thought was: my dad is going to want to know which judge.

He did. He asked in the car before we got out of the car park.

Here is the thing about my father. He is not a pushy parent in the way that phrase usually means. He never made me do debating. He came to it because I did, and then he read about it obsessively, the way he does with everything, and by the time I was fifteen he could break down a first proposition case better than most of my teachers. He would sit at the back of rooms with a notebook. Afterwards, in the car, we would go through it line by line. I loved that. I want to be clear, because of where this is going, that I loved that.

The loss itself was not complicated. We were opposition, the motion was about mandatory voting, and about eight minutes in I realised our opponents had made a genuinely good point — that low turnout among the poorest voters is not a preference, it is a barrier, and compulsion removes the barrier. It was better than the argument I had prepared against it. And I did the thing you are trained to do. I did not engage with it. I reframed, I dodged, I attacked a weaker version of it that they had not said, and I did it smoothly enough that two judges bought it and one did not. The one who did not wrote on her ballot: *Opposition second speaker never actually answered the barrier point.* She was right.

I have that ballot. It is in a folder under my bed.

What I told my father in the car was that we lost. What I did not tell him, for about four months, was that I thought we deserved to.

The change did not happen in a moment, which I know is the wrong shape for this kind of essay. It happened over a season. I started, in practice rounds, doing something that our coach found irritating and my partner found actively dangerous: when the other side said something that was true, I said so. Out loud. Then I argued that it was not sufficient. That is a much harder speech to give. You are handing your opponent a sentence they can quote. You are also, and this took me a year to understand, the only person in the room who now looks like they are telling the truth.

My results got worse for a term and then better. Not the point, but I want to be honest that it worked out, because otherwise this is a story about virtue and it isn't.

The unresolved part is my dad.

He thinks I have talked myself into a handicap. He is not rude about it. He says things like, "You conceded the whole framework there," and, "You know they weren't going to give you credit for being fair." Last November, after a round I lost, he said that competitive debating is a game with rules and I had decided to play a different game and then be disappointed by the score. And I said — this came out worse than I meant it — that I did not want to get good at not answering questions, because I could already see what that turns into, and I would rather lose.

He went quiet. Then he said, "That's not what I taught you."

And that's it. That's where we are. We still go. He still sits at the back with the notebook, and the notes are still the best coaching I get, and I still read them twice. But there is a category of comment I now skip past, and he knows I skip past it, and neither of us has said so.

I do not think he is wrong about the game. He is right that the rules reward the thing I stopped doing. What I cannot make him see, or maybe what I cannot say properly, is that I was becoming very fast at a specific manoeuvre — hearing something true and instantly locating the angle that makes it disappear — and that I did not want to find out who I would be at thirty if I kept practising it four hours a week.

We had a round in Manchester in February. Motion about assisted dying. I conceded a point early, on purpose, in front of him. We won.

In the car he said the judges must have liked me. Which might be a compliment. I have decided to take it as one.
