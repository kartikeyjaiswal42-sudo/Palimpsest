For fourteen months, my dad got up at 6:15, ironed one of his four dress shirts, and sat at the kitchen table by 6:45 with coffee and his laptop open to job boards, dressed like he had somewhere to be. He did not have anywhere to be. He'd been laid off from Halvorsen Machine Tool in March of my sophomore year, after nineteen years there, and for over a year there was no somewhere.

I want to say I found this admirable from the start. I didn't. For a long stretch I thought it was sad, honestly, and I told him so, once, badly, on a Tuesday in the fall when I came downstairs for school and saw him at that table in a pressed blue shirt with his cufflinks in, reading want ads for jobs he was overqualified for. I said something like, why do you get dressed up for nobody, Dad, you're not fooling anyone, you could just wear sweatpants like a normal person going through this. I remember exactly how it landed, because his face went very still, and he just said, I'm not doing it for anybody else.

I respected my dad's opinions more than almost anyone's. He'd run a floor of forty machinists for two decades and never once, in my whole life, seemed unsure of anything. So when he disagreed with me that morning — calmly, not even raising his voice — it shook me more than if a stranger had said the same thing back. I went to school still thinking I was right. Getting dressed for a job search that was going nowhere seemed, to me, like a kind of denial. I thought the honest thing, the healthy thing, was to let the front down and just be sad in your pajamas for a while, like everyone else I knew would have done.

It took me a long time, and one specific morning in January, to understand what he meant. I came down earlier than usual and caught him before he'd started the ritual — he was still in his undershirt, standing at the counter, and for just a second before he noticed me there, he looked like someone I didn't fully recognize. Not sad exactly. Loose. Unanchored is maybe the word for it. Then he saw me, and about ninety seconds later he was upstairs getting the blue shirt, and by the time I left for school he was back at the table, dressed, coffee poured, looking like my dad again.

That's when I got it. The shirt wasn't for the job search. It wasn't even really for us. It was the thing standing between him and that loose, unanchored version of himself he'd caught me seeing by accident. Nineteen years of getting up and being someone specific at a specific time had built a shape, and the shape didn't disappear just because the job did. If he'd taken my advice and let it go, I think something in him would have gone with it that might not have come back so easily.

He found a position in April, at a smaller company, for less money, and he still irons a shirt every morning, though now, at least, there's somewhere to wear it. I never told him I was wrong that Tuesday morning in the fall. I don't think I need to; I think he knew I'd figure it out eventually, the way you know a kid will understand a thing when they're ready and not one day before.

What I took from disagreeing with him isn't really about unemployment, or about shirts. It's that some habits look pointless from the outside because you judge them by what they produce, and my dad's habit was never producing a job. It was producing him, every single morning, on schedule, whether or not the day ahead had earned the effort.
