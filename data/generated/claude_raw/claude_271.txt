The crowbar slipped for the third time and caught me across the knuckles, and I sat back on my heels in the garage and said a word my mother would not have approved of. In front of me was the pallet I'd dragged home from behind the hardware store two weekends earlier, half its slats already pried loose and stacked in a pile that looked less like lumber and more like kindling. I was trying to build a coffee table. I had watched maybe four videos on how to do this, and none of them mentioned that the nails in a real pallet are galvanized and bent over on the underside specifically to punish anyone who tries to remove them cleanly.

My dad stuck his head in around six and asked if I wanted help. I said no, mostly out of pride, and he left me to it. By then I'd figured out that fighting the nails was pointless and started cutting around them instead, and the boards I ended up with were shorter and more mismatched than planned. I sanded them anyway, kneeling on a folded towel with a block sander that ate through three sheets of sandpaper, because somewhere in that first hour I stopped caring whether the table came out even and started caring whether the wood felt right under my hand.

There is a particular quiet that happens when you're doing something with your hands that requires just enough attention to crowd out everything else. I didn't think about the physics test I'd bombed. I thought about grain direction. I thought about whether the gap between two boards would look intentional or just wrong, moved a clamp two inches to test it, and it looked intentional, and something in my chest unclenched a little.

The legs were the hardest part. I'd salvaged four stair spindles from a house down the street being renovated, thick turned wood that didn't match the pallet slats at all, and I liked that they didn't match. I attached them with lag bolts my dad helped me countersink — the one concession I made to needing another person — and when I flipped the table right-side up it wobbled on the uneven garage floor, so I shimmed one leg with a folded piece of cardboard, which is still there, six months later, doing its job.

It sits in my room now, under my window, holding a lamp, three mugs I keep forgetting to wash, and a stack of library books I haven't returned. The top isn't level if you set a marble on it — it rolls, slowly, toward the northeast corner, and I've never bothered to fix that. There's a burn mark near one edge where I set down a soldering iron without thinking, and a water ring from the first mug I left there overnight. I know exactly how the two long boards came from the same section of pallet and how the short one across the middle came from a different bin entirely, cut on a Tuesday when I'd given up trying to match anything.

Sometimes I catch myself running a thumb along the seam between those boards, feeling where the grain lines up and where it doesn't, thinking about the four hours it took to get the legs level, and about my dad in the doorway not offering to fix it until I asked. My friends have Ikea desks that arrived flat in a box with an Allen key taped to the instructions. Mine has a cardboard shim under one leg.
