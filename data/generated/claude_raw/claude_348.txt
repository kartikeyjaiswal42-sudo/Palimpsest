My father coached me from when I was six until I was twelve, and last year I took the under-11s at the same club, on the same pitch, with the same cones out of the same shed. We went two wins, one draw, thirteen losses. I have the sheet. I did not throw it away.

The disagreement started in November and we have not finished it.

My rule was that everyone played half the match. I did not announce it as a philosophy. I just wrote the sheet that way every Saturday, and when we were losing by two at halftime I still took Rahul off and put Freddie on, because Freddie had come to every training session in the rain and had never once complained about not playing.

My father watched from behind the goal, the way he does, hands in his coat. After the Ashwell game in January — we lost six-one and Freddie was on for the last twenty of it — he said in the car that I was being unfair to the good players in the name of being fair to the others, and that at eleven, children can tell the difference between being included and being managed, and that Freddie knew perfectly well he was on because of a rule and not because of anything he had done.

I said that was exactly backwards. I said Freddie showed up in the rain and that was something he had done.

We have had that conversation, in some version, maybe nine times. It always ends the same way, which is politely. He says I am kind. He does not mean it entirely as a compliment. I say he only remembers the winning teams. He does not deny it.

What I did next was smaller than winning an argument. I stopped defending the half-match rule in the abstract and started asking the boys. Not "do you want to play more", because every eleven-year-old says yes. I asked what the worst part of a Saturday was. Four of them said the same thing, and it was not the losing and it was not the bench. It was standing in the line for the first ten minutes of training not knowing what we were doing. So I rewrote training instead. Everything starts inside ninety seconds now, small-sided, no line.

We still lost. We lost the next four. But the sessions filled up, and by March I had nineteen boys at a Tuesday in February, which the club had not had in years, and the man who runs the juniors asked me what I was doing differently.

I do not think I have proved my father wrong. I think it is possible he is right about the matches and I am right about the Tuesdays, and that we are having two arguments that only sound like one. He has come to more training this season. He stands at the far side. Last week he moved a drill I had set up badly and I let him, and neither of us said anything about it, and I have decided that not saying anything is not the same as agreeing.
