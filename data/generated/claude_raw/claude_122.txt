Every morning before school, I would go to the nature preserve behind our neighborhood and sit by the water with a pair of binoculars and a notebook. I was looking for birds. That's what I told myself. But I think what I was really looking for was permission to be alone in a way that my parents wouldn't worry about. Birdwatching felt like a productive hobby, like self-improvement. It was better than just sitting by the water, being lonely.

I was sixteen, which felt too old to be as lonely as I was, so I constructed a narrative around it. I was a serious naturalist. I was building a life list. I was the kind of person who noticed things that other people walked past without seeing. This was true, mostly. But the bird watching was almost incidental to the fact of being somewhere that was entirely mine, where nobody could ask me why I looked sad.

I had a field guide and I had watched YouTube videos on bird identification and I knew about fifty species by their calls. The thing I didn't know—the thing I couldn't know from YouTube—was that knowing a bird and seeing a bird are completely different things. I would hear a call and flip through my guide frantically, trying to match sound to image, and by the time I figured out what I was listening to, the bird would be gone. I had probably seen thousands of birds that first year, and identified maybe fifty of them accurately.

There was this one morning in November when I was sure I saw a Pileated Woodpecker. I was certain. The bird was huge, with the red patch on its head, and it was there for maybe three seconds before it flew away. I sat there for an hour trying to sketch it from memory, trying to make sure I had the proportions right, trying to confirm what I knew I had seen.

When I looked it up later, I realized that Pileated Woodpeckers were extremely rare in our region. The probability that I had seen one was very low. The probability that I had misidentified something else was very high. But I put it in my notebook anyway. I entered it into my life list.

Three years later, in my senior year, I stopped going to the preserve. I stopped looking for birds. What changed wasn't that I realized I had probably never seen a Pileated Woodpecker. What changed was that I realized I had spent all that time alone not actually wanting to be alone. I was hiding from people, and I was calling it a hobby.

I still think about those mornings sometimes, and I wonder if any of the birds I saw were real, or if I had just wanted them to be real badly enough to make them so. I wonder if my whole life list was mostly false sightings, misidentifications, and wishful thinking. I think I've made my peace with that. I was a lonely sixteen-year-old with binoculars and a bad sense of perspective. I saw what I needed to see.

The thing I got wrong was believing that noticing things made me smarter or better than other people. The thing I still think about is how I used that as a shield. If I was a naturalist, I wasn't just lonely. I was dedicated. I was serious. I was the kind of person who had a private interior life.

Maybe I did. But I wish I had spent that time actually connecting with someone. I wish that one of those mornings I had asked someone to come with me, to help me identify what I was seeing. I wish I had let someone else into that private space before I had convinced myself that being alone in it was the whole point.
