I signed up to be a scribe for Devesh because a teacher asked for volunteers and I raised my hand without thinking much about it, expecting a straightforward job: he'd tell me what to write, I'd write it, we'd both go home. That is not remotely what it turned out to be, and the moment I understood that is the moment I actually started caring about the thing I've now spent three years captivated by.

Devesh is blind, has been since birth, and was taking the same eleventh-grade physics course I was, which meant I sat next to him twice a week writing down equations and diagrams he could not see and had to build entirely in his head from description alone. The first few sessions I did this badly. I'd say "draw a free body diagram" and then just draw one, silently, the way you'd take your own notes, forgetting completely that the whole point was that he needed it described, not just recorded.

He corrected me, patiently at first and then less patiently, and one afternoon he stopped me mid-sentence and said something like, you keep saying "here" and "this," and none of that means anything to me, you have to tell me where the arrow starts and where it ends and how long it is relative to the others. I remember feeling embarrassed, and then, almost immediately after, genuinely interested. Because to describe a diagram precisely enough for someone to reconstruct it exactly, you have to understand it far better than when you're just copying it down. I couldn't lean on the picture anymore. I had to understand what a force vector was doing, geometrically, well enough to narrate it.

That was the moment everything about the job changed for me. I stopped thinking of scribing as a favor I was doing for him and started thinking of it as maybe the hardest kind of studying I'd ever done — translation, basically, from a visual language into a spoken one, live, under time pressure, with someone immediately telling me when I'd gotten it wrong. I got better at it slowly. I learned to describe angles using clock positions, to give magnitudes before directions, to build a diagram the way you'd build a sentence, in an order that made sense read aloud rather than looked at.

Three years later I still do this, now for two other students, and it has become the thing I find myself thinking about at random moments — in the shower, on the bus — turning a graph or a chemical structure into words precise enough that someone who can't see it would still know exactly what it looks like. I go to Devesh sometimes with questions that have nothing to do with school anymore, just because describing things precisely, out loud, turned out to be the most absorbing problem I've ever stumbled into, the kind where an hour disappears and I don't notice until someone tells me it's time to go.
