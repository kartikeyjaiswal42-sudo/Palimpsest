Every Tuesday and Thursday for eleven years, at 4:30 in the afternoon, I sat down at the same piano bench in the same small studio above a dry cleaner's and played scales for the first ten minutes before my teacher, Ms. Feld, would let me touch anything that sounded like actual music. C major, then G, then D, around the circle, both hands, metronome ticking at a speed she'd slowly ratchet up over the years until my fingers moved faster than I consciously thought about them moving. I did this so many times that I could probably still do it half-asleep, and for a long time I assumed that ritual — the walk up the narrow stairs, the smell of dry-cleaning chemicals mixed with the studio's old carpet, the ten minutes of scales — was just the boring part I had to get through to reach the real thing.

I quit in March of this year. I told Ms. Feld first, before my parents, which she pointed out was unusual, and I said I didn't know why I did it that way either.

The community I want to talk about isn't the one you'd expect — not a youth orchestra, not a competition circuit, though I did those too. It's smaller and stranger than that. It's the studio itself, the rotating cast of maybe six or seven other students who shared that same narrow staircase over the years, whose lesson slots bordered mine, whom I saw for ninety seconds at a time twice a week for over a decade, in passing, one of us leaving as the other arrived. I knew almost nothing about most of them beyond a first name and whatever piece was currently propped on their stand. And yet I knew, with total certainty, when Owen finally nailed the Chopin nocturne he'd been stuck on for months, because I heard it through the door on my way in and stopped on the landing to listen to the whole thing before Ms. Feld noticed I was late.

That's the part I didn't understand until I stopped going. The scales weren't the boring ten minutes before the real thing. They were the real thing, or at least they were doing something the real thing couldn't — they were the ten minutes where nothing was being judged, where Ms. Feld would half-listen while making tea, where my hands got to just move without anyone, including me, deciding whether it was good.

I quit for reasons that had nothing to do with the music itself and everything to do with time — junior year, and something had to give, and piano was the thing that didn't show up on a transcript the way calculus did. I made the decision in about four days, which felt fast at the time and feels faster now. What I didn't account for was how much of my week had been organized around that Tuesday-Thursday walk up the stairs, how much of my sense of my own competence — the specific, private feeling of a passage finally sitting right under my fingers after weeks of it not sitting right — had nowhere to go once the bench was gone.

I still see Owen sometimes, at school, where he mostly plays baseball and I don't think anyone there knows he can play Chopin. We don't really talk about piano. We don't need to, exactly — there's a strange shorthand between people who shared a staircase for years without sharing much else, a nod that means something like I heard you get that right once and I don't think either of us has forgotten it.

I don't know if I'll go back to it in college. Ms. Feld told me, at my last lesson, that eleven years of scales doesn't disappear from your hands even if you stop practicing them, that they're in there somewhere, waiting. I didn't believe her completely. But I noticed, packing up my sheet music for the last time, that my hands found the closing chord of my last piece without me really looking at the page, the way you find a light switch in a room you've lived in long enough not to need to see it.
