The object is a small brass hand-stamp, the kind you press into an ink pad and then onto fabric, worn smooth on one corner from years of use. It reads, in reverse when you look at the stamping face, SECOND CHANCE — the name my mother gave her second-hand clothing business nine years ago, when she started reselling donated and thrifted clothes out of a rented stall at the Saturday market because my father had lost his job and we needed a second income that didn't require someone to hire her first.

She used that stamp on every single item that passed through her hands, pressing it onto a small cardstock tag she tied to each garment with cotton string. It was her signature, her guarantee, a way of saying she had personally checked this shirt or these jeans for stains and rips before deciding they were worth someone's money. I remember being eight years old, sitting under the folding table at her stall doing homework, listening to the small metallic click of the stamp meeting the ink pad, over and over, a sound so constant in my childhood that I can still hear it if I try.

I inherited the stamp two years ago, not because my mother stopped using it, but because her business had grown enough by then that she'd moved to a proper storefront and bought a proper label printer, and the old brass stamp got relegated to a drawer. I asked if I could have it. She looked at me a little strangely, since it wasn't worth anything, just an ink-stained piece of brass with a rubber grip cracking at the seam, but she said yes.

I keep it on my desk now, next to my laptop, and I use it sometimes on the little zines I make and hand out at school, stamped instead of my name because it feels more honest somehow than typing it. What I think about, holding it, is not really the clothes it once marked, though I think about those too — the winter coats she'd hunt down specifically for families she knew couldn't afford new ones, the wedding dress she once found for a bride who'd had almost nothing to spend. What I think about is the years before I understood what the business actually cost her. The stall in near-freezing weather. The nights she stayed up steaming out wrinkles and hand-mending small tears so a five-dollar shirt looked worth five dollars. The way she never once, in front of me, treated the work as beneath her, even when people occasionally did.

The stamp is a small thing, brass gone slightly green at the edges, worth maybe two dollars if you found it at a flea market and had no idea what it meant. But it is the object I would grab first in a fire, after the obvious things, because it is the clearest proof I have of a particular kind of stubbornness my mother has — the refusal to let a hard circumstance define the quality of the work she puts into surviving it. She never called herself resourceful or resilient, words that get used a lot for people in her position. She just kept stamping tags, one garment at a time, until the second chance she'd named the business after became, quietly, an actual second chance, for her and for us. I keep the stamp where I can see it because I want that particular kind of stubbornness to be visible to me every day, even on days when I don't feel like I have any of my own.
