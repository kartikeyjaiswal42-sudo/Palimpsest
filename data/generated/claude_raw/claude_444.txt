The place is our kitchen table between about nine and eleven at night. Not the kitchen. The table.

My mother went back to university when she was forty and I was twelve. She'd been a dental receptionist for fourteen years and one day she came home with a printout of an application for a nursing program at the community college, folded in half, which she left on the counter for about a week before she said anything about it. My father said do it. I said nothing, because I was twelve and it had not occurred to me that my mother was a person with a future.

Here's what actually happened, though. Nobody in our house had a desk. There was one in the corner of my parents' room that had my father's tools in it. So she worked at the kitchen table, and because she worked there, I worked there, and that was it for the next four years.

That's the whole habit. We sat across from each other with the light on and did our homework.

I want to be clear that it was not warm most of the time. It was mostly boring. She had a highlighter she clicked when she was frustrated, click click click, and it made me want to scream. Sometimes she'd read a definition out loud to nobody. Sometimes I'd get stuck on something and she'd look up and say nothing helpful, because she didn't know algebra either, and we'd both go back to our own pages. Once in tenth grade I told her the clicking was driving me insane and she said sorry and did it again nineteen seconds later.

But something happened over four years that neither of us set out to do.

I got to see what an adult looks like when she's bad at something. That's not a thing kids usually get to see. Adults do their struggling somewhere else, in an office or a car, and they come home with the answer already found. My mother did hers eighteen inches from my face. She failed a pharmacology exam in her second year, the whole thing, and she sat at that table and cried for about ten minutes and then she pulled the book back over and started on chapter nine. She didn't say anything to me about resilience. She just did the next chapter. I was fifteen and I remember thinking, oh — that's what it is. It's not a feeling you have. It's a chapter you open.

I think about that more than almost anything else that's happened to me.

So the reason the table is where I feel most myself isn't that it's peaceful, because it isn't, there's a fridge that hums and my little brother comes through wanting cereal at half past ten. It's that it's the only place I've ever been where being in the middle of something hard is completely normal. Nobody performs there. You don't have to be finished. You don't have to be good yet. You just have to be sitting down with the thing open.

She graduated when I was sixteen. She works nights at a hospital now, three twelves a week, and the table is mostly mine, which I hate a little. I've started leaving my books out overnight so it still looks occupied.

Last month she was home on a Tuesday and she sat down across from me with her recertification packet, and she clicked her pen the entire time, and I didn't say anything about it. I got more done that night than I had in two weeks. I don't fully understand why that works. I've stopped trying to explain it and just started arranging my life so it can keep happening.

That's the place. A pine table with a burn mark near the left corner from a pan in 2019, one overhead light that's slightly too yellow, and a chair that faces the person doing the same thing you're doing.
