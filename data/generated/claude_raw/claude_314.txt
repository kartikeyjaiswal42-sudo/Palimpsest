We were all sitting in the living room when the power went out. It was probably six in the evening on a Saturday. My dad flipped the light switch a couple of times, like maybe the house was just deciding not to participate in electricity for a moment and might change its mind. My mum went to check the main breaker. Nothing. We called the power company and got a recorded message that said there was an outage in the area and they were working on it.

The recording said it would be restored within two to four hours.

It wasn't.

By midnight, we'd accepted that power restoration was probably not happening that night. We found flashlights and candles. We charged our phones on the car battery. We went to bed and woke up still without power. The power company's line said they were still working on it, that it was affecting a larger area than initially reported, that they didn't have an estimate for restoration.

By the second day without power, things started getting uncomfortable. Our fridge was warming up. We had no water because our water pump is electric. My family, which usually exists in pretty separate spheres—my parents in their work, my brother in school, me in school and activities—suddenly had to exist entirely together in a single space that was getting progressively more primitive.

No screens because the devices were dying. No water for showers. No way to entertain ourselves except conversation and reading what we had on paper. We played card games. We borrowed a neighbor's generator and charged our phones just enough to read the news about how bad the outage was getting.

By the fourth day, we settled into a rhythm. We went to a friend's house for showers and to charge devices. We cooked on our camping stove. We slept under layers because without air conditioning, the house was getting warm and humid. We stopped checking our phones constantly because there was nothing to check and nowhere to go that had power.

There's a weird shift that happened around day five or six. We'd moved completely past the frustration phase and into something else—an acceptance, almost, of a different way of living. We got up when it was light and went to bed when it was dark. We had meals at actual mealtimes rather than eating in front of screens. We talked to each other because we literally had nothing else to do.

My brother and I spent an entire afternoon playing chess while my parents read. I can't remember the last time we'd done that. I can't remember a time when my family just existed together without the constant hum of devices in the background.

The power came back on late on day seven. The air conditioner kicked in with a sound like it was waking up. The lights turned on and everything was suddenly too bright. Our phones started buzzing with accumulated notifications and messages and emails from the days we'd been offline.

And I remember feeling genuinely sad about it.

That sounds weird, and I haven't really told anyone because it does sound weird. But losing power felt, in some strange way, like a gift. It forced us to slow down in a way that nothing else could have. It made us into a family that actually spent time together instead of a group of people who happened to live in the same house. I don't want to lose power again, but I don't want to lose the thing that the power outage gave us, which was that experience of being together without any interference.

I think about it in the morning when I wake up and immediately grab my phone, when I default back to the rhythms of constant connectivity. I think about those seven days and I think about the fact that I had to lose power to remember that something different was possible.
