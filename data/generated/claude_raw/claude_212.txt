I spent a month on Ward 12 when I was fifteen, and the belief I challenged was mine, and I got it wrong in a way I am still working out.

The belief was that being ill was a period of suspension. Real life was outside, at school, in the group chat, and the ward was a waiting room I would eventually be released from back into the actual world. I held this so completely that I did not think of it as a belief. It was just the shape of things. So I did the logical thing: I brought my textbooks in, I asked my mother for the GCSE past papers, and I set out to make the month not count. I worked through chemistry on a bed-tray with a cannula in my left hand. I was, I thought, being admirable.

There was a woman opposite me called Pat who had been in for eleven weeks. She did the crossword slowly. She talked to the healthcare assistants about their children by name. I thought she had given up. That is the honest word for what I thought. I had a private, entirely unearned sense that I was doing it correctly and she was doing it wrong, and I never said it, and I am fairly sure she knew.

What I got wrong was the arithmetic. I assumed that time spent in the ward was subtracted from my life and time spent on chemistry was added back. But I was not learning any chemistry. I was reading the same page while a machine beeped. Nothing went in. Meanwhile Pat, who I had written off, knew that the physiotherapist came Tuesdays and that if you asked on Monday you could get an earlier slot, and she told me, and it was the single most useful piece of information anyone gave me that month.

She was not passing time. She was living somewhere, competently, that I had refused to admit was a place.

I went home in April. The belief did not collapse dramatically; it eroded over the following year, mostly at odd moments — realising I could not remember one thing from those textbooks but could remember the exact sound of the drug trolley.

What I think now is that "real life" is not a location you get returned to. It is wherever you actually are, and treating a month of it as a waiting room does not preserve that month, it just wastes it while making you feel virtuous.

I still catch myself doing it. Every time a queue is long, or a lesson is dull, I feel the old subtraction start. I have not fully stopped. But I notice, which I did not before, and I think of Pat and her crossword and the fact that she was better at being alive on that ward than I was.
