I keep the ribbon from that tournament in my desk drawer. A small tricolor thing that says County Champions 2022. When you debate competitively, you get used to winning. You prepare for months, practice every weekend, memorize arguments until they're part of your operating system. You stand at the podium and your brain produces evidence at the right time and the judges vote for you and it feels inevitable. Like you were always going to win.

That tournament was at a school about two hours from mine, in a gym that smelled like old basketball and new carpet. I was defending the resolution that private prisons should be illegal. I'd spent three months researching incarceration statistics, building affirmative cases, practicing responses to every argument I'd ever heard. I was good. I was genuinely good. My teammates knew it. My coach knew it. And I knew that I knew it, which is different from actually being good.

The debate round itself went smoothly until it didn't. My opponent made this argument about government inefficiency that I'd prepared for, but she made it in a way I hadn't. She connected it to something I said in my opening statement—something I'd basically forgotten about by the time rebuttals came around. She had three minutes to talk and she spent all three of them building this one idea where my position actually contradicted itself. It wasn't even particularly clever. It was just correct.

I had ten minutes to respond and I spent nine of them trying to find evidence that disproved her instead of just acknowledging the actual problem with my argument. I ran it three different ways. I added new statistics. I found a study about a completely different scenario that I tried to force into relevance. Every second I spent pretending I was right instead of admitting I was wrong, the judges were checking boxes on their ballots. I lost by two points. Not close enough to feel like a fluke. Close enough to know exactly where it broke.

Afterward, I read the judge's comments. She wrote that I had excellent research and strong advocacy skills but that I seemed unable to abandon a losing position. She said the moment I realized my initial framework was compromised, I should have pivoted to arguing something different—not better evidence for the same broken case. She said good debaters know when to change their minds.

The worst part was that she was right. I'd been so focused on winning that particular round that I'd forgotten the actual point of debate, which is figuring out what's true. Or at least what's more true than the alternative. I spent the ride home not even thinking about the other kids whose teams had won. I just kept replaying the moment when I could have said something like yeah, that's actually a legitimate problem with my case, let me address it differently. But I didn't. I just pushed harder.

I don't keep that trophy anywhere. But I keep the ribbon. Every time I'm in a conversation with someone I disagree with, every time I'm building an argument in a class or a meeting or just my own head, I think about that moment. I think about how much worse the loss felt than any loss ever has, not because of the points but because I'd earned it by refusing to listen to what the other person was actually saying.

I still debate. I still win most of the time. But I'm better at noticing now when I'm just trying to win instead of trying to understand. That moment taught me something about myself that no trophy ever could have.
