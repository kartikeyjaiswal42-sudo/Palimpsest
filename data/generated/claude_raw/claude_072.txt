My grandfather waters his tomatoes twice a week, and when he does it he does it for a long time. The hose runs at the base of each plant until the soil goes dark and holds the shape of your thumb when you press it. He explained the reasoning to me once, standing in his boots at seven in the morning with the light still low: shallow water makes shallow roots. If you sprinkle a little every day, the roots stay near the surface where the sun can reach them, and then one bad week kills everything at once. Deep and rare. He said it the way you say arithmetic.

I did not believe him. I was fifteen, I had read a great deal about drought gardening on my phone, and I had a theory of my own.

That January he went into hospital to have his hip replaced, and the garden became mine for six weeks. Our district was in the second summer of restrictions — buckets only, no hoses, two mornings a week — and the beans were already curling at the edges by the time I took over. I decided the restrictions were an opportunity rather than a problem. Little and often, I thought. A fine mist before the heat came up, a cup of grey water at the base of each plant in the evening, so that nothing was ever completely dry. It seemed kinder. It seemed like a version of care that had more of me in it, which I think now was the actual appeal.

For three weeks I looked right. The leaves stayed green while the neighbours' went grey. I sent my grandfather photographs from the ward, and he sent back short messages that did not say well done.

Then February came, and with it five days above forty-two degrees. I watered in the mornings and again at dusk and it changed nothing. The tomatoes went from firm to translucent in about thirty hours. The beans dropped everything they had. When I finally pulled up a plant to see what had been happening underneath, the root ball came out of the ground in one shallow disc, wide as a dinner plate and barely as deep as my palm. It had never gone looking for anything. I had made certain it never had to.

What I got wrong was not the schedule. I could have learned the schedule from a leaflet at the hardware shop. What I got wrong was the belief sitting underneath it, which was that care is measured by how often you show up. I had assumed that attention given daily must be better than attention given rarely, because it costs more and because it feels more like love. It took a dead garden for me to notice that I had never once tested that assumption. My grandfather's method looks like neglect for six days out of seven. It works because it asks the plant to do something difficult and then gets out of the way.

He came home in March with a walking frame and said nothing at all about the beds. We replanted in April, his way. I still catch myself, though — hovering, checking, offering help before anyone has asked for it, mistaking frequency for depth. I understand the argument now. I am not sure I have won it.
