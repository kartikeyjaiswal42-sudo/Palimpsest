I'm not going to pretend I don't know exactly which essay competition I mean, or exactly what name I used instead of my own, though I'd rather talk around the specifics for a while before I get to them, because even now, writing this, some part of me wants to protect the version of myself who thought that was a reasonable thing to do.

There's a national writing competition our school pushes every fall, the kind with a modest cash prize and a much larger reputation, the kind where past winners get their names read out at an assembly and everyone claps a little longer than the moment probably deserves. I'd entered twice before, under my own name, and placed nowhere both times, not even an honorable mention, and I told myself I'd stopped caring about it, which was a lie I believed hard enough to almost make true.

The third year, I entered again, but I put a different name on the submission — not a wild invented persona, just a slight variation, close enough to pass, distant enough that if it lost, it wouldn't be a loss anyone could trace back to me. I told myself this was about the writing being judged purely on its own terms, free of whatever quiet reputation I'd built up over two prior losses. That explanation held together for about as long as I needed it to.

It won. Third place, actually, not first, a detail that matters to the story in a way I didn't expect at the time. And I remember sitting in that assembly, hearing a name that was almost mine read out loud, watching a teacher scan the room for someone to stand up and finding no one, because the person attached to that name didn't fully exist in any way that could stand up in a school gymnasium and be seen.

I want to tell you the moment I regretted it was immediate. It wasn't. For a few days I just felt a strange, private satisfaction — proof, finally, that the writing itself had been good enough, that the two prior losses had been about something other than my sentences. It took me almost two weeks to notice what that satisfaction was actually costing me, which was the ability to ever claim it. I couldn't tell anyone. I couldn't put it anywhere real. I had built a version of success specifically engineered to be unusable by the actual person who'd earned it.

What changed my mind wasn't guilt exactly, or not only guilt. It was smaller and stranger than that — a girl in my English class mentioned the mystery name at lunch, wondering aloud who it belonged to, half-joking that maybe it was a hoax, and I sat there eating a sandwich and said nothing, and felt, for the first time clearly, how strange it is to sit inside your own accomplishment as a stranger to it. That was the moment, if I'm being precise about it. Not the ceremony. That lunch table.

I emailed the competition organizers eventually, quietly, explaining what I'd done, offering to have the placement withdrawn if that was the right call. They were more understanding than I expected — apparently this happens more than you'd think, kids testing whether their name is the thing holding their work back — and they let the placement stand under a note that it had been submitted under a pseudonym, no further explanation given publicly.

I still don't fully know what I proved to myself that fall. That my writing was good, maybe, which I'd wanted to know for two straight years of losing under my own name. But also that wanting proof badly enough can make you build it in a shape you can't actually use, can't stand up for at an assembly, can't tell your own parents about without a long and complicated explanation. I've entered under my real name every year since. I haven't placed as well. I think, most days, that this is fine.
