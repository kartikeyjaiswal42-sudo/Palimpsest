The scorer's table at Milton Road Recreation Ground is not a table. It is a plastic garden chair and a second plastic garden chair turned sideways to hold the book, positioned at square leg because that is where the shade is after two o'clock. I have sat there most Sundays from April to September since I was eleven. That is where I feel most like myself, and I want to explain why, but I have to explain a mistake first, because the two things are the same thing.

My father plays for a Saturday-and-Sunday side made up of men who work in warehouses and dentistry and one man who drives a coach. He is a middle-order batsman who has been declining slowly and gracefully for a decade. When I was eleven he taught me to keep score, which sounds like a small kindness and was actually a transfer of something. There is a right way to fill a scorebook. Dots in the boxes. A wicket recorded with the bowler's figures aligned. The bowling analysis and the batting total have to agree at the end of every over, and if they don't you have to go back and find where you stopped paying attention. My father is not a precise man in any other part of his life. He loses keys. But he taught me that the book is a promise you make to people who weren't there.

In August two years ago, in a game against Ashbourne, I made an error I have not stopped thinking about. It was the sixteenth over. A man called Dev was on 48 and there was a scampered single that I recorded as a leg bye. I was fairly sure at the time. I was not certain. He was out three balls later for what my book said was 49. He asked me, afterwards, in the way men ask when they are pretending not to care, whether he'd got his fifty. I said no. He said, ah, and went and sat down with his pads still on.

I checked the book that night at the kitchen table. The bowling figures balanced either way, because a leg bye and a run both count as one to the total. There was no arithmetic that could tell me. There was only what I remembered, and what I remembered had gone soft. He might have got his fifty. He probably did. I had decided in half a second, and the half-second had been about wanting to look decisive in front of men twice my age, not about the ball.

I have thought about that far more than the size of it warrants. Dev doesn't remember. He got a hundred the next season. But I understood something at that kitchen table that I would not have understood from being told: that a record is not a neutral thing, that somebody is always making it, and that the person making it can be tired or vain or seventeen.

So now I keep score differently. I ask. I lean over and say to the umpire, was that off the pad, and I don't mind that it makes me look uncertain, because I am uncertain, and the book should say what happened rather than what I would prefer to have seen. I write in pencil for the first pass. I keep a little column on the right for things I'm not sure about, and at tea I go and check them.

That chair at square leg is where I feel most myself because it is the only place where I have completely clear duties and no ability to hide from them. Nobody watches the scorer. Everybody depends on the scorer. You are alone with your own honesty for four hours with a flask of tea, and at the close of play you hand somebody a piece of paper and say, this is what happened, and they believe you.

I would like to be someone worth believing. I am practising in a plastic chair.
