On the shelf above my desk, next to a chipped trophy from a seventh grade robotics competition, sits a rectangle of mild steel about the size of a paperback book, gouged with three ropy lines of weld that get steadily less ugly from left to right. It is not framed. It does not need to be. It is the ugliest object I own and also the one I would grab first in a fire, after the obvious things.

I signed up for Intro to Welding because the elective I actually wanted, AP Studio Art, was full, and I spent the first two weeks making that very clear to anyone who would listen, including, unfortunately, my instructor Mr. Kessler, who did not seem bothered by my complaining so much as unimpressed by it. He handed out ear protection and auto-darkening helmets and safety glasses that fogged if you breathed wrong, and he explained the arc like it was something patient, a controlled lightning you had to earn the right to hold, and I remember thinking that this was an enormous amount of ceremony for what was, as far as I could tell, gluing metal together with more metal.

For three weeks I was bad at it. My beads looked like something a nervous spider had done. I would strike the arc too far from the joint, or too close, and either way I'd get spatter across my gloves and a bead that was more hole than weld, the kind Mr. Kessler would tap with a chipping hammer and watch crumble like burnt toast.

Then, on a Thursday I can place exactly because it was the day before a chemistry test I hadn't studied for, something changed. I struck the arc, and instead of chasing it the way I usually did, I let my hand go slow and stupid and steady, the way you're supposed to hold a hose when you're watering something that bruises easily. The puddle formed. It stayed. I dragged it in one continuous motion down four inches of steel, and when I flipped my hood up, Mr. Kessler was already standing there. He picked up the coupon, turned it in the light, and said, "There it is." Not a big compliment. Three words. I have thought about those three words more than I've thought about most conversations I've had with people I actually like.

What changed wasn't that I got good, because I didn't, not that day. What changed was that for four inches of steel I stopped being a person waiting for a better elective to open up, and started paying attention to the thing actually in front of me. I hadn't expected the object I'd end up caring about most that year to be an ugly rectangle of scrap steel.

I still don't know if I'm going to study something with welding in it. But I kept the coupon, ropy beads and all, because it's the only object I own that marks the exact hour I stopped waiting to be somewhere else.
