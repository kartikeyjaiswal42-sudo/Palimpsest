I failed at something I thought I was good at—understanding Priya. We have been best friends since we were six years old, and I know her better than I know anyone. So when she started expressing political views that I completely disagreed with, I thought I could convince her. I thought I could explain to her why she was wrong, and she would see it, and everything would be fine.

It didn't work that way. What started as a conversation became an argument. Then it became a recurring argument. We would be eating lunch or walking home from school and one of us would bring something up and the conversation would ignite. I would present facts, articles, statistics. I was very sure of myself. She seemed to be choosing not to listen, or worse, not to care about the facts. What frustrated me most was that she is a logical person. We both care about the world and justice. How could we see things so differently?

I kept trying. That's what I did next, which was probably the wrong choice. I sent her links to articles. I brought up the topic strategically during conversations, thinking if I just presented the information right, she would change her mind. I was still operating on the assumption that this was a knowledge problem, that she just needed better information. I couldn't see that I was doing the opposite of listening—I was trying to make her understand me instead of trying to actually understand her.

The argument escalated. Not in the sense that we started fighting or saying cruel things, but in the sense that we were both digging in. She started sending me articles too, from her perspective. I would read them and think they were missing the point. She would do the same with my articles. We were both convinced the other person was willfully ignorant. We were both equally confident. And I was failing to reach her, which meant failing at something I had always believed was my strength—connection with people I loved.

We didn't have a dramatic ending to it. We just stopped talking about politics. It sounds like a small compromise, but it represented a significant shift for me. I had to accept that I couldn't convince her. That's when I actually started listening. Without the pressure to convert her or the goal of winning the argument, I could actually ask her why she believed what she believed. Not why she was wrong, but why this particular political worldview made sense to her.

What I learned is that her beliefs come from different values than mine, not from different facts. We both care about people suffering. We just disagree about the root causes and the solutions. She comes from a place of trusting institutions and gradual change. I come from a place of skepticism about institutions and belief in structural transformation. Neither of us is ignorant. We just built different frameworks.

This doesn't mean we agree on politics now. We still see things differently. But I'm no longer trying to fix her beliefs. When something political comes up, I ask questions. I listen to her reasoning. Sometimes I challenge her gently, not to convince her but to understand her better. And she does the same with me.

I think I was not successful because I defined success wrong. I thought success meant convincing my best friend to agree with me. I measured my failure in her continued disagreement. But that wasn't actually the point of our friendship, even though I had been operating like it was. Real success was figuring out how to love someone even when you see the world completely differently. Real success was learning that deep connection doesn't require agreement on everything.

The argument is still unresolved in the sense that we never reached consensus. But I resolved something else—I resolved my need to win. And that might have been the more important thing to learn.
