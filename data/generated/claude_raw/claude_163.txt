The textbook gives Partition two paragraphs. I know because I counted them in Mr. Ashwell's class in October, sitting three rows back while he read aloud the sentence about "widespread but largely uncoordinated violence accompanying the transfer of populations," and I raised my hand and said that my grandfather walked from Lahore to Amritsar in August of 1947 and that the word "uncoordinated" was doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. Mr. Ashwell is someone I respect enormously — he's the reason I want to study history at all — and he did not back down easily, which is part of why the argument mattered.

He said the textbook has to cover four centuries in one semester and can't give every event the weight a family might want. I said that wasn't really the objection, that the objection was a two-paragraph summary letting students walk away thinking Partition was a logistics problem instead of the reason my grandfather doesn't have a sister anymore. We went back and forth for most of a class period, both of us getting a little sharper than either of us usually allows in that room. He ended up agreeing to let me build a supplementary presentation using oral history, which I did, and which went fine, and which is not actually the disagreement I think about most.

The one I can't resolve is with my grandfather himself. When I asked him, that same week, if he'd let me record him talking about the walk for the project, he said no, immediately, in a tone I'd never heard him use with me before. I pushed. I told him the textbook was wrong, that people needed to hear what actually happened instead of a sentence about population transfer, that his story mattered more than the version Mr. Ashwell's students were being handed. My grandfather said that not everything that happened to him exists to correct someone else's textbook. He said some of it is just his, and that he gets to decide it stays that way, and that I was treating his grief like a source document.

I still think he's wrong about this, mostly. I think silence is how history gets flattened into two paragraphs in the first place, that every family that decides a memory is too private to share is quietly voting for the version that makes it into print instead. I told him that. He didn't get angry, exactly, but he didn't budge either, and we've had some version of this same conversation four or five times since, always ending in the same place: me saying that erasure happens by accretion, one silence at a time, and him saying that a man is allowed to keep something for himself even if I've decided it belongs to history.

I did the presentation anyway, built mostly from an interview my great-aunt gave years ago to a local oral history project before she died, which felt like a compromise and also felt like going around him, which I haven't fully forgiven myself for. Mr. Ashwell's textbook still has its two paragraphs. My grandfather still has his silence, intact, mostly, except for the parts he's let slip out sideways over dinner when he forgets I'm listening for them. I don't know how to want both things at once — his right to keep what's his, and a written record that doesn't reduce him to a footnote about population transfer — but I haven't stopped wanting both, and neither has he stopped refusing me, and I don't think either of us is going to be the one who gives in first.
