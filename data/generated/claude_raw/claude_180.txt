My mother failed the civics portion of her naturalization interview in March, and it was my fault, or at least I had built the thing that failed.

I had made her a system. One hundred flashcards, color-coded by category, plus a spreadsheet that tracked which ones she missed so we could drill those twice as often. I quizzed her every night after dinner for four months. By February she was getting ninety-four out of a hundred in my kitchen, and I was proud of that number in a way I am now embarrassed by.

The officer asked her who the governor of our state is. She knew. She said "Governor" and then stopped, and could not get the name out in English, and said it in Tagalog instead, and then apologized, which she does when she is frightened, and then the next question came and she was already gone. She got four right out of six required. Six, not ten, because she is over sixty-five, which I had known and had somehow never adjusted the studying for.

Driving home she was quiet, and then she said, not angrily, "You never asked me the questions when I was tired."

That was the moment. I had built a system for the kitchen table at seven-thirty, with the rice cooker running and me across from her prompting her with my eyebrows when she paused. I had optimized for a room that would never exist again. Her recall was not the problem. Her recall under a fluorescent light in front of a stranger with a folder was the problem, and I had never tested it once, because testing it would have meant making my own mother uncomfortable, and I had confused kindness with usefulness.

So I changed what we practiced. I stopped drilling content. I had her answer in the car with the radio on. I had my father, who she is not soft around, ask her questions in the living room while standing up. I made her practice saying "Could you repeat that, please" until it came out flat and unembarrassed, because the thing that ended the interview was not a missing fact, it was the apology.

She passed in June. Eight out of eight, though she only needed six. She told the officer she had practiced with her son and the officer said that was nice.

What I actually learned is that I had been measuring the wrong room. Ninety-four out of a hundred at my kitchen table meant almost nothing, and I had been counting it for four months.
