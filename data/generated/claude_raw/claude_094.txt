The lighting booth is in the back left corner of the auditorium, up three metal stairs and through a door that's usually locked. Before sophomore year, I didn't know this place existed. Now I can't imagine school without it.

I started running lights for the drama club kind of by accident. The technical director needed someone and asked around, and I volunteered mostly because my friend Sarah was doing the sounds and I thought it would be fun to sit with her during rehearsals. It was fun at first—just pressing buttons at the right time, watching the stage come alive when you flipped the switches. But then I actually started paying attention to what I was doing.

Every Thursday night during spring semester, I climbed those stairs and sat in a tiny booth with a lighting console that had about thirty cues marked on a printout. The director would call out the scene changes and I'd have maybe three seconds to dim the house lights while bringing up the stage lights to exactly the right level. Too bright and it washed out the actors' faces. Too dim and nobody could see anything. There's this exact point that feels right, and I got obsessed with finding it every single time.

I started coming in early to test different levels. I'd sit in different parts of the audience to see how the light looked from various angles. I made notes on my script about what each cue was supposed to convey—the romantic scene needed to be warm and intimate, the scary moment needed harsh shadows, the climactic monologue needed everything to be just slightly dimmer so the actor's face was the only thing that mattered. It sounds silly now but at the time it felt incredibly important. Like I was actually responsible for whether this performance worked or failed.

The thing is, I was right. Not because I'm particularly talented at lighting, but because once I started really paying attention, I realized that lighting isn't just turning things brighter or darker. It's telling the story. It's saying where the audience should look, how they should feel, what moment matters most. When I got a cue wrong in dress rehearsal one night and accidentally left the stage way too bright during this vulnerable scene between the two main characters, I could see it destroy the moment. The audience laughed nervously instead of sitting with the discomfort that the scene was supposed to create.

I apologized to the director afterward. She said it was fine, it was just tech rehearsal. But I spent two hours that night redoing every single cue, checking them again and again until I was sure I had it right. And when we opened the show the next week, I was positioned in that booth with my hand hovering over the controls, completely focused, and for the first time I understood what it felt like to be truly needed in something.

I've run lights for three plays now. I'm still not perfect. Sometimes I miss cues or hit them slightly off. But something changed in me that first year. I discovered this place where I can sit in the shadows and literally shine light on what matters. Where I can be invisible and essential at the same time. The lighting booth is where I feel most myself—focused, purposeful, part of something bigger than just me sitting alone.
