The first snow I ever saw was on my eighth birthday, and I've been marking every first snow since then, not as a celebration but as a compulsion.

We'd moved to Vermont six months earlier. I'd never lived anywhere with real seasons. Everything before Vermont had been smooth, consistent, predictable weather. Then we arrived in autumn and learned about the change. But nothing prepared me for snow.

It came in November, heavy and immediate, transforming everything overnight. I remember waking up to the sound of my mother calling from downstairs, her voice containing a kind of wonder that I'd never heard before. I came down in my pajamas and stood at the window, watching the world covered in white.

We went outside. I'd never felt anything like the cold, the way it burned my lungs when I breathed in. My mother was laughing. My father was taking pictures. And I was completely disoriented by the beauty of it, by the fact that the world could change so completely overnight.

After that, I couldn't stop watching for it. Every November and December, I'd wake up early and check the weather, checking if snow might be coming. It became this obsessive thing. I'd stand at the window, waiting. And every year, usually sometime in November, it would come. That first snow. That reset of the world.

What's strange is how much this matters to me. I've never been able to fully articulate why, but watching the first snow feels like marking time, like acknowledging that nothing stays the same and that change is beautiful even when it's total.

My mother used to come watch it with me. We'd stand together at the kitchen window and not say anything, just watch the snow accumulate. It was one of the few times she seemed peaceful, like she wasn't worried about anything or thinking about work. She was just present in the moment of the snow.

By the time I was twelve, I'd developed a whole ritual around it. I'd check the weather obsessively starting in October. When the forecast showed snow, I'd be almost unable to sleep the night before. I'd wake up extra early. I'd make hot chocolate. I'd get my phone ready to film it, though I was never sure what I was filming or why it mattered.

My therapist, when I started seeing her for general anxiety stuff, asked me about the snow ritual. She wanted to understand why it mattered so much to me, whether it was calming or whether it was compulsive in an unhealthy way. I tried to explain, but it's hard to articulate. It's not that I'm afraid if I don't watch it. It's not like I think something will be wrong if I miss it.

It's more that watching the first snow is the one thing in my life that hasn't changed. Everything else has—we moved to a different state, my parents' relationship shifted, my friends changed, I've grown up and my body and my mind have changed. But the snow comes, every year, in basically the same way. It's consistent in a world that isn't. It's beautiful in a way that doesn't require anything from me except to be present to witness it.

I watch the first snow and I remember being eight years old, completely amazed by something new. I feel, for a moment, like that wonder is still possible, that I can still be surprised by the world. And then the snow accumulates and melts and it's spring again, and I can wait for next year.

This year, I'm seventeen. The snow might come in November. I'll watch for it, and I'll wake up early, and I'll make hot chocolate. It won't be as pure as it was when I was eight. I'll be aware that I'm participating in a ritual, aware that I'm trying to hold onto something that's already passed. But I'll do it anyway, because some habits are worth keeping, and some small rituals are the things that hold us together when everything else is changing.
