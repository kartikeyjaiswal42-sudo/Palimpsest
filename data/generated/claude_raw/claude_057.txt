I promised the food bank two thousand pounds of canned goods. I told them that number in an email in September, three weeks before our drive even started, because I wanted a number that would sound ambitious on the posters, and two thousand felt round and achievable and impressive all at once. We collected six hundred and forty.

I was the one who chose the number, and I was the one who chose almost everything else about that drive, because I'd run the club for two years by then and had a specific way I liked things done, and I think if I'm honest that specific way of liking things done is a large part of why we came up six hundred pounds short of what I'd promised a food bank that was counting on us for their November distribution.

I turned down help. That's the plain version of it. Two members of the club, a sophomore named Deja and a junior named Miles, suggested early on that we split the outreach across all four grade levels instead of running it centrally through the club's usual channels, get homeroom teachers involved, put bins in the gym instead of just outside the cafeteria. I said no, or something politer than no that still meant no, because I had a system that had worked at a smaller scale the year before and I didn't want to manage the complexity of a bigger one. I wanted to control the thing I'd be accountable for. I told myself this was responsible. It was actually just fear wearing the costume of competence.

The bins outside the cafeteria filled slowly. I checked them most mornings and watched the fill line creep instead of climb, and I kept telling myself there was still time, we'd get a surge in the final week, that's how drives work. There wasn't a surge. There was a slow, honest trickle that added up to six hundred and forty pounds by the deadline, and a food bank coordinator named Mr. Osei who had planned part of a distribution around a number I'd given him with total, unearned confidence.

I had to call him. That phone call is the part I actually think about, more than the shortfall itself. I explained the number, apologized, offered to extend the drive another two weeks, and he was kind about it in a way that somehow made it worse — he said this happens, organizations overpromise all the time, they'd adjust the distribution, don't worry. I hung up feeling like I'd been let off a hook I thought I deserved to stay on longer.

What I got wrong wasn't the estimate. Estimates are allowed to be wrong; that's what estimates are. What I got wrong was mistaking my own comfort for the right process, deciding a smaller, more controllable drive was better than a bigger, messier one just because the messier one scared me. Deja and Miles's idea would have worked. I know that now with a kind of certainty that doesn't do anyone any good in hindsight. Homeroom bins, more hands, more visibility, exactly the kind of reach a drive that size actually needed, and I said no to it because I liked being the person who ran things a certain way more than I liked the idea of the drive actually succeeding on its own terms.

I still run the club. I did things differently the next year — bins in every homeroom, a planning committee instead of just me, a number I built from the ground up instead of picked because it sounded good on a poster. We hit eighteen hundred pounds, still short of what I'd once promised but honest, this time, about how we got there.

I don't think about the eighteen hundred much. I think about the six hundred and forty, and Mr. Osei's voice being gentler than I deserved, and the version of that fall where I said yes to Deja in September instead of learning the same lesson the slow, expensive way. I don't know that I've fully forgiven myself for choosing control over the actual mission. I'm not sure that's the kind of thing you're supposed to fully forgive yourself for. I think you're just supposed to notice it, every time you feel that same instinct rising again, and choose differently.
