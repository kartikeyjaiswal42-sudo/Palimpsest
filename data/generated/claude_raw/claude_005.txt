The bell above the door does this thing where it doesn't ring so much as clatter, like it's annoyed at being disturbed, and I have heard that clatter probably four thousand times and I could still draw you the exact pitch of it from memory. I used to hate that sound. It meant someone needed something, and needing something at our store almost always meant me putting down whatever I was doing and going to find it, because my dad believed customers should never have to ask twice.

I started working the register the summer I turned thirteen, which is younger than you're supposed to legally be doing much of anything, but our store is small enough that nobody was checking. My parents run it out of the bottom floor of a building on Fentress, lottery tickets and cigarettes behind the counter, a cooler of energy drinks that hums louder than it should, a whole aisle just for chips because apparently that's what sells. For years I thought of the register as the place I got stuck. My friends were at the lake or wherever, and I was scanning a can of Arizona iced tea for the ninth time that hour for a man who came in every single day at exactly 4:15.

Here's the thing I didn't expect, though it took me long enough to notice it that I'm almost embarrassed to admit when it happened. It was maybe two years ago, a Tuesday, nothing special about it, and Mr. Okafor came in — the 4:15 man, I finally learned his name after something like three years of ringing him up — and he was in a mood, talking fast about his daughter getting into some program, and I just stood there and listened because what else was I going to do, there was no line. And I realized I knew things about him. Not big things. That his knees hurt in the cold. That he'd started buying the diet version of his usual soda in March, which meant something, I didn't ask what. That he said "take care now" instead of goodbye, always, like a period at the end of a sentence.

I started paying attention after that, and once I started I couldn't really stop. The woman who buys a single scratch-off every Friday and never more, like she's rationing herself against something. The two brothers, maybe eight and ten, who come in after school and count out exact change for one bag of chips to split, and who I now quietly make sure gets rung up as the cheaper size if there's ever a discrepancy, don't tell my dad. The guy who used to buy a pack of Marlboros every day and then one day switched to gum and has bought gum every day since, and I have never once asked him about it because it isn't my business, but I notice, and I think noticing is its own kind of caring even when you don't say anything.

I used to think the register was where time got taken from me. Now I think it's the opposite. I can be back there for a four-hour shift and lose the whole afternoon without feeling like anything was stolen, because I'm not just standing behind a counter scanning barcodes, I'm reading a rotating cast of a few hundred people who trust me, in this small unglamorous way, to know their order and not make them repeat themselves. That's not nothing. My dad never explained this to me in words. He just did it himself, every single day, remembering who takes their coffee black and who's trying to quit smoking and who just lost someone, and I absorbed it the way you absorb anything you watch closely enough for long enough, which is slowly, and then all at once.

I don't know exactly when the shift happened in my head, whether it was Mr. Okafor's daughter or something quieter I didn't clock at the time. But I know I stopped counting down my shifts and started, without meaning to, keeping a kind of ledger of people instead of just money. And that clatter of the bell still isn't a pleasant sound. It's just become one I listen for.
