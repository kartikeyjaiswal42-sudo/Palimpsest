I spent an entire year preparing for the mathematics olympiad.

Not just preparing—consumed by it. I'd wake up at five in the morning to work through problem sets. I'd stay after school to meet with the olympiad coach. I'd do problems until my eyes hurt and my hands cramped. I convinced myself that winning the olympiad was going to define my entire future, that it was the most important thing I could do in high school.

My parents were supportive, which meant they asked me every evening if I'd practiced, if I was making progress, if I thought I was going to win. My friends got used to me canceling plans because I had to study. I turned down social activities, skipped parties, missed events I actually wanted to go to because I was supposed to be training for this competition.

The week before the olympiad, I was confident. Not cocky, but genuinely confident. I'd solved so many problems that hard problems didn't feel as hard anymore. I understood the concepts deeply. I was ready.

I wasn't ready.

The actual olympiad was harder than anything I'd practiced. Not just slightly harder—completely different in a way I hadn't anticipated. The problems required approaches I hadn't developed. They tested edges of mathematics that I had gaps in. I left the competition feeling devastated, knowing I'd failed before the results even came out.

When the results were published, I was even further down the rankings than my worst fears had predicted. I was in the bottom half. My score was below average. All of that year of work, all of those early mornings and canceled plans and single-minded focus, and I'd still failed at the actual thing.

I think I spent about a week being genuinely depressed about it. I remember my mum trying to tell me that it didn't matter, that I'd learned so much through the process, that failing at the olympiad didn't mean I was bad at math. None of that landed. I'd failed at the thing I was supposed to succeed at. That's what mattered.

What messes me up still is that I don't think the failure was necessarily about my intelligence or my work ethic. I think I just didn't have the kind of creative problem-solving brain that makes you good at olympiad math. I could memorize techniques and solve problems I'd seen before. I couldn't generate novel approaches to problems I'd never seen. That's not a failure of effort. That's a limitation of my brain.

But also, I don't regret the year I spent training. I learned mathematics at a depth that I probably wouldn't have otherwise. I developed stamina for difficult problems. I learned how to sit with the feeling of not knowing something and work through it anyway. Those things are useful. They're real.

What I'm still wrestling with is how to think about effort when effort doesn't result in success. There's this narrative that if you just work hard enough, you'll achieve whatever you want. But I worked as hard as I could, and I still failed. So either the narrative is wrong, or I didn't work hard enough, or I'm not capable of succeeding at this thing. I'm not sure which one it is.

My friends don't really know how much I still think about this. They probably think it's weird that I'm sad about something from a year ago that I ultimately overcame. But I think about it because it taught me something that nobody wanted me to learn: that there are real limits to what hard work can achieve. That sometimes you try your absolute best and you still fail. That has to be okay if I'm going to actually be okay.
