Bay fourteen had a jammed conveyor and I told Danny I could handle it myself, which was the wrong call, and I knew it was the wrong call about four seconds after I said it, but by then it was already out of my mouth and Danny had already walked off toward bay nine to deal with something else.

I worked night shifts at a distribution warehouse the summer between junior and senior year, ten p.m. to six a.m., mostly scanning and loading pallets alongside a crew of adults who'd been doing it for years and treated me, correctly, as the least experienced person on the floor. Danny was my shift lead, a guy in his forties who moved through the warehouse like he had a map of it memorized in his knees, and he had a rule about jammed equipment that he repeated probably every single shift: two people, always, no exceptions, because a conveyor jam under load can release fast and you do not want to be the only person standing there when it does.

I broke the rule because I was tired and it was three in the morning and asking for help felt, in that specific sleep-deprived state, like admitting I couldn't keep up, and I very badly wanted to keep up. So I climbed up to clear the jam alone. The belt released while my arm was still in the gap, not violently, nothing dramatic enough for an ambulance, but hard enough to leave a bruise that ran the length of my forearm and hard enough that I understood, immediately and completely, exactly why the rule existed.

Danny found me holding my arm at the water fountain a few minutes later. He didn't yell. That was somehow worse than yelling would have been. He just looked at the bruise starting to come up under my skin and asked, very evenly, whether I thought the rule was for people who didn't know what they were doing, or for everyone.

I didn't have a good answer. I still don't have a great one, honestly, months later, except that I think some part of me believed the rule applied to other people — to the actually inexperienced, to whoever the rule-makers had in mind when they wrote it — and not to me, because I'd been doing the job for six weeks by then and had started to feel like I understood it well enough to bend its edges a little. That's a specific kind of arrogance that I don't think you can be talked out of. You mostly just have to hurt yourself a small, correctable amount and let the lesson land the hard way.

The warehouse crew, I should say, is its own kind of community, one I didn't expect to find anything meaningful in when I took the job just for the paycheck. Nobody there knew me from school or church or any of the usual places people know each other. We knew each other from twelve hundred identical pallets a night and the specific rhythm of a shift that starts when everyone else's day is ending. There's a trust that builds in a place like that, quiet and mostly unspoken, built out of things like watching someone's back near a conveyor, or covering someone's break without being asked, or Danny walking the floor at 2 a.m. checking that everyone's where they're supposed to be like some tired, unglamorous shepherd.

I broke that trust a little, that night, not just the rule itself but the actual premise the rule is built on — that we look out for each other because none of us can fully look out for ourselves in a place with that much moving machinery. Danny didn't write me up. He didn't tell my parents. He just made me work the next three jams with him standing right there, not saying much, watching.

I think about that bruise sometimes, long faded now, when I'm tempted to skip a step somewhere else in my life because I'm tired and it feels efficient and nobody's watching. I don't think Danny ever fully forgot it either, not in a way he held against me, just in a way that changed how closely he watched me for the rest of that summer, and I understood why, and I never once resented him for it.
