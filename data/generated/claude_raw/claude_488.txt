There were twenty-two dead laptops in a store room behind the physics lab, stacked like bricks, and for two years I walked past them almost every day. Most were the same model the government gave out in 2019. In eleventh I asked our vice-principal if I could try to fix some, and after three weeks and a letter from my father he said I could take eight, on the condition that I did not touch anything with a school sticker still on it. They all had stickers. He meant it as a joke, I think.

The first three were easy and made me stupid. One needed a RAM stick reseated. One had a dead CMOS battery. One had a charging port that had come off its pads, and I paid a man in the market forty rupees to re-solder it while I watched. Three working laptops went back to the school in a month, and I was, briefly, the boy who fixed the computers. I let people say it.

The other five did not go like that. Two would not post at all, and I decided the problem was the BIOS, because that is what a forum said about that model, and I flashed a chip using a programmer I had borrowed and a file I had not verified. I did it to both boards in the same evening, which is the single dumbest thing in this essay. If I had done one, waited, and seen it fail, I would have had one left. Instead I had two boards that were now genuinely dead instead of maybe dead, and a third that I cracked around the hinge trying to open it the wrong way.

I did not tell anyone for eleven days. When the vice-principal asked, I said the parts were not available, which was not a lie and was not the truth either. The project was stopped in December, politely, with the remaining laptops put back in the store room. I have to say that nobody was angry with me. That made it worse in a way I still cannot explain properly.

My father and I have not agreed about this for a year and a half. He repairs air conditioners and washing machines and he has done it since he was nineteen, so he has an opinion, and his opinion is that the mistake was doing the work for free. He says a man who is not paid is not allowed to say no, and cannot ask for time, and cannot say I do not know this one, bring it back Thursday. He is not wrong about the pressure. I did rush because I wanted to show three more before the term ended. But I think he is defending the wrong thing. I did not brick those boards because nobody paid me. I bricked them because I could not admit, out loud, in front of a teacher, that I had reached the end of what I knew. He says that is the same thing. I say it is not, and we go around like this, usually at the dinner table, usually with my mother telling us to eat.

What I did next was smaller than the story deserves. I bought a cheap logic probe and a multimeter and spent last summer at my father's workbench, not on laptops, on things he was already fixing, mostly holding the torch. I learned to write down the symptom before touching anything, and to change one variable, and to keep the old part in a bag with the date on it. In July I asked the school for four laptops again and this time I gave them a page saying what I could do, what I could not, and what I would do if I damaged one. Two are working. One is a screen I cannot afford. One I have not opened, and I am not going to until I know what is wrong with it.

My father still thinks I should charge. I still think that is not the lesson. He was the one who drove me to the school in July with the four laptops in the back of the scooter, so I know what he actually believes, whatever he says at dinner.
