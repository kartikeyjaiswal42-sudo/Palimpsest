My family had owned a piece of land outside the city for as long as I can remember. It wasn't really developed or valuable—mostly it was just empty land with some trees on it and a lot of emotional weight from generations of family history. My grandfather bought it. My father was born there, technically, before they built the house in the city. My grandmother has spent every summer there for fifty years.

When I was fifteen, there was a real-estate developer who approached my father about buying the land. The offer was substantial—a lot more money than my father had expected. He was actually considering it seriously, which shocked everyone in my family.

My grandmother was absolutely furious. She said the land was not his to sell, that it belonged to the family, that selling it would be betraying everything it meant to own something that had been in the family for decades. She said my father should be ashamed for even considering it.

My father said the land was his legally and financially, and if he wanted to sell it, he had every right to. He said my grandmother needed to accept that people change and priorities change, and what had made sense to keep fifty years ago didn't necessarily make sense now. He said they couldn't afford the property taxes forever and that the money would actually improve our family's financial stability.

They had a real argument about this. My grandmother stopped speaking to my father for several weeks. The family was divided. Some aunts and uncles sided with my grandmother. Others thought my father was making a rational financial decision.

I sided with my grandmother, which I think was unfair to my father.

I remember telling him that he was being selfish, that some things matter more than money, that he should value what the land meant to the family rather than what some developer was willing to pay for it. I was righteous about it. I was absolutely certain that he was wrong.

But I was also sixteen, and I didn't have to pay property taxes or worry about financial stability or manage complex decisions that affected multiple generations. I was just holding onto nostalgia.

My father ended up not selling the land. He decided that what the land meant to my family outweighed the financial benefit of selling it. He made that choice not because I convinced him, but because he went through his own process of deciding what mattered.

The thing I was wrong about was assuming that I knew what should matter to him. I was wrong about thinking that the fact that I loved the land and had memories there meant he was wrong for considering selling it. Just because something mattered emotionally didn't mean it made financial sense to keep it. Those two things can both be true at the same time.

And even though my father ended up keeping the land, I still think he should have felt free to sell it if he wanted to. I think I should have supported his right to make that decision even if I disagreed with the decision itself. I was so busy being upset about the potential loss that I didn't consider what he actually needed from his family in that moment.

We've never really resolved this, and I'm not sure we will. My father kept the land partially because of family pressure. That's not a great reason to keep something. But I'm also not sure what would be different if I'd handled it differently—if I'd been less judgmental, more curious about his reasoning, more willing to acknowledge that his needs and concerns were legitimate even if they conflicted with what I wanted.

I think about this whenever I'm tempted to judge someone for a choice they're making with their own resources. I remember how certain I was that my father was wrong, and how little I actually understood about the full picture.
