My father's motorcycle has been broken for fifteen years. It's a 1983 Honda, blue and silver, and it stopped running sometime in my childhood. It lived in the garage behind stacks of boxes and forgotten equipment, gathering dust, visible but unused. My father never talked about it much, but I knew it had mattered to him once. He had photographs somewhere of himself riding it, younger than I've ever known him, looking free in a way he hasn't looked since.

Last summer, the summer before my junior year, I decided I was going to fix it.

I told my father my plan, and he was skeptical. We hadn't even agreed on what the problem was. The engine was seized, or the fuel system was shot, or both. Neither of us actually knew. I'd never rebuilt anything more complex than an IKEA bookshelf. My father had let it sit so long that he'd stopped believing restoration was possible.

But I was seventeen and stubborn and convinced that this could be simple. I watched YouTube videos. I bought tools. I spent the first week just clearing out the garage, dragging boxes of my childhood to the street, making space for this project that I was now deeply committed to.

I started with the engine. I drained the oil that had turned to sludge. I removed the carburetor, which was so corroded it took three days of soaking and scraping to get it clean. Every session left me with scraped hands and more questions than answers. Why was this bolt stripped? Why wouldn't the piston move? How was I supposed to know if I was fixing something or making it worse?

My father watched from the garage doorway for those first few weeks, but he didn't help. I think he was afraid. Afraid that I was making it worse. Afraid that his bike would stay broken and now it would be my fault. Afraid, maybe, of hoping for something he'd stopped believing in.

I failed more than I succeeded. I ordered the wrong part twice. I discovered that the frame itself had rust damage I couldn't fix. I spent an entire week trying to get a fuel line connected, only to discover I'd been using the wrong connector the whole time. I sat on the garage floor more than once wanting to quit, with my hands bleeding and the motorcycle looking exactly as broken as when I started.

By late July, I was exhausted. School was coming. I had spent my summer transforming myself into someone covered in grease and disappointment. My father finally came into the garage one afternoon and asked me why I was still doing this.

I didn't have a good answer. The motorcycle wasn't going to change my life. I wasn't learning a marketable skill. I wasn't gaining anything that made sense to put on a college application. And yet, I found that I couldn't stop. There was something about the work itself that had become necessary to me. The problem-solving. The commitment to something imperfect. The fact that every small success had to be fought for.

My father started helping after that. Quietly. He didn't take over. He just stood beside me and looked at the bike and said things like, "You might try WD-40 on that bolt," or "I think I remember the original fuel line was thinner." He shared memory and experience without taking back control.

We didn't finish that summer. The motorcycle still isn't running. But we got the engine to turn over for the first time in fifteen years. We got the fuel system working. We cleared rust from places we didn't know were rusted. It's closer to working than it has been since my childhood.

What I realized through this project is that my identity is tied to this kind of work. I need to do things that are difficult and uncertain and require both persistence and humility. I need to struggle with problems and ask for help without giving up. I need to work alongside people I love toward something that might not work out perfectly.

The motorcycle broke something in me too, I think. Or broke open something that was closed. It showed me who I am when I'm not trying to be impressive. Just someone willing to work, to fail, to try again, to learn from someone who came before me. That matters more than finishing.
