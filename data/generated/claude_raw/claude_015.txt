The robot stopped moving eleven seconds into our thirty-second autonomous run, wheels spinning in place while the arm we'd spent six weeks perfecting hung frozen an inch above the scoring peg, and I stood behind the field wall watching our entire regional competition end in front of four hundred people because of a single line of code I had written and never gone back to check.

I was the lead programmer, which at our school meant I was a junior who'd taught herself enough Java over two summers to be trusted with the autonomous routine, the part of the match where the robot runs on pre-written code with no human control, no joystick, nothing but whatever logic you'd loaded onto it that morning. I'd written a sensor threshold that worked perfectly in our garage, where the lighting never changed, and I hadn't accounted for the competition floor's harsher overhead lights throwing off the exact reading my code depended on to know when to stop the arm. It was a small mistake. It cost us the match anyway, and with it, our shot at advancing.

My first instinct, standing there while our captain, a senior named Idris, tried to get the robot moving again with the manual override and couldn't, was to decide I was done. Not just done with that match — done with robotics generally, done with the specific feeling of being the person whose one overlooked variable had erased six weeks of eleven other people's work. I remember doing the math in my head, right there at the wall, on how many hours the team had put in and how thoroughly I'd wasted them with something as small as an untested light threshold.

What changed my mind wasn't a pep talk, and nobody on the team gave me one anyway — most of them were too busy dismantling the robot for the drive home to spend much time consoling me. It was watching Idris, twenty minutes later, sitting on the equipment case with the robot's control board open on his lap, already tracing the sensor logic back to find exactly where the threshold had failed, not looking for someone to blame, just looking for the actual cause, the way you'd look for a leak in a pipe. He found it in about ten minutes. He held up the board, showed me the line, and said, "yeah, this needed a wider tolerance range, that's a two-minute fix," in the same tone he'd use to point out we were low on zip ties.

I had expected the failure to feel like the end of something, and instead I watched a seventeen-year-old treat it like a diagnostic step in a much longer process, one bad data point among the hundreds we'd collect before the next competition. That was the moment I actually changed my mind — not about robotics being fun, I'd already known that, but about what a failure like this one was supposed to mean. I had been treating the mistake as a verdict on whether I belonged on the team, when Idris was treating it as exactly what it was, a bug, findable and fixable, no more personal than a stripped screw.

We fixed the threshold that night in the school parking lot, headlights from someone's car aimed at the robot so we could see the sensor readout. It didn't undo the loss. We didn't advance that year. But I stayed on the team, wrote a wider tolerance into everything after that, and started treating every failed run in practice the way Idris had treated ours on stage — as information, not indictment. I think about that specific ten minutes on the equipment case more than I think about the actual match, because it's the reason I'm still doing this at all, still writing code I test twice as carefully now, still willing to be the person standing behind the wall when something goes wrong, because something eventually always does, and the only real choice is what you do in the ten minutes after.
