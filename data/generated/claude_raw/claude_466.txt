The library had a card catalog. This is the first thing you absolutely need to understand about this particular library. Not a digital system. Not a computer database or anything modern. A card catalog. Actual wooden card catalog cabinets that held thousands of handwritten index cards organized by the Dewey Decimal System. It was beautiful in an archaic way, genuinely beautiful to look at, and it was also completely useless because nobody knew how to use it and nothing in it was accurate anymore.

The school librarian, Mr. Hammond, asked me to reorganize the library in my junior year. I was the kind of student who read a lot and seemed organized and didn't mind spending time in the library during lunch. That made me, in his mind, the perfect person for the job. He said he wanted to update the system. He wanted the card catalog to actually reflect what books were in the building, because apparently the previous librarian had stopped updating it in 2007. Books had been donated. Books had been lost. Books had moved. The index was years out of date.

He gave me a hand-written list of the current inventory and told me I could work on it piecemeal over the semester. A few cards a day. No pressure. He said he'd pay me minimum wage for my time, which was generous, except the job took about three times longer than either of us anticipated.

The first thing I realized was that the card catalog was a fascinating artifact. Each card was handwritten, some in ink from the '80s, some updated in pen. You could see the history of the library through them. You could see what books were popular at different times based on the wear patterns on the cards. You could see where someone had crossed something out and written the correction. It was tactile history. It was beautiful.

The second thing I realized was that updating it was going to require me to actually interact with the books. To physically go into the stacks, find them, confirm they existed, update the information. The library had grown since anyone had properly organized it. The organization had become almost intentional in its chaos. Some books were shelved where they were supposed to be. Some books were just on a shelf somewhere. Some books that were in the inventory didn't actually exist anymore. Some books that existed weren't in the inventory at all.

I started creating a system. I'd work through each call number section and physically verify every single book. I'd update the cards. I'd note books that needed to be ordered. I'd find books that were missing from the catalog and create new cards for them. It was tedious and meticulous and it took forever.

But here's what actually happened. As I worked through the library section by section, systematically and methodically, the entire building became familiar to me in a completely different way than it had ever been. I knew where everything was. I understood the logic of the Dewey Decimal System not as an abstract concept you learn in a class but as something deeply functional—how it grouped knowledge together, what kinds of books ended up next to each other, how the system reflected the actual way human beings categorize and organize their understanding of the world.

I also got to know Mr. Hammond. We'd talk about books while I worked. He'd come check on my progress and we'd end up having these conversations about why organizing information matters, about how libraries are about more than just storing books. He talked about the library as a democratic institution, a place where knowledge was supposed to be accessible to everyone. He talked about how outdated systems become useless systems, no matter how beautiful.

By the end of the semester I hadn't finished the entire catalog. But I'd reorganized three-quarters of it. I'd created a new system where the physical shelves actually reflected the index. I'd discovered books nobody knew existed. I'd learned what work felt like when it was meticulous and kind of boring but mattered.

What I grew from was understanding that some accomplishments are quiet. That organizing a library catalog is not glamorous or impressive to other people. But to Mr. Hammond, who suddenly had a usable system, and to the students who could actually find books they were looking for, it mattered. And doing something that mattered, even in a small way to a small group of people, changed how I thought about what kinds of work were worth doing.

That card catalog taught me that meaning doesn't have to be big to be real.
