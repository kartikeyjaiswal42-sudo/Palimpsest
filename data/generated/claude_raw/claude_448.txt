My mother says I dance like someone apologising. She said it the first week, standing in the doorway of the bedroom while I tried to hold my wrist in the shape the video showed, and she has said it in some form every week since, and I have never once agreed with her, and I still do not.

The festival was in August, in the community hall behind the Sikh temple on Fenton Road, and I did not want to go. I want to be honest about that because the version of this story where I discovered my heritage and wept into a dupatta is not the version that happened. I did not want to go because I had been going since I was six and every year it was the same folding chairs, the same aunties asking which university, the same three uncles arguing about the sound system. My mother wanted me to perform. Not attend — perform. Giddha, with the group of women she has known since she came here in 1998. She said, you are seventeen, next year you will be somewhere else, do this one thing.

So I learned. Six weeks, four evenings a week, in the bedroom with the door shut because the living room had my brother in it. My mother taught me the boliyan first, the couplets you call out, and this was the beginning of the argument. She wanted me to shout them. Not sing — shout, the way you would shout across a field to a woman working three rows over. I could not do it. I could get my voice to about half the volume she wanted and then something in my throat would close. She would say louder and I would say I am being loud and she would say no, that is your school voice.

Here is what I think and what I have not been able to make her understand. She grew up where the dance was the ordinary way a person moved. I grew up where it is a thing you take out of a box. When I shout the boliyan in a bedroom in a semi-detached house on a Tuesday, I am not doing what she did at nineteen. I am performing what she did at nineteen. The shame in my throat is not shame about being Punjabi. It is the shame of being watched doing something I have not earned. She hears that as a smaller version of the same thing. She hears rejection. And I cannot get the distinction across without it sounding like an excuse, which possibly it is.

I danced at the festival. I was fine. The clapping was the clapping you get for a seventeen-year-old who tried. Afterward she pressed her hand flat against my back, which is what she does instead of hugging, and she said, better. Not good. Better.

We had the argument again in November. We will have it again. I have started to think this is not a problem I am supposed to solve, that some inheritances arrive as arguments rather than as objects, and that the argument is the inheritance. I do not know if she would accept that. She would probably say I am making a theory out of not practising enough. She might be right. I still cannot shout the boliyan properly, and I still do not think it is because I am ashamed.
