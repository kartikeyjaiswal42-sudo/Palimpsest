The insomnia started around junior year and it just didn't stop.

I couldn't fall asleep at night. I'd lie in bed for hours and just stare at the ceiling. My mind would start racing about things—school, college, whether people liked me, random conversations I'd had in the cafeteria that I'd suddenly realize sounded awkward. I'd check my phone and see that it was two in the morning and I had to be up at six, and that anxiety about not getting sleep would make it even harder to fall asleep.

After about a month of this, I went to the doctor. She said it was probably stress and prescribed some relaxation techniques. Meditation. Progressive muscle relaxation. Keeping the room cool. All the standard advice.

None of it worked.

By month four of insomnia, I was a mess. I was falling asleep in classes. I was getting sick more frequently—apparently sleep deprivation tanks your immune system. I was emotional about stupid things. I was struggling to concentrate. My grades were slipping. My parents were worried. I was convinced that I'd permanently broken my brain somehow and I'd never be able to sleep normally again.

I went back to the doctor. She referred me to a sleep specialist, but the first appointment was three months out. I was supposed to just suffer through this.

So I developed a routine.

Around eleven at night, I'd start getting ready for bed even though I knew I wouldn't sleep. I'd take a warm shower. I'd make tea. I'd read something that was boring—not something I wanted to read, something deliberately tedious. I'd sit in my room with soft music playing. I'd journal about whatever was keeping me awake. I'd do breathing exercises. I'd lie there and just accept that I might not sleep, and somehow that acceptance made things slightly better.

I still didn't sleep very well. But I moved from getting maybe two hours to getting maybe four or five. And the routine itself became something I could count on, something predictable in a period where sleep felt absolutely random.

About six months in, something shifted. I still wasn't sleeping perfectly, but I was sleeping more consistently. My body had adapted to the routine. The anxiety about not sleeping had decreased because I had an actual process for managing the insomnia instead of just lying there panicking.

What I realized later was that the insomnia had been the symptom of a bigger stress issue that I eventually addressed—I talked to a therapist about test anxiety and perfectionism and all the things that were making my brain unable to shut off at night. That's what actually fixed the underlying problem.

But while I was waiting to address the underlying problem, the thing that helped me survive was having a routine. Having something I could do every single night that felt like I was taking action, even when the action wasn't actually making me sleep. Having a process made the insomnia less terrifying.

I still have nights where sleep doesn't come easily. But I'm not afraid of those nights anymore because I know I have tools and I know my body can function even on less sleep than I'd prefer. The insomnia taught me something about resilience and adaptation that I probably wouldn't have learned otherwise.
