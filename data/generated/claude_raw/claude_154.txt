When we adopted Maya from the shelter, the volunteers told us she'd been badly abused. She wouldn't make eye contact. She wouldn't take treats from our hands. She was afraid of sudden movements, which meant our normal household felt like a minefield of startling events. My mum wanted to return her after two days. I convinced them to give her a month.

The thing was, I read everything I could find about helping rescue dogs adjust. Most sources said you needed patience, consistency, and time. Some talked about specific training techniques. But Maya wasn't a regular scared dog. She seemed to have learned that the world was dangerous in a way that went deeper than fear. She didn't just flinch at loud noises. She seemed to expect that trusting anyone would result in pain.

Every evening after school, I'd sit on the kitchen floor while Maya watched me from across the room. I wouldn't move toward her or call her over. I'd just sit there for twenty or thirty minutes, reading or doing homework, treating her presence as completely normal. It was a small, repeated thing that my research said might help her begin to recalibrate her understanding of what humans were like.

For weeks, nothing changed.

I remember one specific afternoon in November when I sat there on that cold floor and felt genuinely frustrated. Everyone talked about rescue dogs making miraculous transformations, but this didn't feel miraculous. It felt like I was doing nothing, wasting time on a dog who was never going to trust anyone. I thought about telling my parents that they'd been right to want to return her, that we should find her a home where maybe a professional trainer could do something we couldn't.

Then Maya moved.

She didn't come to me. She just shifted her position slightly, moving about a foot closer than she'd ever positioned herself before. She still wasn't looking at me. She was actually looking away. But she was closer. I remember not wanting to ruin it by reacting too much, by suddenly trying to pet her or make a big deal of it. I just sat with that small change and felt something shift in my own understanding of what I was doing.

After that, the increments were still small. Two weeks later, she ate a treat from my open palm, keeping her head turned away. Another month passed before she let me touch her shoulder for half a second. But each small step meant something. Each one was proof that she was slowly learning something different about what it meant to interact with a person.

We never got her to a state where she became a normal, relaxed dog. She remained anxious, still struggled with certain situations. But now, two years later, she seeks me out sometimes. She'll put her head on my lap in the evening. She's learned to play, something the shelter staff said she'd probably never do. She's learned that staying near me doesn't lead to harm.

Looking back, I think about how those repeated small sessions on the kitchen floor taught me something important about the kind of person I want to be. I learned that you can't force transformation. You can only show up consistently, day after day, proving through repetition that you're trustworthy. You can be patient with someone's fear. You can believe in their capacity to change even when the changes are so small they're almost invisible.

That kind of slow work mattered. It still does.
