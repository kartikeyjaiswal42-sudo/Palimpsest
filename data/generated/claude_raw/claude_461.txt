There's a small enamel pin shaped like a maple leaf sitting on the corkboard above my desk, slightly bent at one corner from the year it spent riding around in the front pocket of a backpack, and it's the only thing left in my room from the year Kaisa lived with us.

She came from a small town outside Turku, an exchange student for what was supposed to be one academic year, arriving the August before tenth grade with two suitcases and this pin, which she'd bought at the airport in Helsinki on her way out, not because it meant anything yet but because, she told me later, she wanted something small in her pocket to remember what home looked like before she left it behind for a year.

We shared a room for the first two months, an arrangement my parents apologized for constantly and Kaisa never once complained about, and I remember being nervous around her at first in a way I hadn't expected — not because of any language barrier, her English was better than most people's at our school, but because I didn't know how to be a sister to someone I'd known for exactly nine days. The pin came up early. I noticed it on her jacket the first week and asked about it, and she took it off and put it in my hand and told me about the town, the specific gray-green color of the water near her house in autumn, her grandmother's dog, and I remember thinking, holding this small piece of bent metal, that she'd handed me something much bigger than a pin.

The year went the way exchange years go — homesickness in October that she tried to hide and mostly couldn't, a Christmas where she video-called home for six hours straight and came out of her room red-eyed and quiet, and then, slowly, real belonging, a version of her that laughed at our family's specific jokes and argued with my dad about politics at dinner like she'd been doing it for years instead of months. By spring she wasn't a guest anymore. She was just my sister, in every sense that mattered, for eleven months.

She left in June, and at the airport, in the fifteen minutes before she had to go through security, she took the pin off her jacket again and put it directly into my hand, closed my fingers over it, and said she wanted me to have the piece of home she'd carried the whole way here, because I'd become part of what home meant to her by the time she was leaving.

I haven't seen Kaisa in person in two years, though we message constantly, and I know exactly which corner of a map the gray-green water is in even though I've never stood next to it myself. The pin stays on the corkboard, not in a drawer, where I see it every single day, a small bent object that somehow holds an entire year inside it — the nervous first week, the homesick October, the version of family neither of us expected to build with someone who was only supposed to be passing through.
