The judge's paper said "aggressive" three times in the same paragraph, and not as a compliment, and I was still holding it in the hallway outside the round room when my partner Sam came out and asked how bad it was.

We'd lost in the semifinal of States to a team we'd beaten twice that season. I had gone into the round the same way I went into every round — fast, loaded, interrupting cross-ex the second I heard an opening, treating every exchange like a wall I needed to be first to climb over. It had worked all year. It worked right up until it didn't, against a pair from a school two hours north whose first speaker, a quiet sophomore, just kept letting me finish, nodding, and then dismantling exactly what I'd said with about a third of the words I'd used to say it.

The judge's ballot didn't say I was wrong on the substance. It said something worse, in a way — it said the judge had stopped being able to tell what I actually believed underneath all the speed and interruption, that somewhere around my third rebuttal I'd become a wall of noise instead of an argument, and that the other team's stillness had read, in the room, as confidence, while my volume had started to read as something closer to panic.

I didn't believe it at first. I told Sam the judge just liked their style better, some judges do, it's subjective, we'd get a different panel next time and win easily. I said all this in the parking lot afterward, fast, the same way I'd argued the whole round, and Sam let me finish — actually let me finish, didn't interrupt, which I noticed even in the state I was in — and then said, quietly, that maybe I should read the ballot again when I wasn't still angry.

I read it again that night. Then again a week later. It said the same thing both times, which is usually how you know a criticism is actually true and not just a bad mood on someone's part.

What changed wasn't sudden, and it didn't happen in that hallway, even though that's the moment I keep coming back to when I try to locate it. It happened slowly, over the following month, in practice rounds where I made myself do something that felt almost physically uncomfortable — stop talking. Let my opponent finish a sentence I already knew, in the first four words, that I disagreed with. Wait a full two seconds before responding instead of cutting in on the last syllable of their point. Sam started timing my pauses in practice, actually holding a stopwatch, because I genuinely could not tell the difference between two seconds and half a second without external proof.

The sophomore from that semifinal round beat me again later that season, at a different tournament, in a round I lost on a much narrower margin, arguing much more carefully than I had before. I remember shaking her hand afterward and asking, somewhat pathetically, how she'd learned to do that — the stillness, the letting people finish. She said her old coach used to tell her that the person who's actually right doesn't need to talk over anyone to prove it, that speed is what you use when you're not sure your argument survives someone else finishing their sentence.

I've thought about that sentence more than almost anything anyone's said to me in four years of doing this. I don't think I've fully become the debater she already was that day. I still catch myself starting to cut someone off, some reflex from three years of training myself the opposite way, and I have to physically stop my own mouth mid-syllable. But I keep the ballot from that semifinal in the folder with my other tournament papers, the good ones and the bad ones together, and it's the one I go back to first when I'm packing for a new round, before any of the ones that say I won.
