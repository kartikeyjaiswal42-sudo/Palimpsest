For almost a year after my grandmother's diagnosis, I avoided reading anything about dementia at all. My mom would leave pamphlets on the kitchen counter from the neurologist's office and I'd walk right past them, not out of denial exactly, more because it felt like studying the thing that was taking her apart would somehow make it more real, more official, like I was signing off on it just by getting curious.

That changed on a random Tuesday in February, for a reason I still can't fully explain. My grandmother — we call her Bibi — looked right at me during a visit and asked my mother, politely, who the nice young man was. Not upset. Just asking, like I was a new nurse she hadn't met yet. I went home that night and, instead of avoiding it the way I'd been avoiding it for months, I opened my laptop and just started reading. Not pamphlets this time. Actual papers, or at least the parts of actual papers I could understand, about how memory is stored, how it's retrieved, why some memories survive this kind of damage and others don't.

I didn't plan to fall into it the way I did. I read for four hours that first night, past midnight, way past when I should've stopped for a physics test the next morning. I read about how procedural memory — the stuff your body just knows, like riding a bike, or in Bibi's case, kneading dough for her flatbread — tends to survive far longer than the memory of names or recent events, because it's stored differently, in different circuitry, less dependent on the parts of the brain that plaques and tangles hit first. That's why she can still make perfect roti with her eyes basically closed but couldn't tell you what she ate for lunch an hour ago. Once I understood that, I started noticing it everywhere in her, little pockets of intact memory floating inside a system that was failing everywhere else, and I couldn't stop trying to map which was which.

I don't know exactly why this is the thing that captivates me instead of just devastating me, though I think it's probably both at once. There's something almost unbearably interesting about a disease that doesn't erase a person evenly — it takes some rooms in the house and leaves others completely untouched, and you never know in advance which room is next. I've spent whole Saturday afternoons reading about hippocampal atrophy and tau proteins when I was supposed to be doing college applications, and I genuinely lose track of time doing it, which almost never happens to me with schoolwork.

I mostly turn to Oliver Sacks now, whose case studies read less like textbooks and more like actual stories about actual people, which somehow makes the science land harder than a diagram ever could. I also email a neurologist at the university two towns over, a woman named Dr. Petrosyan who runs a memory clinic and answered my first cold, slightly awkward message with a full page of reading recommendations, which honestly surprised me — I hadn't expected a real researcher to bother with a high schooler's questions, and she's answered three more emails since.

I still don't fully understand my own turn from avoiding this topic to being unable to stop reading about it. Maybe understanding the mechanism made the loss feel less random, more like something with a shape I could hold, instead of just a woman disappearing without a reason attached. I know the reading won't fix anything for Bibi. I know it probably won't even make visiting her easier most days. But somewhere between the pamphlets I used to walk past and the papers I now read at midnight, the fear turned into something closer to attention, and attention, it turns out, is a thing I can actually do something with.
