My grandmother's pincushion is a red tomato with a little strawberry hanging off it by a thread, and half the pins in it don't match anymore because I've been losing hers one at a time for three years and replacing them with whatever's in the jar. It sits on my sewing table, which is really just the kitchen table with a sheet under it so I don't scratch the wood, and it has been there through four shows now at the Millbrook Community Playhouse, where I'm the seventeen-year-old they somehow trust to run wardrobe for eighty adults who mostly can't sew a button.

I got the job because nobody else wanted it. That's the honest version. I said yes because my grandmother taught me to sew when I was nine, mostly Halloween costumes, and it felt like something I could actually be useful at, unlike, say, the acting, which I have zero talent for and everyone agrees on that, including me.

The failure happened during Beauty and the Beast, last winter. I was in charge of the Beast costume — not just a costume, honestly, more like an engineering project, this huge layered coat with fake fur and shoulder pieces built up on foam to make him look bigger than he is, plus a cape that had to move right under stage lights. I'd never built anything with that much structure before. I measured our Beast, a guy named Trevor who works at the hardware store during the day, back in October, and then built the whole thing over two months mostly working off those first measurements, because I was proud and didn't want to keep bothering him for fittings.

Big mistake. Huge, actually. Trevor lost about fifteen pounds over the fall — some health thing, nothing dramatic, he's fine now — and nobody told me because why would they, it's not the kind of thing you announce, and I never scheduled a second fitting because I was so sure I already had it right. Dress rehearsal, four days before opening. He puts the coat on. The shoulders sit an inch too wide on each side, which sounds small, but on stage, under lights, it read as a man wearing a coat built for someone else. Which, it turns out, he was.

I sat in the greenroom after that rehearsal and did not handle it well. I cried, actually, in the bathroom, which I'm only admitting because this essay is supposed to be honest. Four days. A structural costume that had taken two months. My director, a woman named Diane who has run that theatre for something like thirty years, found me and did not say it would be fine, because it might not have been, and I appreciated that she didn't lie to me about it.

What I did instead was take the coat apart at the shoulder seams — actual surgery on something I'd already finished — and rebuild just that section, working off Trevor himself this time instead of a two-month-old number on an index card. I used my grandmother's pincushion the whole time, the actual physical object, pinning and repinning that seam probably nine times before it hung right, losing two more of her pins to the carpet in the process. I didn't sleep much those four days. The coat was ready by dress on the last night before opening, not before, and it looked right, and nobody in the audience ever knew any of this happened.

Here's what I actually took from it, and it isn't some tidy lesson about perseverance, because I think that undersells what happened. What I learned is that I trust my own first measurement too much, and that trusting it is a kind of laziness dressed up as confidence. I don't build anything now without a second fitting scheduled in, no matter how sure I feel walking out of the first one. The pincushion still sits on my table missing more of its original pins every season, and I like that about it, actually — it isn't the same object it was when she gave it to me, and neither, honestly, am I.

I still have the shoulder piece I cut off that night, in a drawer with extra buttons and thread that doesn't match anything. Every so often I find it and remember exactly what four days with no sleep feels like, and exactly what it's for.
