The rejection emails for the varsity soccer team came in a pattern. Freshman year, then sophomore, then junior. I printed them out, which seems odd now, and kept them in the desk drawer with my cleats.

Those Adidas cleats were expensive. My mom bought them before freshman year, when I believed that wanting something badly was the same as being good at it. Black with white stripes, they wore in at the heel from constantly putting them on and taking them off, checking to make sure they were still there, still real.

After freshman year, I switched to club soccer. It was fine. I got to play. But it wasn't varsity, and I couldn't stop thinking about that distinction, as if there was something fundamentally different about the players on that team. Something I lacked.

By sophomore year, I had developed a ritual. The night before tryouts, I would take out the cleats and wear them around my room for an hour. I would imagine what it would feel like to be good enough. I would practice the things I would say to the coach if he cut me, trying out different levels of grace and dignity. Spoiler: there is no graceful way to be told you're not good enough. There's just the sound of your name not being called during the final roster announcement.

The emails got shorter each year. Freshman: "Thank you for your effort." Sophomore: "Better luck next year." Junior: Just a list of names. I wasn't even on it to not be on it.

I didn't try out senior year. I wore the cleats sometimes anyway because they were molded to my feet and no other shoe fit quite right. People would ask if I was going to try out again, and I would say no, and I meant it, but it wasn't because I had given up. It was something else, something quieter than giving up.

What I realized—and this took three years, which is embarrassing—is that I had built my entire identity around being someone who wanted something I couldn't have. The cleats in my drawer weren't actually about soccer. They were about refusing to accept that some things don't work out no matter how much you want them. They were about the person I wanted to be, not the person I actually was.

In senior year, I stopped thinking about varsity. I played club soccer because I loved the sport, not because it was a stepping stone to something else. Somewhere in that shift, the cleats stopped being a symbol of failure and started being just cleats, good shoes that fit my feet.

I think I finally understood what I had been fighting against. It wasn't the coach or the team or even my own abilities. It was the belief that rejection meant something was wrong with me. It turns out rejection just means you didn't fit into that particular place. The person I turned out to be fit better elsewhere. The person I became wasn't better because of varsity. I became better by accepting that varsity wasn't mine, and finding what was.
