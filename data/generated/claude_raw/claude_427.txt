I signed up to be a scribe for a blind student named Idris because a sign-up sheet was taped to the counseling office door and I had a free period, and I want to be honest that my first reason for doing it was almost that thin. I did not know Idris. I knew he sat two rows over in AP Biology with a braille display and a cane folded under his desk, and that he laughed loudly at things that were not always funny, which I later learned was on purpose, a way of making a room comfortable with him before it had the chance to be awkward.

Being a scribe meant reading exam questions aloud and writing down his answers exactly as he said them, word for word, no editing, no fixing. It sounds simple. For the first few tests it was simple, mostly multiple choice, mostly quiet rooms. Then came the AP Bio free-response section in May, ninety minutes, three long questions about cellular respiration and gene expression, the kind of exam where a wrong transcription could cost real points on something he actually understood.

I got tired around the second question. That's the plain truth of it. I'd read for over an hour, my voice going dry, and when he started dictating his answer about the electron transport chain, he used the phrase "proton gradient" and I wrote "proton gradient," and later, when he corrected a sentence and asked me to change "increases" to "decreases" two lines up, I found the wrong line. I fixed the wrong sentence. He asked me to read it back to confirm, the way he always did, and I read back what I'd written, which was correct in the spot I'd changed and now wrong in the spot I hadn't touched, because in swapping the word I'd apparently also cut the original clause that made the sentence make sense at all.

He caught it. Of course he caught it — it's his own reasoning, and even without seeing the page he could hear that what I read back didn't sound like something he'd said. We fixed it together, slowly, with four minutes left on the section, both of us more rattled than we let on. He got the score he needed. I only found out weeks later, when the results came back, that it had been close.

What I think about is not the mistake itself so much as the ten seconds before he caught it, when I read the sentence back and thought it sounded fine. I had gotten comfortable enough in the role to stop double-checking my own hearing, to trust the fatigue in my own head over the precision his work actually required, and comfort turned out to be exactly the wrong thing to feel in that chair. Being part of that arrangement, week after week, had made me feel like I belonged to something — his exams, his effort, the strange tight community of just the two of us and a proctor at the back of the room — and belonging made me careless in a way that a stranger might not have been.

I still scribe for him. He never brought the free-response mistake up again, which somehow makes me think about it more, not less. I check every line twice now, out loud, before I let either of us move on.
