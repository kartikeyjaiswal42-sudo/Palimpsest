The radio station I started sophomore year broadcasts to exactly one place: the cafeteria speakers, during the twenty-eight minutes of lunch, on a system left over from some previous administration that nobody had used in years. I found the equipment in a closet off the AV room while looking for extension cords for something else entirely, asked a teacher if I could use it, and got a yes mostly because nobody thought it would go anywhere.

For the first six weeks, close to nobody listened. I know this because I could watch the cafeteria while I talked, and people kept eating and talking exactly as they had before I started, like the speakers were just producing background noise indistinguishable from the hum of the ice machine. I'd planned playlists, prepared little segments about music or school events, and most days I was talking into a microphone that fed a room where I was pretty sure I was the only one paying attention to what came out of it.

I kept doing it anyway. Every single school day, I'd get to the cafeteria fifteen minutes before lunch started to set up, and I'd run the full twenty-eight minutes whether three people looked up or nobody did. I didn't have a plan for how this was supposed to turn a corner. I just decided that if I stopped the first time it felt pointless, it would always feel pointless, and the only way to find out if it could become something else was to keep showing up on the days it clearly wasn't working.

Somewhere around week nine, a kid I didn't know came up afterward and asked if I could play a specific song the next day, for his friend's birthday. I did. The day after that, someone else asked for something. Neither of these felt like much on their own, but they were the first evidence that the room had actually been listening the whole time, quietly, without me knowing it. By the spring, I had a rotating cast of about eight people asking for shoutouts or songs most weeks, and the cafeteria had gotten noticeably quieter during the mornings I gave school announcements, which I hadn't expected at all.

Nothing about the routine itself changed much between week one and week twenty. I still showed up fifteen minutes early, still ran the same twenty-eight minutes, still talked into a microphone in a cafeteria that mostly ignored me for the first two months. What changed was that I stopped needing daily proof that it mattered in order to keep doing it, and I think that's the actual obstacle I was up against, more than the silence itself — the temptation to quit something the moment it stops giving you evidence that it's working.

I still run the station most days, now with two other students helping. It's not big. It's a cafeteria at lunch, not an actual broadcast reaching anyone outside a hundred-foot radius. But I think the six weeks nobody was listening taught me something I wouldn't have learned if it had worked immediately, which is that most things worth building don't announce the moment they start working. You just keep showing up until, one day, someone asks for a song.
