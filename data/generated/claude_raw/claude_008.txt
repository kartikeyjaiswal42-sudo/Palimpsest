What I taught myself, standing behind the register at my family's shop, was how to tell when somebody is about to ask for credit, and my father and I have never agreed about what to do with that.

The shop is on the corner of a street with four hundred flats above it. We sell milk, bread, eggs, sweets, phone recharge, and the specific brand of hair oil that the aunties on the third floor will walk past two other shops to get. I have worked there since I was eleven, first counting change and then, from about fourteen, alone on Sunday mornings and every evening from six.

Nobody taught me the reading. My father certainly did not; he would say there is nothing to teach because everyone is the same and you just serve them. But there is something to teach, and I learned it the way you learn anything with no instruction, which is by being wrong repeatedly in public.

The signs are small and boring. A person asking for credit will hover near the rack by the door for slightly too long, letting the queue clear, because they do not want to say it in front of anyone. They will buy less than usual and it will be the wrong things — atta and dal, no biscuits, nothing for a child. They will ask a question about the price of something they have bought every week for six years. And almost always, right before, they will say something about the weather.

I know maybe thirty faces this way. I can tell you who is in trouble on our street before their family knows.

My father's rule is that we give credit to anyone who asks, in the book, no interest, no limit, and we do not chase it. He has run the shop this way for twenty-two years and there is a ledger under the counter with entries in it from 2009 that will never be paid. He does not think of it as charity. He thinks of it as the price of being a shop on a street rather than a shop on a highway.

My position, which I have argued with him perhaps a hundred times, is that we should offer before they ask. That if I can see it coming from the door, and I can, then making them stand there and construct the sentence is a cruelty we are choosing. Put it in the book quietly. Say the machine is down and I will note it. Spare them the asking.

He says no. He says absolutely not, and this is the only subject on which my father raises his voice at me. His argument is that the moment I decide who needs help before they have said so, I have made myself a judge of the street, and I will be wrong, and I will be wrong in a way that humiliates a man who was only buying dal because his wife is away. He says the asking is not the cruelty. He says the asking is the person keeping their own account of themselves, and it is not mine to take.

We have not resolved this. I do not think we will. Last winter I did it my way with a man from the second floor — I said, take it, pay me whenever, before he had opened his mouth — and he did not come back to the shop for eleven weeks, and when he did he paid the whole amount in cash and did not look at me. My father never mentioned it. He did not have to.

I still think I was right about the man and wrong about the eleven weeks, which is possibly the same as being wrong.

I still watch the door. I have not stopped learning it. I have only stopped acting on it, which is its own kind of unfinished argument.
