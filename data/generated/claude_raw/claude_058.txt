We spent three weeks planning the food drive. I mean we actually spent time on it—my friend Anaya and I, along with a few other people from student council. We made spreadsheets, calculated how much a family of four would need to survive for a month, reached out to local businesses to see if they'd contribute. We were going to collect five hundred kilos of food. That was our number, our goal, the thing we told everyone we were aiming for.

The problem was that we believed it.

I think we genuinely believed it the way you believe things when you're seventeen and you've just decided you're going to organize something and suddenly you can picture it happening exactly as you've planned it. We told the school, we told the local temple, we posted on social media. We said we were collecting five hundred kilos of food for families in the area who didn't have enough. It sounded real because we said it with confidence. We had a number.

By the end of the two-week collection period, we had a hundred and thirty-seven kilos. People brought things in, sure. A family brought rice. Someone's mother sent dal. We got some canned vegetables. But the gap between what we'd promised and what we'd actually collected was so enormous it felt obscene.

The night we counted everything, I sat in the school gymnasium looking at the piles of food and felt absolutely sick. I'd been wrong about what was possible. I'd overestimated how much people cared, or how much they were willing to act on that care. I'd been wrong in the way that matters most—I'd made promises on behalf of a community that turned out not to be ready to make those promises.

Anaya wanted to just give out what we had and consider it done. I understood that impulse. It was the easy way out. But I couldn't stop thinking about the people we'd told would receive this food. I couldn't stop thinking about us going to them and saying, "We collected five hundred kilos," when we only had a little over a quarter of that.

So I told my parents about it.

This is where things got complicated, because my father disagreed with how I wanted to handle it. He said we should just give out what we'd collected, that trying to do more would exhaust us, that the families would be grateful for what they got and we shouldn't be so hard on ourselves. We'd tried, he said. We'd put in effort. That mattered. He wasn't wrong, exactly. But he was also saying we should keep the lie intact—the lie of the five hundred kilos, the lie that we'd managed to do what we'd promised.

I wanted to tell people the truth. I wanted to say that we'd failed at our goal, but here's what we'd actually managed, and here's what the actual need looks like, and here's what would actually help if people wanted to participate. I thought that truth, even though it was a failure, was more honest and more useful than pretending everything had gone according to plan.

We didn't resolve this. We still don't, really. My father was right that the families who received the food were grateful, that a hundred and thirty-seven kilos was better than zero. He was right that we put in genuine effort and that the effort was valuable. But I'm convinced he was wrong that we should have just accepted the gap between what we promised and what we delivered, and moved on.

I think about what we could have done differently. We could have set a more realistic goal. We could have been more transparent in our communications about what we were actually collecting. We could have kept the collection open longer, or adjusted our strategy when it became clear we weren't reaching our initial target.

But mostly I think about how powerful it is to just say, "I was wrong about this." How important that is. How much harder it is than saying it after the fact.

I still think I was right to push for honesty, even though it created conflict with my father. But I also understand now what he was trying to protect—he wanted me not to feel like I'd failed, and I wanted accountability for having promised something we couldn't deliver. Maybe both things can be true at the same time. Maybe the failure matters and the effort matters. Maybe I don't get to decide which one matters more.
