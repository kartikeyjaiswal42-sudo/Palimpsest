There is a nail beside our kitchen door with last year's calendar hanging on it, back side out, and a pencil on a string. Twice a day, after the milking, I write two numbers on it. Morning litres, evening litres. I have done this since I was thirteen. That is the entire habit, and it is the most useful thing I have ever taught myself.

Nobody showed me how to keep accounts. I started because our neighbour, who runs the kirana shop, has a long red ledger he writes in whenever anyone takes something on credit, and I liked how sure of himself he looked doing it. My first version was silly. I recorded only the good days, because a day when Sona gave three litres was depressing to write down. It took me almost a year to work out on my own that a record you edit is not a record, it is a mood.

Then came the argument about whether to keep the cows at all. It went on for most of a year, on and off, usually at night. My father wanted to sell; the shed roof needed work and the price of dry fodder had gone up twice. My mother did not, and her reasons were about things you cannot put a rate on. Both of them, at some point, turned around and asked me for the calendar. I want to be clear that I did not save anybody. The numbers did not settle it. They just made the argument about the same thing for the first time.

And they showed me my own hole. Three years of milk, in pencil, and not one line about what the milk cost. I had counted the thing that was easy to count and pleasant to see going up. That is the part that stays with me, because there was no teacher standing there to say, and the expenses? Nobody marks your work when you teach yourself. You can be careful and consistent for a thousand days and still be measuring the wrong half of the problem, and you find out only when somebody needs the answer.

The calendar has four columns now. Milk in the morning, milk in the evening, what went out that day, and a small box at the end where I write anything unusual, like the day Lali would not stand up. We still have both cows. The decision gets re-opened every few months and I no longer think that is a failure; it is just what a thing with no clean answer looks like. What I taught myself was not bookkeeping, really. It was that being honest twice a day, in pencil, about something small, is a slow way of earning the right to have an opinion about something large. I still do it. It takes eleven seconds.
