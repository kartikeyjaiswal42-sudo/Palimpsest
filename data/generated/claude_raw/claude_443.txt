My mom enrolled at the community college the same week I started tenth grade. Same registration deadline, weirdly. She'd wanted to finish her degree since before I was born, something in early childhood education, and she'd stopped halfway through because of me, technically, though she never once said it that way and would probably be annoyed I just did.

Here's the disagreement, and it's still not resolved, not really. When she got into the transfer program at the state school two years later, she picked the one that was fifty minutes away instead of the one fifteen minutes from our house. The close one had the same major. It just didn't have the professor she wanted, some woman she'd emailed a dozen times who studies how kids learn language, and my mom decided that mattered more than the extra hour and a half of driving every day, more than being home when I got back from practice, more than basically everything our schedule had run on for two years.

I was furious about it in a way I still feel a little embarrassed by. Not because the drive itself changed much — I could make my own dinner, I was sixteen, it's not like I was five — but because it felt like she was choosing the harder, less convenient version of her own life on purpose, right when things had finally settled into something that worked for both of us. I told her that. I actually said the words "you're choosing this professor over us," which is a genuinely unfair thing to say to your mother and I knew it was unfair even while I was saying it, and I said it anyway.

She didn't yell back, which almost made it worse. She just said she'd spent seventeen years choosing the convenient option, every time, without exception, and that she loved us and didn't regret it, but that this one time, just this one, she wanted to pick the thing that was actually right for what she was trying to become instead of the thing that was easiest for the rest of us to live around. Then she went to bed. We didn't talk about it again for something like a week, which in our house was a long time — we're not people who let things sit, usually.

I still think I was a little bit right. The drive really was hard on her, some weeks she'd get home at eight thirty and just fall asleep on the couch still in her coat, and I felt guilty every single time I saw that, like I'd somehow won an argument I didn't actually want to win. But I also think — and this took me a long time to admit, longer than I'd like — that she was more right than I was. She'd spent two decades organizing her decisions around what worked for everyone else. Watching her make one decision, just one, around what worked for her, and having her own kid call it selfish? That probably stung more than she let on.

We never actually settled it. She graduated last spring, walked at the ceremony in a cap that didn't fit right, and I cried more than I expected to, sitting there next to my dad, watching her cross that stage at forty-two. I never told her I was sorry for what I said sophomore year. She never told me I was wrong to say it. It just sort of got absorbed into everything else that happened over those two years, the way arguments do sometimes in families, not resolved so much as outgrown, sitting there quietly under all the ordinary stuff that came after.

I think about it more than she probably knows. Not with guilt exactly — closer to just noticing it, the way you notice an old bruise is still a little tender if you press it the right way.
