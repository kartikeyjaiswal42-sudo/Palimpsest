I taught myself Portuguese because of a boy in Belo Horizonte who beat me in a chess app in 2022 and then sent a message afterwards that just said "good rook." His name is Léo. He was fifteen then and his English was about the level of that message, and mine was the only English he had ever used with a person instead of a textbook.

Nobody taught me the language. There was no class at my school and no money for an app subscription, so I did it the way you do things when the only resource is time. I downloaded a frequency list of the two thousand commonest Portuguese words and made cards for them, three hundred at a time. I watched a Brazilian cooking channel with the subtitles off, then with them on, then off again. I read the same six pages of a Clarice Lispector story roughly forty times over one winter, not because I understood it but because I had decided that if I could get through those six pages I would be able to get through anything. I still cannot really get through them.

What learning it without a teacher showed me is not the thing I expected, which was something about discipline. I did have to be disciplined. But the real lesson is about feedback, and it is uncomfortable, and it took the form of a mistake I made with Léo that I still think about.

By the second year I could hold a conversation. And what I started doing, without ever deciding to, was correcting him. He would write to me in English and I would answer in Portuguese, and then I would add a line at the bottom fixing his prepositions. I thought this was generous. I thought we were trading. I was, in my head, a very good friend who was helping. In April of that year he stopped replying for eleven weeks.

I had a whole theory about why. He had gotten bored of me, my Portuguese had gotten good enough that I was no longer interesting, he had real friends. I constructed all of that from nothing and I mostly stopped messaging, out of pride.

In July he came back and told me that his father had lost the shop in March and that they had moved to his aunt's in Contagem and that he had been working afternoons. It was in the first paragraph. He was not being dramatic about it. And then he said something that I have not gotten over, which was that he had wanted to tell me in March but that writing to me had started to feel like homework.

I had spent two years teaching myself his language and I had learned nothing about how to be talked to. Every correction I sent was a small signal that the point of the exchange was performance. I had made a friend into a practice partner and I had done it while congratulating myself.

What I did after that is the only part I am proud of. I stopped correcting anything, permanently. And I changed how I study, which sounds unrelated but isn't. When you have no teacher, the temptation is to become your own teacher — to grade everything, including other people. What I actually needed was to become my own student, which means being wrong out loud, at length, without a red pen anywhere in the room. My Portuguese got noticeably worse for about three months after I stopped optimizing and started just talking. Then it got much better, and it got better in a different direction: I can now argue in it, and be unclear in it, and say something badly and try again.

Léo and I have never met. We talk maybe twice a week. He is doing a technical course in electronics and he sends me photographs of circuit boards with captions I do not always understand. I do not fix his English. I have never told him about the eleven weeks and what I assumed during them, and I think about telling him, and I think what would actually be the point, and then I do not.
