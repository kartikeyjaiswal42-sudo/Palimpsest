My mother decided to become a citizen when I was fourteen. She had lived in the country for fifteen years, worked, raised a family, paid taxes, but she had never formally become a citizen. One day she decided it was time. She bought a study book and asked me to help her prepare for the test.

I thought I would be good at this. I'm a good student. I understand how to study and how to teach. What I didn't understand is that my mother's relationship to this test was completely different from my relationship to academic tests. This wasn't something she was doing to get a grade or to satisfy requirements. This was something she was doing to claim full belonging to a country she had already sacrificed a lot for.

I approached it like an academic problem. I made flashcards. I organized the material into categories. I drilled her on facts about the Constitution and the history of the country and the structure of government. She studied. She got most of it right when I tested her. I was confident she would pass.

But when she took the real test, she failed. Not on the academic part—she got the questions about government right. She failed on the spoken English portion. The evaluator said her pronunciation was too difficult to understand. Her grammar wasn't perfect. Her accent was too thick.

I felt like I had failed her. If I had been a better teacher, if I had coached her on her accent, if I had practiced the speaking portion with her more, maybe she would have passed. I had treated the test like it was about knowledge and it turned out to be about something else too. It was about sounding American enough.

What I got wrong is I didn't acknowledge that this test wasn't neutral. It wasn't just measuring if she understood the material. It was measuring if she could communicate in a way that felt comfortable to the evaluator. My mother had done the work. She had learned the material. But she had an accent that the test couldn't accommodate.

She studied again and took it a second time. Same result. I remember her crying after that test, not about the material but about the fact that her way of speaking was being used to measure whether she deserved to be a citizen. She had been here fifteen years. She had built a life here. Her way of speaking shouldn't be the barrier.

She took a third time. I coached her differently. Not on the material—she knew that. I coached her on pacing, on enunciation, on how to make herself sound like what the test expected. It felt wrong to teach her to change herself, but I also knew she deserved this status. The test was going to stay biased so we had to work around it.

She passed on the third attempt. She's a citizen now. She went through the citizenship ceremony and got her certificate. I was proud of her in a way that goes beyond normal pride. I was proud that she didn't give up even when the test was measuring something unfair.

But what I still think about is how I initially approached this as purely academic when it was actually personal and political. How I treated her preparation as if knowledge was enough when the barriers were bigger than knowledge. This taught me something about how I use my education—that sometimes using it to help someone means understanding what the real barrier is, not just the official one. That's something I carry into everything I do now.
