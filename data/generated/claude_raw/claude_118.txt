My parents opened the restaurant when my older brother was two years old. I have a photograph from the opening week: my dad standing in front of a small storefront with a handwritten sign, my mum in the kitchen wearing an apron that says someone's name I don't recognize. They looked exhausted and proud in equal measure.

By the time I was born, the restaurant was established enough that I essentially grew up in it. My earliest memories are of being underneath the tables during lunch rush, playing with pots in the kitchen while my mum cooked, falling asleep in a corner booth while my parents closed up for the night. The restaurant was what my family did.

Twenty-two years after my parents opened it, my father decided to close it.

The decision didn't come suddenly, though my brother tried to argue that it had. My father had been quieter for the past year, staying up later looking at the accounting books, talking to my mother in low voices about rent and staffing costs and how much the delivery platforms were taking. The restaurant was still doing okay, still had regular customers, still had a purpose in the community. But it wasn't thriving the way it once had, and my father was tired in a way that didn't improve even on his days off.

My brother was furious. He'd started talking about taking over the restaurant eventually, about eventually leaving his job in finance to come back and run it. He thought my father was giving up. He thought they should fight harder to keep it alive. I remember him saying, "Dad's just burned out. If he took a month off, he'd feel differently about this."

I think my brother was wrong, and I've been angry at him for not seeing it, which is probably why I'm writing about this instead of just moving on.

The thing my brother didn't understand was that for my father, the decision to close wasn't a failure—it was a release. He'd given the restaurant twenty-two years. He'd been there at four in the morning to start prep. He'd listened to customers' problems and their stories and their complaints. He'd watched food trends change and rent increase and his own back and knees start to give out from standing all day. The restaurant had given my family stability and purpose and a living, and then it had also started taking more than it was giving back.

My brother wanted my father to keep sacrificing himself for something that was also sacrificing him.

That's where we disagreed, and that's where we still disagree. My brother thinks I'm being disloyal by understanding our father's decision. He thinks I should have fought with him to keep the restaurant open, like that somehow proves I care about the family legacy or understand the value of what our parents built. But I think understanding that my father deserved to be tired, that he'd given enough, that sometimes the most important thing you can do is choose yourself—I think that proves I understand what family actually means.

The closing happened over several weeks. My father reduced hours gradually, stopped taking on new catering projects, started saying goodbye to regular customers. We held a final service on a Saturday, and it was packed with people who'd eaten at the restaurant over the years. People brought their kids and their grandkids. Someone brought a cake. The whole thing was sad and celebratory at the same time.

My father cried afterward, and I held his hand.

I haven't resolved this with my brother. He's still sort of angry about the whole thing, still mentions sometimes how the restaurant could have been saved if my father had just held on longer. But I've made peace with it, or I'm making peace with it. I've realized that loyalty to your family doesn't mean asking them to keep destroying themselves for something they built. Sometimes it means letting them rest.
