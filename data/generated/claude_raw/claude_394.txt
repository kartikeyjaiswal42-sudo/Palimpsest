I spent four years at St. Augustine's, which was supposed to be building my character and my faith in equal measure. Every morning, I woke up at six to make it to school by 7:15 for the morning service. Every evening, we said grace before dinner. Every Sunday, my family went to church. This was the rhythm of my childhood, from age eight to age twelve, the routine so consistent that I never really thought to question it.

The questioning started small. A friend at St. Augustine's stopped going to the optional Friday night youth group, and when I asked her why, she said she just didn't believe it anymore. Just like that. She didn't believe. I was shocked, partly that she could say it so casually and partly that no one seemed to punish her for it. She still came to school. She still participated in everything else. She just wasn't at youth group on Friday nights.

After that, I couldn't stop questioning. Not in an angry way at first, just noticing things that seemed odd. The contradiction between what we were taught about charity and the way the school excluded students whose families couldn't afford tuition. The way the religion class was taught as doctrine rather than theology, as if there was one right answer rather than centuries of different interpretations. The way doubt was framed as a sin rather than as a natural part of thinking about anything important.

Every morning, I would sit in the chapel and listen to the readings. It became this ritual of internal debate. I'd hear something that made sense, and I'd also hear something that made me uncomfortable. The cognitive dissonance started to feel like the most honest thing in my life. I wasn't pretending to believe. I was genuinely sitting with the contradiction, morning after morning, trying to work it out.

By the end of ninth grade, I'd decided I didn't believe anymore. Not dramatically, not in a moment of rebellion, but gradually, like a door closing quietly. I kept going through the motions. I still attended the services, still said grace, still wore the uniform and followed the rules. But I was going through it as a practice rather than as an expression of faith.

My parents knew something was wrong before I told them. I think they could see it in how carefully I was performing, how deliberate every action had become. When I finally said I wanted to leave St. Augustine's, my mother was disappointed but not surprised. My father took longer to come around.

Leaving the school was harder than I expected. It wasn't just leaving the academic environment. It was leaving the identity I'd had since I was eight years old. I was a St. Augustine's kid. That was part of how I understood myself. Leaving meant rebuilding that entire sense of who I was supposed to be.

The interesting thing is what stayed with me from those four years. Not the doctrine or the specific teachings, though I respect them more now than I did when I was sixteen and angry about contradiction. What stayed was the habit of spending time thinking about big questions. The morning services, which I resented so much, had given me a practice of sitting quietly with complicated ideas. I didn't know how valuable that was until it was gone.

At my new school, public and secular and utterly different, I missed the chapel sometimes. Not because I missed God or the faith, but because I missed the built-in time to think. Everyone at my new school seemed to be rushing from one thing to the next without ever stopping to wonder about the foundational questions of how to live.

I'm grateful for St. Augustine's now, which surprised me. I'm grateful that I was given the structure and the language to question faith in the first place. I'm grateful that they didn't punish me for my doubt. And I'm grateful that it forced me to actively decide what I believe rather than just inheriting it.

My parents and I still don't talk about it very much. My mother remains quietly disappointed. My father has made peace with it. And I've learned that faith, whether religious or otherwise, requires active belief. It can't just be something you do because you've always done it. At some point, you have to choose it or let it go. I let it go, but I kept the part that mattered—the habit of sitting with hard questions and not rushing past them.
