The scorebook is a Sportcraft, spiral-bound, red cover gone pink at the corners from twelve years of my father's thumb dragging across it before every over. I still have it. It lives in the bottom drawer of my desk under things I don't look at often, and every so often I take it out and open it to the page from the district semifinal, the one with the smudge, and I think about the only real fight my father and I ever had about anything that mattered.

I started keeping score for his club matches when I was nine, mostly because nobody else wanted the job and I liked having a job. He taught me the notation himself, sitting on an upturned bucket beside the boundary rope: dot for no run, the little triangle for a boundary, W with the circle for how a man got out. By the time I was thirteen I could keep a full scorebook faster than the official scorer the league sent, and my father, who captained the team and took the game more seriously than a grown man with a day job probably should, trusted my book over anyone's.

The semifinal was the exception. He was given out, caught behind, off a ball that everyone on our side of the boundary saw brush nothing but his pad. I saw it too. I was twelve feet away with the clearest angle in the ground. The umpire raised his finger anyway, and my father walked off without a word, which was its own kind of protest, because normally he'd have said something.

After the match he came and found me at the scorer's table and asked to see the book. He wanted me to change the dismissal. Not the runs, not the result — just the note beside his name, from "c Behind b Rathi" to something softer, "given," with a question mark, so that when people read the book years later they'd know it was disputed. He said the book was the only record that would outlast anyone's memory of the day, and he didn't want his name attached to an outcome he didn't believe in.

I told him no. I remember exactly how it felt to say it, my stomach folding in on itself, because I had never told my father no about anything that wasn't chores. I said the book records what the umpire decided, not what actually happened, that's the whole point of it, that's what makes it a real record instead of a story we tell ourselves. If I start changing entries because a player disagrees, the book stops meaning anything.

He didn't yell. He's not a yeller. He just looked at me for a second too long, said "fair enough," and walked to the car. We didn't talk about it again that day, or really ever, not directly. But he never asked me to change another entry, not once in the five more years I kept his books, even when I know he wanted to.

I still don't know if I was right. Some nights I think I was twelve years old lecturing a grown man about integrity I hadn't earned the right to lecture anyone about. Other nights I think it's the only completely honest thing I've ever told him. What I know is that the smudge on that page isn't ink — it's where he pressed his thumb into the wet ballpoint while he was still deciding whether to ask me again, and stopped himself, and I've never asked him what he was thinking in that second, because some part of me is afraid of an answer that would prove I should have just changed the entry and let him have his one soft place in an unforgiving book.
