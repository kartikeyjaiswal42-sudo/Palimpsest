The object is a phone case, one specific one, deep purple with a cracked-marble pattern that peels a little at the corner, sitting in the shoebox where I keep the sixty-three unsold ones I couldn't return. I started a business selling custom phone cases the summer before eleventh grade with four hundred dollars saved from two years of babysitting, a heat press I bought secondhand off a woman moving out of state, and a business plan that consisted mostly of the sentence "everyone needs a phone case."

I designed twelve patterns, ordered blanks in bulk, and set up a table at the Saturday farmers market for six weeks straight, certain that a fifteen-dollar case with a marble swirl or a pressed-flower look would sell itself to anyone under twenty-five walking by. I sold eleven cases total that summer. I still have sixty-three of them, including the purple one, which I kept specifically because it was the very first one I ever heat-pressed, warped slightly at one edge because I left it under the press four seconds too long and didn't yet know that four seconds was the difference between a good case and a slightly ruined one.

The business failed for reasons that seem obvious now and were completely invisible to me at the time. I never asked anyone at that market what they'd actually pay for a case, I just decided fifteen dollars sounded fair. I didn't research what phone models people around here actually carried, so half my inventory fit a phone that had already been discontinued by the time I finished my first batch. I spent more on a professional-looking table banner than I ever made back in sales. I was, in short, a seventeen-year-old with a heat press and a lot of confidence and almost no actual information about the market I was trying to sell into.

What I took from those sixty-three unsold cases wasn't a lesson about phone cases specifically. It was the discovery that I genuinely like the part of running something that most people find tedious — the spreadsheet of costs, the awkward conversation with a stranger at a table who says no, the redesign after a bad batch, the slow unglamorous math of figuring out where the money actually went. Most of my friends who've started small projects quit after the first flop. I found the flop interesting enough to keep a case from it on my desk.

That's the thing I think I'd bring to a campus that wouldn't otherwise be there: not a polished success story, but someone who's already sat with a failed product and found the failure more useful than embarrassing. I'd want to be part of any group on campus building something from nothing — a student-run venture, a pop-up market, a maker space nobody's fully figured out yet — because I've already learned, at a folding table on a Saturday morning, that the learning happens in the version that doesn't sell, not the version that does. I still have the heat press. I've since sold about forty more cases, slowly, to people who actually asked for a specific design first.
