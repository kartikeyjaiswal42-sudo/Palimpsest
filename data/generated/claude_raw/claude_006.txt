The register sits at the counter of my family's convenience store like it has for twenty years. It's not one of those modern touchscreen systems that looks sleek and efficient. It's mechanical, heavy, with worn plastic buttons and a metal drawer that slides out with a satisfying click. The number pad is rubbed smooth from the thousands of fingers that have punched in prices and codes and quantities. Some of the numbers are barely legible anymore.

I've been working behind that register for four years, since I was thirteen. On Saturdays and after school, I ring up chips and milk and lottery tickets and energy drinks for people I've started to recognize by face. Mrs. Chen comes in Wednesday mornings for her coffee. The teenager from down the street always buys the same gum, same time. The man with the dog stops by most evenings.

This register has taught me something unexpected about the ordinary. When you stand behind a counter and interact with hundreds of people over thousands of transactions, you start to see the small stories embedded in what they buy. A father buying formula and diapers and looking exhausted. A girl buying supplies for a craft project, her hands already paint-stained. An elderly woman who always chats for five minutes and seems to have nowhere else to be. A teenager buying ice cream after, presumably, a bad day.

I used to think working this register was just a way to make money. Something temporary, something I'd move past quickly. My parents owned the store, and helping out was just what you did in a family business. But standing at this register, I've watched how it sits at the center of a lot of people's days. Someone's morning routine. Someone's emergency stop. The place where a neighbor is briefly visible as a whole person, not just a voice or a face passing on the street.

The register is old and imperfect. It gets stuck sometimes. The receipt printer jams and I have to coax it. The drawer occasionally doesn't close smoothly. Nothing about it is elegant or efficient in the way modern things are supposed to be. But every day it works. Every single transaction, it rings up, the drawer pops open, the receipt prints out. It's reliable in a way that feels important.

Working this register has made me think about value differently. The convenience store isn't glamorous. The job isn't impressive in the way that summer internships or college-application-looking volunteer work is supposed to be. But it's real work that helps real people, and the small relationships that form at this counter have mattered to me more than I expected.

My father still works this register too. He knows the regulars' names, their orders, something about their lives. He's shown me that there's dignity in this work, that knowing your community and serving it well is a form of contribution that's worth doing. That a job you show up for, day after day, quietly and reliably, matters even if no one external is keeping score.

This worn-out register has been a teacher in its own way. It's taught me that the ordinary things, done consistently with attention and care, add up to something meaningful. That showing up for people matters. That even a simple transaction can contain a moment of genuine human connection.
