The belief I challenged was that old things should be made to work again, and I was wrong to challenge it in the way I did, and I have thought about it for two years.

My uncle Pran died in the spring of my sophomore year and left, among the things nobody wanted, a Philips valve radio from 1961 in a wooden cabinet the colour of weak tea. It had been in his flat in Kanpur since before my mother was born. When they cleared the place my mother asked if anyone wanted it and there was that particular silence families have, and I said I would take it, mostly because I could not stand the silence.

I decided immediately that I would restore it. I had never restored anything. I had watched perhaps eleven hours of videos of men in England replacing capacitors, and from this I had assembled a conviction that the correct thing to do with a broken object of this kind is to make it play again. That was the belief. I did not think of it as a belief. I thought of it as an obvious fact, the way you think of gravity.

So I got a soldering iron and I opened the back of it.

Inside there were forty years of dust and a smell like hot dust and old paper, and a folded piece of paper wedged behind the speaker that turned out to be a repair slip from a shop in Kanpur dated 1974, with my uncle's name on it in a hand I did not recognise, and an amount. Sixteen rupees. There were also pencil marks on the inside of the cabinet, tick marks, maybe thirty of them, which I have never been able to explain and which no one alive can explain either.

I removed everything that was original except the transformer and the case.

That is the part I got wrong. I replaced every capacitor, which needed doing. I also replaced the mains cord, and the knobs, because two were chipped, and the speaker cloth, because it had a tear, and I sanded the cabinet down and refinished it, and I threw away the chipped knobs and the torn cloth and the old cord, and somewhere in that process the repair slip went into a bin bag with the sanding dust and I did not notice for three days.

It works now. It sounds very good, actually. It picks up two AM stations and hisses beautifully between them.

And it is not his radio. It is a radio, of the same model, sitting in his cabinet. The chipped knob was chipped by somebody's hand. The tear in the cloth had a cause. Every mark I sanded off was information, and I destroyed all of it in about eleven days because I had never questioned the assumption that restoring means returning to new.

What I understand now, and did not then, is that there are two different things people mean by fixing. One is making a thing perform its function. The other is keeping a thing that carries a life. They are often opposed and nobody tells you that, because the videos are all made by people fixing objects that were never anyone's.

The outcome is that I have become annoying about this. When my mother wanted to repaint the doorframe with our heights on it I made a real argument out of it and she thought I was being ridiculous, and maybe I was. The doorframe is still there, unpainted, with a smudge at 132 centimetres that is me at nine.

I keep the radio on in my room in the evenings. I like it. That is the uncomfortable part — I like it, and I know exactly what it cost, and both of those stay true.
