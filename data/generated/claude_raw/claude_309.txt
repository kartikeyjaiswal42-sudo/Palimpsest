Every Sunday for the past two years, I've read out loud to strangers for an hour, and for a long time I thought I was the one doing the giving.

I started at Hollis House, the hospice near my grandmother's old apartment, because a volunteer coordinator visited our school looking for readers and I signed up mostly to get out of a Sunday-morning family obligation I didn't want to explain to anyone. I expected it to be sad in a way I could brace for once and then get through. It was sad, some days. It was also, most days, much stranger and more ordinary than I'd braced for.

The first patient I read to regularly was a man named Walt who wanted nothing but westerns, the pulpy kind with a gunslinger on the cover, and who fell asleep about fifteen minutes into most sessions, and who I kept reading to anyway because the nurse told me his breathing changed when the room went quiet, so the sound mattered more than whether he was following the plot. I read him probably nine or ten of these books over four months. I have never once discussed a Louis L'Amour novel with anyone in my actual life, and I could now, with some confidence, hold a conversation about frontier justice tropes that nobody in my world wants to have.

The challenge, if I'm being honest about what the challenge actually was, wasn't the sadness. It was showing up on the Sundays I didn't want to. There were mornings I sat in the car in the parking lot for five minutes before going in, mornings after a bad week at school where the last thing I wanted to do was walk into a building full of people near the end of their lives and perform cheerfulness I didn't feel. I almost quit twice. Once in the fall of sophomore year, when a patient I'd been reading to for three months died on a Tuesday and nobody told me until I showed up the following Sunday expecting her, book in hand, and found her room already occupied by someone else. I sat in the parking lot that day for closer to twenty minutes.

I went back the next week anyway. I don't have a clean explanation for why, except that the habit itself had become load-bearing in some way I couldn't fully explain to my parents when they asked if I wanted to stop. It wasn't that I'd gotten used to loss. I don't think you get used to it. It was that showing up had turned into the thing I did regardless of whether I felt like it, the same way you show up to practice on a day you're tired, and somewhere in there the habit started teaching me something the individual Sundays couldn't have taught me on their own — that consistency isn't the same as enthusiasm, and you can build the first without waiting around for the second.

I still read on Sundays. Different patients now, mostly, since two years is a long time in that building. I still don't love every visit. Some weeks I still sit in the parking lot a minute longer than I need to before going in. But I go in. That's the entire lesson, as far as I can tell, distilled down to something almost embarrassingly simple after two years of showing up: the value was never really in any single Sunday. It was in not letting a bad week decide whether the next one happened.
