For most of the fall I was convinced that our United States history textbook was lying, and that Mr. Ferraro was defending it because he was tired.

The chapter on the Homestead Strike ran two paragraphs. It said there was a labor dispute at a steel plant, that violence broke out, and that the strike failed. It did not say who fired first, or that the Pinkertons came up the river on barges at four in the morning, or that seven workers died. I had read about it because my great-grandfather worked at Homestead's successor mill, and my mother tells the story at Thanksgiving with her hands. So I raised it in class, and when Mr. Ferraro said we needed to move on to the tariff, I said the book was propaganda. I said it in a voice I regret.

He kept me after. I expected a lecture about respect. Instead he pulled the teacher's edition off the shelf, opened to the copyright page, and showed me the adoption stamp: our district had bought this edition in 2009 because it was the cheapest of the three the state approved. Then he showed me the state framework, a spiral-bound thing listing eleven required topics for the semester, of which Homestead was not one. Then he showed me his own binder, tabbed, full of primary sources he had photocopied over twenty-two years, including a page of the Pinkerton testimony.

I had walked in thinking the argument was between a truthful account and a false one. What I saw on his desk was a different argument entirely: between a hundred and eighty days and four hundred years. Every page the book gave to Homestead was a page it took from somewhere else. He had not chosen to omit my great-grandfather. He had inherited a container and was trying to pack it honestly.

That was the moment I stopped arguing and started asking questions, which is a less satisfying thing to do and much more useful. I asked how he decided what to cut. He said he asks which omission a student is most likely to correct on their own later. Homestead, he said, has a museum forty minutes away.

We did the primary sources in a lunchtime group he let me organize, six of us, then nine. The textbook still says two paragraphs. I still think that is wrong. But I no longer think wrongness and dishonesty are the same thing, and I no longer assume the person defending a limit is the one who set it.
