I have a spreadsheet on my laptop with fourteen tabs. Cost of goods sold, labor as a percentage of revenue, rent-to-sales ratios for restaurants in three different zip codes, a whole tab just for how delivery apps take their cut before the money ever reaches the register. Nobody made me build this. I built it at midnight, most nights, for almost a year, and I could not tell you exactly why except that it feels like the only way I know how to keep asking a question that doesn't have a good answer anymore.

The restaurant was called Marchetti's. My great-uncle opened it, my grandfather ran it after him, and my dad took it over when I was seven. Twenty-two years, one location, a kitchen that still had my grandfather's handwriting taped above the stove telling whoever was cooking not to rush the sauce. It closed in March. I was in the dining room the last night, folding chairs, and I did not cry, which surprised me, because I'd been crying about smaller things for weeks before that.

Here is the part I keep circling back to on the spreadsheet at midnight. Two years before it closed, my dad wanted to sign up with the delivery apps. Everyone else on our street already had. I told him not to. I was fifteen and I had strong opinions about things I did not fully understand, and I remember standing in the kitchen telling him that the apps take almost a third of every order, that it would make us look like everyone else, that people came to Marchetti's because it felt like it belonged to somebody, not like it belonged to an algorithm. He listened to me. He usually did, which I understand now was its own kind of mistake on his part.

We held out for another year while three other places on the block signed up and started showing up in searches I didn't know people were doing. By the time my dad finally did add us, the accounts you need to build up, the reviews, the ranking inside the app itself, all of it takes months to earn, and we didn't have months left to spend earning it. I don't actually know if the apps would have saved us. That's the thing about the spreadsheet. I have run the numbers eleven different ways and I still don't know, and there is a version of this essay where I tell you I found the answer and it fixed something in me, but that isn't true.

What is true is that I spend hours now reading about unit economics for restaurants that have nothing to do with mine, comparing margins, reading forum posts from line cooks about why their places failed, watching hour-long videos from people who ran diners in Ohio, because somewhere in all of that I think I'm looking for the version of the story where fifteen-year-old me wasn't wrong. Or where being wrong wouldn't have mattered anyway. I lose entire nights to it. My mom will knock on my door at one in the morning and ask what I'm even looking at, and I never have a clean answer.

I don't turn to any one person for this, honestly, which feels like it should embarrass me more than it does. I turn to strangers on the internet who ran small businesses and are willing to talk about exactly how they failed, because nobody in my actual life wants to talk about the specifics of ours. My dad especially doesn't. So I go looking on my own, tab after tab, trying to build something out of numbers that might explain a year I can't undo, and mostly what I've learned is that I'm still not done asking.
