There is a chunk of two-by-four zip-tied to the clutch pedal of my grandfather's 1988 Ford, worn smooth on one corner from three decades of boot heels, and for a long time I thought he'd cut it for me the summer I turned twelve. It turns out he'd cut it in 1994, for my mother, and just never taken it off.

I found this out by accident, the way you find out most of the important things about your family. I was complaining, mildly, that the block wobbled a little under my foot, and my grandfather said something like "your mother used to say the same thing" without looking up from the fence post he was setting. I asked when. He said the summer she was twelve, same field, same truck, same block, and that he'd meant to replace it with something sturdier for twenty-something years and never gotten around to it. I didn't think much of it at the time. I was more focused on not stalling out on the incline by the barn.

I think about it constantly now. My grandfather taught me to drive that summer in a hay field with no traffic and no consequences beyond a startled cow, shifting me through first and second gear on that same worn block while explaining, patiently, for the fortieth time, why you ease off the clutch instead of dropping it. He'd taught my mother the same way, on the same pedal, and somewhere in the two decades between her lessons and mine, he'd kept that scrap of wood in the truck through every oil change and every winter it sat under a tarp, not because he needed it for anything, but because there was a chance, someday, that another kid would need to reach a pedal they were too small for.

That's the part I find myself grateful for in a way I didn't expect to be. It would have been easy for him to just cut a new block when I came along, and I never would have known the difference. Instead I got to learn to drive on the exact same piece of wood my mother's foot had worn down first, which means some part of her twelve-year-old self was still sitting in that truck with me, and my grandfather had apparently been quietly betting on that for twenty years without telling anyone.

I don't think he meant it as a gesture. I think he's just the kind of person who doesn't throw things away that still work, and it happened, by accident or by faith, to turn into one of the more moving things anyone has done for me. It's changed the way I think about small, unglamorous acts of keeping things — not grand gifts, just a man refusing to get rid of a scrap of wood for two decades because some part of him assumed there'd be a reason to need it again. I've started doing it myself, saving things I can't justify keeping, on the same quiet bet that someone, eventually, will need exactly that thing, worn down in exactly that shape.
