There is a specific kind of quiet that happens when you finally get a derailleur indexed correctly after twenty minutes of small, stubborn adjustments, the chain finally moving cleanly across all seven gears instead of grinding or skipping, and I have chased that quiet more afternoons than I can count in our garage, which by now has more bike tools in it than car tools, something my father brings up more than he probably realizes.

It started three summers ago with my own bike, a hand-me-down with a bent derailleur hanger I had no idea how to fix, and a YouTube channel run by a guy in Ohio who talks entirely too slowly and explains everything twice, which turned out to be exactly what I needed. I watched maybe forty of his videos before I trusted myself to touch anything, and then I fixed my own bike, and then, almost without deciding to, I fixed my neighbor Théo's bike, whose chain kept dropping every time he stood up to pedal. Word got around a ten-year-old's block faster than word gets around anywhere else. By the next summer I had a rotation, eleven or twelve kids on my street and the next one over, bringing me flat tires and seized brakes and one memorably mangled front wheel that I trued back into something rideable over three separate evenings.

I lose real time to this. Not the vague kind of losing track people mean when they say it about a good book. I mean I have started a wheel truing at four in the afternoon and looked up to find it's past seven, my mother calling me in for dinner twice already, my hands black with chain grease I'll be finding in the creases of my knuckles for two more days. When something in a bike isn't working, there's always a specific, findable reason, a barrel adjuster a quarter turn off, a spoke tension uneven on one side of the rim, and chasing that reason feels like the cleanest kind of thinking I get to do all week.

My father doesn't understand why I do all of it for free. He's not wrong that I could charge, that eleven-year-olds' parents would probably pay fifteen dollars a repair without blinking, that I've put maybe four hundred dollars of my own babysitting money into tools and tubes and brake pads over three years with nothing coming back the other way. He brings this up periodically, not angrily, just genuinely puzzled, the way you'd be puzzled watching someone give away something that's clearly worth money. I've tried to explain that charging would change what it is, that a kid handing me his broken bike and trusting me to fix it before soccer practice is a different transaction than a customer handing me cash, and that I'm not sure the second version would still make me lose track of time the way the first one does. He nods when I say this. He still doesn't agree, and I still don't think he's going to.

We've had some version of this conversation probably six times now, always a little different, always ending in the same unresolved place, both of us sure we're right. I don't think either of us is going to convince the other, and for reasons I can't fully explain, I've stopped trying to end it in a win. Some arguments you just keep having with the people you love, in between fixing derailleurs, because the disagreement itself turns out to be part of how you know each other.
