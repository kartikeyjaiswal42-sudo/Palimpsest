My mom went back to university when I was seven, and I spent the next decade convinced this was the worst thing she had ever done to me. She was forty years old. I remember thinking it was selfish. I was seven. My opinion on selfishness was not sophisticated.

She was in classes from eight until three. Then she came home and made dinner and did homework with me and did everything else she'd always done, but there was something different. She was tired in a way I blamed her education for. No vacations because of exams. Pasta for dinner because she was studying. The house was messier. She was less available.

I was furious about this on her behalf, which is what kids do when they see their parents sacrifice things. They interpret it as personal betrayal.

What I got wrong was that I thought going to university was something she did instead of being a mom. I didn't understand she was doing both. That choosing yourself doesn't mean abandoning your kid. I spent years resenting the pasta dinners and the weekends she spent studying instead of playing with me. I thought she was being selfish. I thought a good mom would just stay home.

My mom graduated last year. She's working as a consultant in environmental policy now. More importantly, she's different. She talks about her work with a confidence she never had before. Not confidence as a mom, but confidence as herself. Having something that was just hers mattered to her. It changed her.

The thing I still think about is all those years I spent being resentful. All the times I thought she was doing something wrong. All the ways I punished her in my head for not being available in exactly the way I wanted.

But now I understand what she was actually teaching me. That you can want multiple things at once. That being a mom and being a person with your own goals aren't mutually exclusive. That sometimes loving your kid means showing them you have your own life. That you matter beyond your role as a parent.

I know I'll face this myself someday. If I have a daughter, I'll want to do what my mom did. I'll want to keep growing, keep building something. And because of what my mom did, I know I can do both. I have a model of what it looks like to be a woman who doesn't stop at motherhood, who isn't satisfied with just doing the expected thing.

That's worth all the pasta dinners. I just wish I'd understood it when I was seven instead of taking until sixteen.
