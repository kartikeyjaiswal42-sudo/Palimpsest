The summer before eleventh grade I took a job picking peaches at an orchard forty minutes outside town because my cousin Elena worked there and said it paid better than the movie theater. I lasted six weeks and quit twice, and it's the second time that actually taught me something.

Picking fruit sounds simple until you've done ten hours of it. You're up a ladder in ninety-degree heat, twisting each peach a specific way so it comes off clean without bruising, and if you bruise too many the crew lead docks your bucket count. My hands cramped into claws by lunch. My back ached in a way I didn't know backs could ache at sixteen. The pay, once I did the math on hours versus buckets, came out barely above what I would have made folding shirts at the mall.

By the fourth week I had decided I was quitting for good. I told my mother at dinner, laid out the whole case — the pay, the heat, the exhaustion. She listened and didn't argue, which somehow made me feel worse than if she had. She just asked if I'd told Elena yet, since Elena had gotten me the job and would have to explain my absence to the crew lead, a woman named Dolores who did not tolerate flakiness.

I drove out the next morning meaning to give notice. What changed my mind was Dolores herself, not Elena. When I told her I was done, she didn't try to talk me out of it. She just said, "Fine, but finish today's row first, because a half-picked row goes to waste." That felt fair. So I climbed the ladder one more time.

Without the mental exit already scheduled, without rehearsing my resignation speech, I actually paid attention to what I was doing. I got faster. I stopped bruising the fruit. By the end of the row my bucket count was the best I'd had all summer, and Dolores, walking past, said only, "See."

I didn't quit that day. I finished out the season, through the worst heat wave of July, because I'd learned something in that one row no lecture from my mother could have taught me: that half of what feels unbearable about a hard job is the part where you've already decided to leave it. I still don't love peach picking. But the exhaustion I was running from was partly the exhaustion of not committing to the work in front of me.
