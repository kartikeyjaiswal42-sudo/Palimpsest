The recipe card lived in a metal box on top of the refrigerator, and until I was fourteen I don't think I ever took it down myself. It was my grandmother's, written in pencil that had gone soft and grey with age, the corners furred from forty years of flour and thumbs. Down one side she'd added notes in a different pen, later, corrections to herself: "not too much cumin," underlined twice. That card is the reason I can tell you, almost to the minute, when my grandmother and I first disagreed about something that mattered to both of us.

My mother had a work trip that couldn't be moved, a conference in another state, and rather than leave my father to fend for himself and my two younger brothers, she asked if I would cook dinner. Not help cook. Cook. I chose the card without asking anyone, because it was the dish my grandmother made better than anything else, a lamb and potato stew she called by a name I never learned to spell, and because some part of me wanted to prove I could do the thing she did without seeming to try.

She came over that afternoon anyway, ostensibly to keep my youngest brother occupied, actually, I think, to watch. And when I reached for a can of tomatoes because the recipe called for tomatoes and none were fresh, she stopped my hand. Canned tomatoes have a taste to them, she said, a tinniness that never fully cooks out. She wanted me to walk to the corner store instead. I didn't want to walk to the corner store. I wanted to follow the card exactly as it was written and get dinner on the table before my father got home from picking up my brothers, and I told her so, more sharply than I meant to.

She didn't argue back the way I expected. She just said that the recipe was never really finished, that she was still changing it the year she wrote that card and every year after, and that if I wanted to cook it the way she actually made it, I had to be willing to change it too. Then she sat down at the table and let me decide.

I used the canned tomatoes. I want to be honest about that. I was seventeen and stubborn and running out of time, and I told myself efficiency mattered more than a taste I couldn't even identify yet. The stew came out fine. Everyone ate two bowls. My father said it tasted just like hers, which I understood even then was a kindness more than an accurate report.

But I kept thinking about what she'd said, days after, in a way I didn't expect to. Not about the tomatoes specifically, but about the idea that respecting a recipe, or a person, doesn't mean copying them exactly. She hadn't been angry that I did it my way. She'd been telling me, in the only language she really had for it, that the card in the metal box was never meant to be a set of instructions I followed to earn her approval. It was a record of someone still deciding, dish after dish, what she believed good food required, and she wanted me to start building my own version of that same argument with myself.

I still have that card, actually. My grandmother gave it to me the year she moved into an apartment too small for a box of anything. I've added my own notes to it now, in blue ink next to her pencil, and one of them, right beside the tomatoes, says fresh if you can get them, canned if you can't, and don't feel guilty either way. I don't think she'd mind that I disagreed with her. I think that was always the point.
