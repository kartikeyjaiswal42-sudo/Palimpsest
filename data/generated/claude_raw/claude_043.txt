My dad and I still argue about the motorcycle, and I don't think either of us is going to win, mostly because we're not actually arguing about the motorcycle.

It was my grandfather's, a 1978 Honda CB400 he bought used in the eighties and never quite finished fixing before he died two years ago. It sat in our garage under a tarp for a year after that, and my dad kept saying he was going to sell it to a guy at his work who restored bikes, get it out of the way, stop tripping over it every time he looked for the leaf blower. I asked if I could try fixing it myself instead. He said sure, mostly, I think, because he assumed I'd lose interest by June.

I did not lose interest by June. I spent basically that whole summer out there — carburetor cleaned three times before I got the jetting right, a new battery, a seat I reupholstered badly and then less badly, hours on forums trying to figure out why the electrical system kept grounding out. My hands smelled like gasoline for three months straight. My mom made me take my shoes off before coming inside.

My dad would come check on me sometimes, and at first it was fine, even good — he knew more than he let on, and he'd point out things, show me how to bleed a brake line. But somewhere around July it turned into something else. He started saying things like "you know he never actually got that thing running, right, in like fifteen years," which is true, and which I think he meant as a warning, but which I heard as him telling me to quit. I'd say something back about how that wasn't the point, and he'd say everything's the point of something, Jake, and we'd both go quiet and do something else for a while.

We never really settled what the bike meant to either of us, which I think is the actual disagreement, underneath the one about torque specs and whether I was wasting my summer. I think my dad needed it gone because looking at it half-fixed in the garage was like looking at his father half-here, and finishing it, or worse, making it run, wasn't going to bring anyone back, so what was the point. I think I needed the opposite — I needed to finish something my grandfather started, because I'd barely known him, honestly, he died when I was fourteen and lived two states away most of my life, and the bike was the closest thing I had to actually meeting him.

I got it running in August, the night before I had to go back to school. I rode it around the block twice, badly, stalling once at the stop sign. My dad watched from the driveway with his arms crossed and didn't say much, and I couldn't tell if he was proud or just sad in a way he wasn't going to explain to me. He still brings up selling it sometimes. I still say no. Neither of us has really said the real thing to the other, the thing about grief looking different for two people who loved the same man in different ways, and I don't know if we ever will. But the bike runs now, and some nights he comes out and looks at it with me, quiet, and that's not resolution exactly. It's something close to it.
