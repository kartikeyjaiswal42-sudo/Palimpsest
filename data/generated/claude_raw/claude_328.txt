My brother Daniel is twenty and he fits industrial refrigeration units, mostly in supermarkets, mostly at night because that is when you can shut a chiller down. He is very good at it. He earns more than our mother did at his age and he owns a van. He did not go to university, and he did not go because he decided not to, and I have never been able to leave it alone.

We had the argument properly once, in the summer I turned sixteen, in the car park of a Tesco in Warrington at two in the morning while he waited for a compressor to come down to temperature. I had got a lift with him because I was interested in what he did, which was true, and because I wanted to make my case, which was also true and which he could see coming from about forty miles away.

My argument was the obvious one and I made it badly. That he had got four As. That his physics teacher had cried, actually cried, when he said he was not applying. That the trade was fine now but bodies do not stay fine, and that our uncle has a shoulder that does not go above his head any more. That he was closing a door at eighteen that gets heavier every year you leave it. I had prepared this. I think I even said the phrase "opportunity cost", in a car park, to my brother, at two in the morning.

He listened all the way through, which is worse than interrupting. Then he said: you keep telling me what I am giving up, and you have never once asked me what I am getting.

So I asked. And what he said was that he had spent thirteen years in rooms being told that the point of what he was doing now was what it would let him do later. Thirteen years of later. And that on his second week of the apprenticeship, a woman who ran a corner shop in Runcorn had shaken his hand because her freezers were working and she was not going to lose four thousand pounds of stock, and that nothing at school had ever felt like that, and he was not willing to be told that the feeling was a lesser thing than a degree by people who had never had it.

I did not have an answer. I still do not, exactly. But I also did not change my mind, and that is the part I find hard to write down honestly. I think I am right. I think there is a version of Daniel at thirty-five who wants to do something else and finds that the door has swollen shut, and I think loving someone includes saying so before it happens rather than after. I also know that every time I say it I am telling him that the life he is actually living is a provisional one, a holding position, and that must be an unbearable thing to hear from your younger brother.

So we have arrived at a settlement neither of us likes. I do not raise it. He knows I have not stopped thinking it. Occasionally he sends me a photograph of some enormous piece of pipework at three in the morning with no caption, and I send back a photograph of my chemistry notes, and that is the whole conversation, conducted entirely in exhibits.

I am applying to university this year to read engineering, which he thinks is funny, because half of what made me want it is watching him work. He drove me to an open day in the van. On the way back he asked whether they had said anything about refrigeration cycles and I said yes, actually, quite a lot, and he said good, and neither of us said anything else for a while.
