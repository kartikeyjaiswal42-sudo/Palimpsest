For two summers I was a lifeguard at a pool that almost nobody used. Tuesday and Thursday mornings, half six to half nine, at the leisure centre where the early session had maybe four regulars and some weeks had one. So what I mostly did was sit in a high chair above eight lanes of undisturbed water and watch nothing happen.

Nobody teaches you that part. The qualification teaches you rescues. You spend a weekend hauling a rubber dummy off the bottom and learning how to get someone out with a suspected spinal injury, and you come away thinking the job is the emergency. It isn't. The job is the four hundred hours before the emergency, and there is no course for that, and I had to work it out on my own, badly, over two years.

What I got wrong is simple to state and took me a long time to see.

I thought watching was a thing you did with your eyes. So I kept them open and I kept them pointed at the water, and I thought that was the job being done. And because almost nothing ever happened, I had no way of knowing whether I was doing it well. There is no feedback. If you watch perfectly for three hours, the result is that nothing happens. If you watch terribly for three hours, the result is also, almost always, that nothing happens. Those two mornings look identical from the outside and, more dangerously, they feel identical from the inside.

So I drifted. I want to be honest about how far. I was not on my phone — that would have got me sacked and I knew it. But I was in my own head. I planned things. I had long arguments with people who were not there. I would come out of a stretch and genuinely not be able to tell you whether the man in lane three had been swimming or resting for the last four minutes, and I would tell myself that if something had happened I would have noticed, because I was looking.

That is the thing I still think about: I was looking at the water for three hours and seeing it for perhaps forty minutes of that, and I had no idea, because looking and seeing use the same muscles from where I was sitting.

What broke it was small. A woman, one of the regulars, got out of the pool one morning and stood at the shallow end holding the rail for a while, and I clocked it and assumed she was catching her breath. She was there maybe ninety seconds. Then she left. And afterwards — an hour afterwards, in the changing room corridor — I found out from the receptionist that she had felt faint and gone and sat in reception for twenty minutes before driving home.

Nothing happened. That is the point. Nothing happened and I still had not seen it. She was six metres from me in my direct line of sight and I had processed her as scenery.

After that I stopped trusting my eyes and started building a system, which is the actual thing I taught myself. I gave myself a route: near lane, far lane, deep end, shallow end, the doors, back to the near lane, and I did it as an actual circuit, and I made myself name what I saw silently. Blue cap, breaststroke, steady. If I could not name it I had not seen it. I also learned that ten minutes standing up is worth thirty sitting down, and that if I let myself get warm I was finished, so I would take the cover off the chair.

Any experienced guard would tell you the scan pattern in a sentence. But there wasn't an experienced guard there at half six, and so I invented a worse version of something that already exists, which I have since decided is what most self-teaching actually is.

The larger thing it showed me is about attention rather than swimming pools. I had assumed attention was a state — that you were either paying it or not, and that a conscientious person paid it. It isn't a state. It's a task, and it decays, and it decays invisibly, and the only defence is a structure you impose on yourself from outside because you cannot feel it going.

I still think about the woman on the rail. She was fine. She came back the following Tuesday. But I sat in a high chair looking directly at her and did not see a person in trouble, and I would have sworn on anything that I was watching.
