I own a whistle that cost a dollar forty-nine at the drugstore near my house, the kind with an orange plastic pea inside that rattles instead of the metal ball a real referee's whistle has. I bought it two summers ago because I needed one by Tuesday and didn't want to order anything online and wait, and it has been in my pocket or around my neck for roughly two hundred practices since then, which is more time than I've spent with almost any other single object I own.

I coach an under-11 boys' team, seven to nine of them showing up on a given day, at a park with a field that floods in one corner every time it rains for more than an hour. I didn't plan to do this. My high school required a certain number of community service hours, and the rec league needed an assistant coach the same week I needed something to put on a form, and the two things matched up. I figured I'd do a season and move on to something that looked better on an application. That was almost two years ago.

The whistle became a routine almost by accident. The first week, I didn't have any system for getting nine-year-olds to stop running around and listen to me, and a parent volunteer suggested I get a whistle just so I had some sound that meant "come here" that wasn't my own voice getting hoarse. I started using three short blasts to call everyone in from wherever they'd scattered to on the field, and one long one to mean stop whatever you're doing right now. I didn't think about it much. It was just easier than yelling.

What I didn't expect was how much the kids came to depend on that specific sound. Somewhere around the sixth or seventh week, I noticed that the three short blasts did something instantaneous — kids who'd been fully checked out, picking grass, arguing about a video game, would drop everything and jog over the second they heard it, faster than they responded to their own names. It became a signal that meant practice, that meant this particular hour of the week, that meant me. A few of them started imitating the sound with their mouths when they wanted the rest of the group's attention during a scrimmage, which I found funnier than I probably should have.

There's a kid on the team, Dev, who joined midway through last season after his family moved from another part of the city. He didn't talk much those first few weeks, hung back at the edges during water breaks, flinched a little when the ball came his way like he expected to get in trouble for missing it. I don't think he trusted much about the situation, a new team, a new coach, a new set of kids who already had their own jokes. But he learned the whistle faster than anyone. Three short blasts and he was already moving before some of the boys who'd been on the team since the beginning. I think, for him, it was something he could get right immediately, on day one, in a place where almost nothing else felt familiar yet.

I still use the same whistle. It's chipped on one side now, the orange pea inside has faded to a dull cream color, and I've had to replace the cord twice because the boys keep tugging on it when they run past me, which I've decided to allow because stopping it would mean explaining why it bothers me, and it doesn't, not really.

I didn't understand, when I picked it off a drugstore rack because it was cheap and available, that I was choosing the one object that would end up marking two years of Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, that would become the sound nine specific nine-year-olds associate with being called back to something, with belonging somewhere for an hour twice a week regardless of what the rest of their day looked like. I don't think the whistle did anything on its own. I think it just happened to be there every single time, which turned out to be the whole job.
