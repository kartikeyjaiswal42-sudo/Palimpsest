We moved from Bangkok to Stockholm when I was nine. My parents didn't ask me if I wanted to move. They didn't ask my younger sister or my older brother either. It was a job opportunity for my father. It was one of those adult decisions that children are simply expected to absorb without complaint. So we absorbed it.

The first winter was the thing that I don't think we were prepared for. Not prepared in the practical sense—we bought coats and boots and heating systems worked fine. We were unprepared in a different way. We didn't know what being cold all the time would do to us, to our family, to the way we related to each other.

My mother hated it. She said it from the first week and kept saying it every day after that. The darkness. The snow that was beautiful for the first month and then just kept existing, kept needing to be moved and walked through and dealt with. The way the city looked like it was shutting down at three in the afternoon because the sun went down. The way people seemed to disappear into their houses and there was no street life, no spontaneous gathering of neighbors, no flowing in and out of public spaces the way there had been in Bangkok.

My father loved it. He said the snow was beautiful and the winter air was crisp and the skiing was incredible and people were friendlier than he expected. He would come home excited about things he had done, places he had been, and my mother would listen with visible irritation. She didn't want to hear about how wonderful winter was. She was stuck inside because of winter. She was isolated because of winter. She was slowly being erased by winter, or at least that's how she talked about it.

What made it an argument is that they couldn't leave it alone. They didn't have one conversation about whether we would stay or go. They had fifty conversations. My mother would suggest moving back to Bangkok or maybe to somewhere warmer. My father would say we had committed to three years. My mother would say she couldn't do three years. My father would point out that the kids were adapting. My mother would argue that adapting to something terrible was not a virtue. On and on. Through that entire winter and into the spring.

My brother tried to stay out of it. My sister cried sometimes but didn't really understand what the argument was actually about. I understood that my mother was lonely and my father was happy, and that these two things were directly opposed to each other. If he stayed happy about Stockholm, she had to stay sad about it. If she convinced him to leave, they could both be home. But he wasn't convinced.

What's unresolved is that my mother's sadness and my father's joy both turned out to be true. She really was isolated. Winter really was hard for her. And he really did love it. Really did find something there that he wasn't finding elsewhere. Neither of them was wrong. They just had irreconcilable experiences of the same place.

We ended up staying the three years. Then we ended up staying longer. Now we've been here for seven years. My mother learned Swedish. She found community. She's not happy exactly, but she's built a life. My father got promoted. He has friends. He takes every opportunity to ski.

But that argument never fully resolved. It just stopped being active. It's still there underneath, the feeling that my mother sacrificed something to make my father happy. That my father was insensitive to her suffering. That they made different choices about this place and they're both correct about it.

I think about it now because I'm applying to universities and some of them are far from home. I keep thinking about my mother's winter, about being somewhere that doesn't fit you, about the cost of loving someone who loves something you hate. I don't know if I'm afraid of becoming my mother or afraid of becoming my father. But that first winter taught me something about the difference between compromise and resentment. Between staying somewhere and staying somewhere while being angry about it. My parents stayed together through that winter, but they never quite agreed about it. That's a kind of way to live, I guess. I just know it's harder than it looks.
