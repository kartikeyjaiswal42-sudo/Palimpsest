On the shelf above my desk there is a piece of steel about the size of a paperback, two flat plates joined at a right angle, with a bead of weld running down the inside corner. It is not a useful object. It holds nothing, it fastens nothing, it is not a bracket for anything. It is the first fillet weld I laid down that did not have to be ground off and started again, and I keep it because it is the closest thing I have to a photograph of the day I found out what I was going to be interested in.

I took shop because my timetable had a hole in it. That is the whole origin story. Design and Technology, Thursday afternoons, in the workshop behind the science block that smells of cutting oil and hot metal and something faintly like a swimming pool. Mr Doyle taught it, and Mr Doyle had spent twenty-two years fabricating for a company that made agricultural trailers before he retrained, and he had a way of looking at your work that was worse than being shouted at. He would pick it up. He would turn it towards the window. He would put it down and say, again.

Welding is not what I thought it was. I thought it was gluing. You put the hot thing on the join and the metal sticks. What it actually is, and this took me most of a term to feel rather than know, is that you are melting both pieces of the parent metal into a single pool and then walking that pool along a line at a speed you have to hold steady inside about half a second either way. You are not attaching two things. You are briefly making them one thing, in a puddle roughly the width of a pencil, while looking at it through a lens so dark that in a lit room you can see nothing at all through it.

That last part is what broke me for weeks. The helmet goes down and the world disappears. You cannot see the workpiece, or your hands, or the line you drew in soapstone. You strike the arc and suddenly there is a small white sun eight inches from your face and the only thing in the universe is the pool. Everything I had learned about doing tasks — check, adjust, look around, check again — was useless, because there is nothing to look at except the one thing you are making. Mr Doyle said the same sentence to me about forty times: watch the puddle, not the wire.

My first attempts looked like something a bird had done. Too fast and you get a thin high bead sitting on top of the joint, welded to nothing, which will snap in your hands. Too slow and you burn through and there is a hole where your work was. Too far away and the arc goes long and the weld comes out full of porosity, little trapped bubbles that you cannot see until you cut it open.

The piece on my shelf came in March. I remember it because for about eleven seconds I was not thinking at all. My hand moved at a speed I had not decided on, and I could feel through the rod what the metal was doing, and when I lifted the helmet the bead was even, stacked like coins, no undercut at the toes, and Mr Doyle picked it up and turned it to the window and said nothing for a while and then said, right, and gave it back.

I have thought a lot about why that mattered so much more than any essay I have ever handed in. I think it is because steel is not persuadable. An examiner can be met halfway. A weld either has fusion or it does not, and if it does not it will fail under load in a way that is not about my opinion. There is a particular relief in a subject that cannot be argued with. It made me want to know why — why the heat-affected zone goes brittle, why some steels crack if you cool them too fast, why a bead stacks like that at all. That question is why I want to study materials engineering.

The little steel corner does not do anything. But something in it fused all the way through, and I made that happen with my hands, in the dark, at a speed I had to learn in my arm rather than my head. I would like the next four years to be more of that.
