The most important object I own is a cracked twenty-litre bucket that used to hold wall putty. It is white going grey, there are four holes drilled in the bottom with a heated nail, and a split runs down one side which my father closed with a strip of cycle tube and copper wire. It sits in the north-west corner of a balcony that is four feet by nine. For six years it has grown chillies.

The seeds came from my grandmother, in a plastic film canister that smelled of her cupboard. She grew sannam chillies in Guntur district on ground that belonged to her, and when my father took a job in Ghaziabad and we became people with a flat instead of land, she gave him the canister at the railway station as though it were money. He put it on a shelf and forgot it. I was eleven, and I found it, and I planted eight seeds in the putty bucket because it was the biggest container in the house. Two came up.

Almost everything I know now, I learned because that balcony is a bad place to grow anything. It faces north-west, so it gets direct sun only from about half past two until sunset, and in the monsoon it gets sheets of water off the roof of the flat above. I started dragging the bucket, first by hand and later on a wooden board with castor wheels my uncle welded, so it followed the light along the railing. I learned that market soil goes to concrete in a pot, and started mixing it with cocopeat and the compost from a second bucket where our vegetable peels go. I learned that chilli flowers on a balcony often fall off because there is no wind and no bee, and that you can shake the plant yourself, or touch each flower with a cotton bud, which I did for one whole February like a fool and which worked.

The year I was fourteen I lost everything. Thrips, which are so small that for three weeks I thought the leaves were curling from heat. I sprayed a neem solution I mixed too strong and burned what the insects had left. The bucket stood empty from March to July with a dead stem in it, and I did not throw the stem away, which my mother thought was strange. I had run out of the canister seeds by then, all except a pinch, and I was afraid of using them badly.

What got me back was that our neighbour in 301 asked for green chillies one evening because the shop had shut. I had none, and I minded that more than I expected. There are fourteen containers on our balcony now, and eleven of them are chillies: the Guntur ones from the canister, a bird's eye variety a friend's mother sent from Kerala, and a bhut jolokia that has produced exactly nine pods in two years and is the most arrogant plant I have met. I keep seeds in labelled envelopes and give them to whoever asks. Six flats in our building grow something now, mostly badly, and there is a WhatsApp group where people send photographs of yellow leaves and I say the same four things about drainage.

I am careful about what I claim for this. I have not solved anything. Eleven pots do not feed a family, and my grandmother, who died two winters ago, would have laughed at the amount of trouble I go to for a harvest she would call a handful. But I think this is the part of me that explains the rest. It is where I learned to look at a problem for three weeks before I understood it, and to accept that a thing can be entirely my fault and still be fixable. It is also, honestly, where I kept hold of a place I have only visited nine times.

The bucket is still in the corner. The copper wire has gone green and I have not replaced it, because the repair is now older than most of my memories of the plant that first grew there. Whatever I study, wherever I go, I am fairly sure I will start by finding out which direction the window faces.
