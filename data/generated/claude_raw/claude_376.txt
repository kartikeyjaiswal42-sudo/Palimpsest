The hair dryer was on its highest setting and I was running it back and forth across the plastic sheet, the way the box showed, and the wrinkles were not coming out. They were moving. Every time I flattened one section another one bloomed two feet away, and my mother was standing behind me in her coat holding the second roll of double-sided tape, saying nothing, which in our family is a whole paragraph.

This was the third window. The box said "insulates up to five windows" and had a photograph on the front of a woman in a cardigan smiling at a pane of glass. It cost fourteen dollars and ninety-nine cents at the hardware store on Kildare Avenue, and I had picked it out myself, because I was the one whose English was best and because I had spent two evenings reading about heat loss on the library computer and had arrived home the expert.

We had come in August. Nobody had explained a Chicago January to us in a way that meant anything. My uncle had said cold, and we had a word for cold, and the word was wrong. The apartment was on the third floor of a building from 1926 with single-pane windows in wooden frames that had been painted shut and then painted again, and you could hold your palm four inches from the glass in the bedroom and feel the outside come through as a steady, patient pressure. The radiators clanked and gave off heat that seemed to die halfway across the room. My sister slept in a hat.

So: the film kit. Tape around the frame, stretch the plastic over it, shrink it tight with a hair dryer until it disappears and becomes an invisible second pane with dead air behind it. That is how it works. That is genuinely how it works, and it works well, and I could explain the physics of it to you then and can now.

What I did not do was clean the frames first. The instructions said to wipe them with rubbing alcohol and let them dry completely, and I read that line, and I decided it was the kind of instruction that exists for people who are careless. Our frames had eighty years of paint and dust and a film of cooking grease from the previous tenants. The tape went down on top of all of it and held beautifully for nine days.

I came home from school on a Thursday to find the bedroom sheet hanging off the top corner and rippling in the draft like something breathing. By Saturday all three had come away. My father tried to press one back and the tape rolled up under his thumb into a gray worm and took a strip of paint with it.

We did not have another fifteen dollars for a second kit that month. What we had was a roll of packing tape, and my mother spent an evening taping the plastic directly to the glass in overlapping strips, which does not create a sealed air pocket and does not insulate much of anything, and which looked exactly like what it was. It stayed up until April. Everyone who came to the apartment could see it from the street.

I keep the empty box. It is flattened, in the bottom of a drawer in my desk with old chargers and a passport photo of my sister at nine. The woman on the front is still smiling at her window. On the back, in a panel I did not read until March, there is a diagram with four steps, and the first one is a small drawing of a cloth and a bottle, and underneath it the words: Surfaces must be clean and completely dry. Do not skip this step.
