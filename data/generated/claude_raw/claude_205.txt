My co-host Naomi and I disagreed, from almost the first week, about what our podcast was supposed to sound like, and by the time I finally came around to her side of it, the show only had one episode left in it.

We started it in January, an interview show built around a simple premise — talk to people at our school with unusual part-time jobs or strange hobbies, keep each episode under fifteen minutes, release weekly. I wrote out a full question list before every recording, six to eight questions, in order, and I ran the interviews the way I imagined a real journalist would, moving crisply from one prepared question to the next, checking items off a legal pad as we went. I thought structure was what made something sound professional. I'd grown up half-listening to news interview shows in the car with my dad, and in my head, professional meant tight, meant controlled, meant nothing left to chance.

Naomi hated this from the start, and she said so, respectfully but plainly, after our very first episode, an interview with a kid who trained falcons for a bird-control company. She said the falcon guy had clearly wanted to talk about something that came up sideways, almost by accident, about how his dad had taught him the whole thing before dying two years earlier, and that instead of following that thread, I'd glanced at my legal pad and moved on to my next prepared question about falconry equipment, because it was next on the list. She said the equipment question got us a fine, forgettable answer, and the thread we cut off might have gotten us something real. I respected Naomi a lot — she was a better writer than me, sharper in conversation generally, someone whose opinion I actually weighed instead of just hearing — and I still disagreed with her completely. I told her structure was what kept an interview from wandering into nothing, that loose conversation sounds unprofessional, that we'd lose whatever audience we managed to build if episodes felt aimless instead of tight.

We had some version of this argument before nearly every recording after that. Episode two, with a girl who did voice acting for indie games, I stuck to my list again, and Naomi went quiet during the debrief afterward in a way that told me more than anything she could have said out loud. Episode three, an interview with a guy who restored old bicycles in his garage, I loosened up slightly, let one tangent run an extra minute, and immediately steered us back to the list the second it started to feel unstructured, still not willing to fully let go of my plan.

Episode four was the one that actually changed my mind, and it happened almost by accident, not because I'd decided in advance to try it Naomi's way. Our guest that week, a junior who kept bees on her family's small plot of land outside town, mentioned partway through my third prepared question that she'd started keeping bees the same month her older brother had been deployed overseas, and that tending them had become the thing that got her out of bed on the mornings she was most worried about him. My legal pad had a question right after that about honey yield and hive maintenance costs. I looked down at it, actually looked down at the specific words on the page, and for the first time all season I just didn't ask it. I asked her instead what it felt like to check the hive on a morning when she hadn't heard from him in a while. She talked for almost four minutes straight, unprompted, more honestly than anyone had talked in three previous episodes combined, and Naomi, sitting across the table, caught my eye for just a second, not smug, just quietly confirmed in something she'd been saying since January.

That episode is the best thing we made, and it's also the last one we made. Naomi had a heavier course load starting that spring and the show had drained more of both of our afternoons than either of us had planned for, and without ever really deciding to stop, we stopped, the way a lot of things end. I think about the timing more than I probably should — that I only actually understood what Naomi had been telling me for four months on the exact episode that turned out to be our final one, which feels less like an ending and more like an argument I finally lost just as it stopped being available to keep having.
