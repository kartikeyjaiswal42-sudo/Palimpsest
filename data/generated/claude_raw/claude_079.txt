I started working the ten-to-four shift at the regional distribution center last summer, unloading trucks and scanning pallets alongside a crew of mostly adults twice my age, one of whom was a man named Dale who'd worked there almost eleven years and taught me more in my first two weeks than the actual training video did in three hours.

Dale and I disagreed about one specific thing, repeatedly, for months, and never really resolved it. Every time a piece of equipment on the floor seemed slightly off — a pallet jack pulling to one side, a guard rail on the conveyor sitting loose, a floor sensor that beeped when nothing was there — I wanted to file an incident report, the little yellow form that goes to the shift supervisor and, eventually, up to corporate safety. Dale thought this was, in his words, a good way to get labeled a problem before you'd even earned the right to have opinions. He'd worked there long enough to watch two people get quietly moved to worse shifts after filing too many reports, and he genuinely believed, from real experience, that keeping your head down and just working around a busted guard rail was the smarter play for someone who needed the job.

I respected him completely. He was the reason I wasn't fired in my first month, when I mis-scanned an entire pallet and he covered for me without being asked. But I filled out the yellow form anyway, almost every time something seemed wrong, quietly, on my own break, dropping it in the supervisor's box without making it a whole conversation. It became a habit before I really noticed it had become one — check the equipment at the start of shift, note anything off, file the form if it seemed worth filing, do it again the next night. Dale would sometimes see me writing one out and just shake his head, not angry, more resigned, like he was watching a younger version of himself learn a lesson he'd already learned the hard way.

In October, the guard rail on conveyor belt six, the one I'd flagged twice that same month, came loose while a coworker named Priscilla was clearing a jam near it. She wasn't hurt, not seriously, just a scraped forearm and a bad scare, but it was close enough that corporate safety actually came out to inspect the whole line the next week. When they pulled the maintenance file on that conveyor, my two reports were sitting right there with the exact date and description of the loose rail, weeks before it actually failed. The supervisor thanked me in front of the crew, which was its own strange discomfort, and Dale, standing a few feet away, didn't say anything at all, just nodded once, which from Dale is close to an apology.

We still disagree about it, honestly. He thinks I got lucky that the paper trail helped instead of hurt me, and he's not wrong that it could have gone the other way, that plenty of people file reports and get nothing but a reputation for it. I think the habit was worth keeping regardless of how it turned out, that writing something down the moment you notice it's wrong matters independent of whether anyone reads it in time. Neither of us has really moved off our position. But he stopped shaking his head when he sees me filling one out, and some nights now he mentions something himself, almost like he's asking me to notice it for him.
