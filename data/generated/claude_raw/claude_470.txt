I taught myself physical therapy exercises from YouTube videos while my father relearned how to walk, and what that taught me is that the internet will teach you how to do basically anything, but it won't teach you how to stop being afraid.

He had the stroke on a Tuesday morning. He was in his fifties. He was relatively healthy. He went to the gym. And then he had a stroke and the left side of his body stopped working the way it was supposed to work. It was the kind of thing that happens to other people's fathers, not ours. But it happened to ours.

The hospital was loud and fluorescent and everyone there seemed to know what they were doing except us. They ran tests and did scans and used words like "ischemic" and "prognosis" that didn't mean anything to me. What mattered was that he was alive and his brain was damaged and nobody could really tell us what that meant for his future. The doctors talked about recovery. The physical therapists talked about neuroplasticity. Everyone talked about the "window" where recovery was most possible, and that window was weeks, maybe months, and after that it got harder.

We came home with discharge papers and a list of exercises he needed to do. Daily. Repeatedly. The exercises were simple. Arm movements. Leg movements. Balance work. The theory was that by repeating them, his brain would rewire. New neural pathways would form. His body would relearn things it had forgotten.

My mom hired a physical therapist to come twice a week. But the rest of the time, between those visits, he needed to do the work. And my mom couldn't do it alone—she had to work to keep insurance. So I started helping. I'd come home from school and we'd go through the exercises. Twenty reps of this. Hold this for thirty seconds. Do that five times.

I watched YouTube videos about stroke recovery to understand what we were doing. I learned about motor cortex and muscle memory and why repetition mattered. I learned that every single repetition was theoretically helping his brain reorganize itself. I learned that motivation mattered because people gave up too easily. I learned all of this from various physical therapists and recovery specialists on YouTube, people I'd never met explaining their expertise to a camera.

What I didn't learn from YouTube is how to watch your father try to lift his arm and not quite get there. How to see him frustrated that his body wasn't doing what he wanted. How to hear him say things like "I used to be strong" with his voice breaking. YouTube doesn't teach you how to keep showing up every single day to do something that feels like it's not working. To do fifty reps of an exercise that looks tiny and meaningless but is supposedly rewiring his brain. To believe in something you can't see happening.

I was afraid the whole time. Not the dramatic kind of afraid where you can't function. Just the persistent kind where you're doing everything right but you're terrified it's not going to be enough. That his brain wasn't going to rewire. That the neuroplasticity wouldn't happen. That he was going to be stuck like this—limited, frustrated, no longer the person he was before.

By month four something shifted. He could lift his arm higher than he could before. He could walk further. The progress was real and measurable. But that's when I realized that my fear hadn't actually changed. Even with evidence that things were improving, I was still afraid. I was still bracing for something bad. I was still preparing for the worst case scenario.

What YouTube didn't teach me is that hope and fear can exist at the same time. That you can see someone getting better and still be terrified that it's not going to last. That recovery is ongoing and uncertain and you can do everything right and things can still go wrong. You can do fifty reps every day and still not know if it's enough.

He's mostly recovered now, years later. He still does exercises. He'll probably always do exercises. But I learned without any teacher that some things just require you to show up and do the work and live in the uncertainty anyway. YouTube can teach you the mechanics. It can't teach you the faith that it matters.
