There's a stopwatch in my backpack, the cheap plastic kind you'd get free with a magazine subscription, and it doesn't even work anymore — the display went dead sometime last spring — but I still carry it to every tournament, and I still take it out of my bag before every round and set it on the table next to my flow pad, dead screen and all.

It was Sam's before it was mine. My debate partner handed it to me in a parking lot the fall of junior year, a few weeks after I'd lost a semifinal round at States that I should have won, in a match where the judge's ballot used the word "aggressive" three separate times, not as a compliment. I'd argued the whole round the way I argued every round back then — fast, loud, cutting in the second I heard an opening I could exploit, treating silence like wasted time. The other team's first speaker just let me finish. Every time. And she beat me doing it.

Sam's fix wasn't a pep talk. It was the stopwatch. He said I needed to physically time my pauses in practice because I genuinely could not feel the difference between two seconds of silence and half a second, that my sense of pacing had been trained wrong for three years running and no amount of me just deciding to be different was going to undo that on its own.

So we made it a routine. Every Tuesday practice round, before we started, Sam would click the stopwatch and hand it to whoever was judging that day, and the rule was simple: after my opponent finished speaking, I had to let two full seconds pass, visible on that little plastic screen, before I opened my mouth. It sounds small. It felt, the first few weeks, like holding my breath underwater — physically uncomfortable in a way I hadn't expected silence to be. I'd sit there counting in my head, one, two, watching the numbers change, fighting the exact same reflex every single time.

I did this every Tuesday for months. Not because it was fun — it wasn't, particularly, it felt like practicing patience is supposed to feel, which is to say tedious and slightly humiliating — but because it was the one thing standing between the debater I'd been and the one I was trying to become. The stopwatch broke in February, right in the middle of a practice round, the screen just going dark mid-count, and by then it didn't matter. I'd counted to two so many times that my body did it without the numbers anymore.

I won a round at a tournament in March against a team ranked well above us, and afterward the judge said something about my composure, my willingness to let silence sit in the room instead of filling it, and I remember standing there thinking about a dead stopwatch sitting uselessly in my backpack, still doing its job somehow, months after it stopped being able to tell time.

I don't need it anymore, not really. I could leave it home and nothing about my next round would change. But I still take it out before every match and set it next to my flow pad, dead screen up, and I couldn't tell you exactly why except that it feels less like superstition and more like keeping the receipt for a debt I paid off slowly, one uncomfortable Tuesday at a time, and some habits you keep going through even after they've stopped being necessary, because the going-through is what taught you something in the first place, and you're not entirely sure who you'd be without it.
