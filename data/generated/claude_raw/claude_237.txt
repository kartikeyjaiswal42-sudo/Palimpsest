The map is a 1996 New York City subway map, laminated, folded so many times over so many years that the fold lines have gone white and slightly translucent, like the plastic is wearing through from the inside. It belonged to my grandfather, who rode those trains every weekday for over twenty years to a printing job in the garment district, and it ended up in a box of his things after he died, along with his reading glasses and a pocketknife and a stack of receipts nobody needed to keep. I was ten. I took the map because nobody else wanted it.

I have never been to New York City. I want to say that plainly because it matters to what I did with the map, which was memorize it. Not casually — completely. I could tell you the order of every stop on the 4 train from Woodlawn to Utica Avenue. I knew which transfers required a walk versus a same-platform switch, which lines ran express during rush hour, where the map's own geography lied a little to keep the diagram clean. I did this for years, on my own time, for no reason anyone in my family understood, and when relatives asked what I was "into" I didn't really have an answer that made sense out loud, so I just said trains.

When I was fourteen, my mother planned a trip to New York for my grandfather's unveiling, the Jewish ceremony that happens about a year after a death, when the headstone gets set. It mattered enormously to her — she hadn't been back to the city since the funeral, and she was anxious about the whole day in a way I didn't fully register at the time. I told her I'd handle getting us from the hotel to the cemetery in Queens. I was proud of this. I'd been waiting years, essentially, for a reason to use what I knew.

I planned the route off my grandfather's map. I didn't check anything current, because in my head the map wasn't outdated, it was just the map, the one I'd built my entire understanding of that city on. I got us on the wrong line entirely — a station had been renamed and another had closed for renovation sometime in the years since 1996, and neither of those changes existed anywhere in what I'd memorized. We ended up nearly forty minutes late to the cemetery, arriving as the rabbi was already partway through, my mother crying in a way that had nothing to do with being late and everything to do with the day itself, except that I had made her late on top of it.

What I think about isn't really the wrong train. It's that when we finally sat down afterward, my first instinct was to explain what had changed on the line, to defend the map, to make sure everyone understood it wasn't my fault, it was the MTA's. I actually said something like that, at my grandfather's unveiling, to my mother, who was still holding a tissue in her fist. I wanted to be right about the subway system more than I wanted to be present for what the day actually was.

I still have the map. It's not laminated well enough anymore to survive much more folding, and I don't touch it as often as I used to, because for a long time picking it up meant I was choosing the version of that afternoon where I was the expert instead of the version where my mother needed her son and got a kid reciting station names instead. I know both things can be true about the same object. I haven't figured out what to do with that yet, so mostly I just keep it in the drawer and take it out on my own, quietly, the way I probably should have all along.
