I started fixing broken laptops in the library sophomore year, unofficially, after I got a school-issued one running again for a freshman who'd been told by the IT office it would take three weeks to replace. Word spread fast. By spring I had a waitlist taped inside my binder, mostly cracked screens, dead batteries, and the specific virus that seemed to hit half the ninth grade every February from a shared flash drive nobody would admit to using.

The mistake I still think about happened with a senior named Fatima's laptop, which wouldn't boot past a blue error screen. I decided the fastest fix was a full reset, and I told her, confidently, that resets don't touch personal files, which is true on a school-managed laptop with everything backed up to the cloud automatically. Hers wasn't fully synced. I didn't check that before I started, because by then I'd done maybe thirty resets without a single problem and had stopped treating each one as its own separate risk. She lost four months of coursework for a research project, including interview recordings she couldn't easily redo, and I watched her realize it in real time, standing next to me at the library table.

There was no way to undo it. I helped her rebuild what I could from memory and old emails, which recovered maybe a third of what was gone, and I sat with the rest of it, the part that just stayed lost, longer than I expected to. I had gotten good enough at the mechanical part of fixing laptops that I'd quietly stopped respecting how much of someone's actual work lives inside a machine I was treating like a routine repair.

I check for backups manually now, every single time, no exceptions, even when someone insists it's fine and they're in a hurry. It slows me down. I think it's the only reason anyone still trusts me with their laptop at all.

What I'd bring to campus is that same slow habit, not the technical skill by itself — plenty of people can reset a computer. I'd bring the specific caution that comes from having actually cost someone something real, the instinct to ask what's at stake before I touch anything, and a genuine willingness to be the person other students go to at eleven at night when something breaks and the help desk is closed until morning. That role doesn't exist unless someone decides to be it, unglamorously, one broken laptop at a time.
