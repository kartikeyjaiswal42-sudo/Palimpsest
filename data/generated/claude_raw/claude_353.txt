My uncle Ray gave me his old Zenith radio the year it stopped working, which tells you something about the gift already. Big wooden cabinet, cracked dial glass, a knob missing off the front so you had to turn the tuning shaft with pliers. He said, "It's yours if you want it, it's junk otherwise," and I said yes before really thinking it through, because I liked the idea of fixing something more than I understood what fixing it would actually take.

It took about a year, on and off. I learned to solder that spring, badly at first, burned myself twice, replaced capacitors that had gone bad decades before I was born, chased a hum through the circuit for six weeks that turned out to be one bad ground wire the whole time. Ray helped some of it, especially early on, showing me how to read the tube layout, but mostly he just let me struggle through it, coming by every couple weeks to ask how the "old girl" was doing, like she was a horse and not a radio.

Here's the part we still argue about. When it finally worked — actually pulled in a station, static clearing into an AM broadcast one night in my garage, loud enough that I yelled for my mom to come hear it — Ray wanted me to sell it. Said a working Zenith from that era was worth real money to a collector, more than I'd guess, and that I should let it go and put the money toward something useful, a car, college, whatever. I said no. I'm still saying no. He brings it up maybe twice a year, usually at Thanksgiving, half-joking but not entirely, and I give him the same answer every time, and neither of us has budged an inch in three years of this.

I don't think he's wrong that the money would help. I think about that sometimes, actually, more than I let on to him. But I didn't spend a year chasing a bad ground wire because I wanted a radio-shaped investment. I spent it because there was something about bringing a dead thing back to a state where it could do the one job it was built for — pull a signal out of the air and turn it into a voice in a room — that felt more important than what the wood and the tubes were worth to a stranger. Ray, who has sold off half his own collection over the years, genuinely doesn't understand this distinction, or maybe he understands it and just disagrees with where I've landed on it, which might be worse, in a way, because it means we're not confused, we're just different.

The radio sits in my room now, and it still works, mostly — the volume knob sticks a little in humid weather, and I still haven't found a replacement for the missing tuning knob, so the pliers live on the shelf next to it, part of the whole apparatus at this point. Ray still asks about her at Thanksgiving. I still say she's not for sale. We've never resolved it and I don't think we're going to, and there's something almost comforting in that now, the two of us circling the same argument every November, both of us right about our own reasons, neither one giving ground, the radio playing quietly in the next room the whole time we talk about it.
