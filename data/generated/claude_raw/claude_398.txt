Mr. Chen let me use the lab after school, which seems like a small thing but actually transformed the entire trajectory of my high school experience.

I got to know him in Chemistry when I was in tenth grade. I was genuinely interested in chemistry—not just in the classes, but in the actual science. I'd ask questions that went beyond what we were covering in the curriculum. I'd read about chemistry research that was being done at universities. I asked him if there was any way to do actual experiments outside of the standard lab schedule.

He said yes. He gave me a key and told me I could come use the lab after school, as long as I cleaned up after myself and didn't do anything dangerous. The only real condition was that I had to document everything I did.

That key changed my life. I mean that sounds dramatic, but it's true.

I started spending hours in that lab. I'd work on experiments that nobody was asking me to do. I'd test hypotheses that I was curious about. I'd read papers and try to replicate the experiments. I'd fail at things and then figure out why I'd failed and try again. There was nobody watching me, nobody grading me on whether I was doing it right. There was just me and the science and the need to understand something.

The only conflict I had with Mr. Chen about this was when he wanted me to slow down.

I was probably doing too much. I was coming in every single day. I was staying for two or three hours. I was getting obsessive about this one experiment where I was testing crystal growth rates under different conditions. He pulled me aside and said I seemed like I was burning out, that I should maybe take it easier, do the lab work less frequently.

I remember being frustrated with him about it. I felt like he was trying to limit me, like he was being parental in a way that I hadn't asked for. I told him I was fine, I wanted to keep going. I understood the risks of burnout, and I was managing it.

We had a sort of argument about this, or not an argument exactly, but a real disagreement. He was worried about me. I was annoyed that he was worried about me.

But he was right, which I only realized later.

I did eventually get burned out. I got into an experiment so deeply that when I hit a wall I couldn't get past, I fell into this spiral of self-doubt about whether I was actually capable of doing this kind of research. I didn't want to go to the lab anymore. I felt like I'd failed, and everything else felt tainted by that failure.

It took me about a month to climb out of that hole. Mr. Chen was patient about it. He didn't say anything. He just let me come back when I was ready, and we went back to our routine.

What I understood later is that he wasn't trying to limit me. He was trying to teach me something about how to be a person who does science in a sustainable way. He was trying to show me that burnout is real and that protecting yourself from it is part of being a good scientist, not a sign of weakness.

We're still in disagreement about some things. He wanted me to focus more on the coursework and less on the lab work. I wanted to do everything. But I think he was right that I needed to learn how to care about something without letting it consume me entirely. That's a lesson that goes way beyond science.
