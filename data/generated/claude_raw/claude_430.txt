The business lasted three weeks. I thought that was something I'd be ashamed of, but mostly I just think about that three weeks as the place where I finally understood myself.

It started the way a lot of stupid ideas start—with me thinking I had discovered something nobody else had thought of. Phone case designs. But not boring designs. Cool designs. Designs that meant something. I'd learn design software, I'd commission artwork from artists, I'd sell them online, and I'd make thousands of dollars. It was obvious. Why wasn't everyone doing this?

I made the business plan on a Saturday. It was meticulous. I calculated margins, researched suppliers, created a whole marketing strategy. By Sunday night I'd registered as a business, created an Instagram account, and ordered a bulk supply of phone cases from a wholesaler. I was so confident. I've never been more confident about anything.

The first two weeks I was doing everything. I was learning Adobe Illustrator at midnight. I was designing cases that I thought were clever. I was setting up the online store. I was trying to convince artists to work with me. I was telling everyone I knew about the business. I was this person who was doing something. Finally, after years of talking about business ideas and never doing anything, I was actually doing something.

Then I sold two phone cases. To friends. Out of pity, I think, looking back. And I realized that the designing part was taking hours and the selling part was not working at all. And the business plan I'd been so proud of had an assumption that just wasn't true—that people actually wanted unique artistic phone cases badly enough to pay a premium for them.

I could have pivoted. That's the entrepreneurship advice—pivot, adapt, find the right market. But instead I just stopped. I felt this sudden exhaustion that wasn't physical. It was existential. I realized I didn't actually care about phone cases. I didn't care about business. What I'd cared about was the idea of myself as a person who was building something. The actual building was just unpleasant.

I told myself that it was fine, businesses fail all the time, that wasn't a reflection on me. But the truth is I was embarrassed. Not because it failed but because I'd put so much energy into the idea of it while simultaneously not caring at all about the actual work. I wanted the success without the grind. I wanted to be seen as someone who was doing something without actually having to do the something.

I left the website up for a few months, thinking maybe something would happen. Nothing did. Eventually I took it down. I wrote off the bulk order of phone cases as an expensive lesson.

What changed my mind wasn't a single moment but a slow realization. The place where I feel most myself is when I'm not performing a version of myself. When I'm not trying to look like a person who's building something. It's when I'm actually interested in something, when the doing and the identity are the same thing, not performing as a person.

Now when I have an idea for a business or a project, I ask myself whether I care about the actual work or just about being seen doing it. Usually it's the latter, and I just don't do it. Sometimes it's genuinely the former, and then I'm willing to put in the time. The business taught me that filtering mechanism. It taught me to be more honest with myself about my own motivations.
