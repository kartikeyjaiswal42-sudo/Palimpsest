The duffel bag is army green, canvas gone soft at the corners, and one wheel has been missing since our third move, replaced by nothing, so it drags along one side with a scraping sound I could pick out of a crowded hallway blindfolded. There's a luggage tag looped through the handle with five city names on it, four of them crossed out in different pens, because I kept meaning to buy a new tag and never did. That bag has been packed and unpacked in six different bedrooms since I was eight, which works out to a new school roughly every eighteen months, which my dad still calls opportunity and I have, more than once, called something else to his face.

He's a civil engineer who takes on infrastructure contracts, bridges mostly, sometimes a water system, and the work goes wherever the project is, which has meant Ohio, then Texas, then back to Ohio but a different city, then Georgia, then North Carolina, then here. Every time, the duffel bag came first, packed the night before the movers arrived with whatever I couldn't stand to put in a box, and every time I told myself the same thing, that this would be the last one, that surely a person only needs to start over so many times before the universe runs out of new schools to put them in.

Two years ago, when he came home and said there was a project in Charlotte, I told him no. Not in the calm way I'd rehearsed it in my head for approximately a year, but standing in the kitchen, saying that I was fifteen years old and had exactly one friend group I hadn't had to explain myself to from scratch, and that his job had cost me every single one before that. He said, and I remember this exactly, that he wasn't going to apologize for providing for us, and that I'd adjust the way I always adjusted, like adjusting was a skill I should be thanking him for instead of a wound I was tired of reopening. I said some things back that I mostly still stand by and one thing I don't, about wishing he'd married someone whose job didn't drag us everywhere, which was cruel and aimed at my mother by accident, and he went quiet in the way he goes quiet when he's decided a conversation is over rather than resolved.

We moved. I packed the duffel bag myself that time, wheel still dragging, tag still crossed out and rewritten. We have never actually finished that conversation from the kitchen. We don't fight about it anymore, mostly because I stopped bringing it up and he stopped explaining himself, which isn't the same as agreement, just a kind of ceasefire neither of us negotiated out loud.

I'm packing that same bag again now, for college, and it will be, undramatically, the seventh time. I've gotten strangely good at making a new place feel livable within about a week, which is either the best thing my childhood gave me or the thing I resent most about it, and I genuinely don't know which, some days both at once. My dad helped me pick out the flight. He didn't mention the bag. Neither did I.
