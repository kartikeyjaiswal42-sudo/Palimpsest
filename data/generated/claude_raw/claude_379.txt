We moved from Cebu to Winnipeg in August, which everyone told us was the easy month, and by November I understood what they meant by easy, because by December the easy part was long gone and the temperature stopped making sense to me as a number and started just meaning pain in a specific spot on my face.

My father's solution to almost everything that first winter was the same: put on your boots, we're walking to the corner store. It didn't matter if we needed anything. Some nights we walked the six blocks there and back and came home with a single carton of milk we didn't need, because the walk itself was the point, though he never explained it that directly. He said only that if we hid inside all winter waiting for it to feel normal, it would never feel normal, and that the fastest way through was through, on foot, every single evening, in whatever the temperature happened to be.

I hated it for the first month. My eyelashes froze on the walk home more than once. I complained the whole six blocks some nights, in Tagalog, so the few people we passed wouldn't understand exactly how much I was suffering. My father just kept walking, unbothered, occasionally pointing out something — a specific house's Christmas lights, a dog in a small coat, the way the snow squeaked differently depending on how cold it actually was, a detail I didn't believe until I noticed it myself.

Somewhere around January the walk stopped being a punishment and started being the one part of the day I could count on. We started recognizing the same people at the corner store, the same dog walker, the same old man who cleared his own walk and half of his neighbor's without being asked. My father would nod at him every night, and eventually they started talking, mostly about the snow, weeks before either of us kids knew the man's name. That's how we met Mr. Halvorsen, who later showed my father how to actually use a snowblower instead of shoveling by hand, which was the single most useful piece of information anyone gave us that entire winter.

The obstacle wasn't really the cold, though the cold was real and I am still, three years later, not fully reconciled to it. The obstacle was the specific fear that we'd landed somewhere that would never feel like ours, that we'd spend years circling the edges of a place without actually being let in. What I learned from my father's stubborn nightly walk, which felt at the time like a strange and pointless ritual, is that belonging doesn't arrive all at once as a feeling — it arrives in blocks of six, repeated enough times that a neighborhood starts to recognize your shape coming down the sidewalk in the dark.

We still do the walk most evenings, though less as ritual now and more because it's genuinely become the thing we do. Mr. Halvorsen waves from his porch when he sees us, snowblower parked and ready by November first every year since. I don't love winter. I've stopped needing to.
