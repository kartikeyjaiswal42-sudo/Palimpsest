My mom used to say that I would never make it in another country because I was too used to things being a certain way. The comment stung when she said it, though she meant it as a challenge, not an insult. What I didn't tell her was that staying the same way in a new country is what I had chosen to do, and it took her six months to figure out that the choice itself was what changed me.

When we moved to Singapore in the middle of tenth grade, I brought my stubbornness with me like luggage. I woke up every morning at six-thirty, which is what time I had always woken up. I packed my lunch the same way I had always packed it. I bought the same brand of shampoo that I had used in Delhi, at an expensive expat store, because switching felt like giving up. These tiny habits were the only things that felt like mine in a place where everything else was unfamiliar.

But here's what happened, and I still don't fully understand the mechanism of it: those small routines became the bridge from the life I had known to the life I was actually living.

Every morning, waking at the same time, I would open my window and notice something new about the humidity or the light or the sound of people starting their day outside. Every time I made the decision to pack my lunch the same way, I became slightly more confident that I could be consistent somewhere, even here. The shampoo, which I bought at an expat store, led me to talk to the shop owner, who mentioned a study group at a different school, which led me to meet people outside my immediate circle.

These weren't glamorous changes. They were the kind of thing you don't even notice happening while it's happening. I was just trying to stay the same, and in doing so, I became someone new. It sounds paradoxical, but it's not really. It's just that small repeated actions accumulate in unexpected ways.

My mom brought it up once, maybe three months into the year. We were eating dinner, and she said, "You've settled here, haven't you?" And I realized that I had. Not because I had made some big decision to integrate or be brave or open my mind to new experiences. I had settled because I had been too stubborn to change my morning routine, and my morning routine had eventually changed everything else.

I still prefer some of my Indian brands. I still think of Delhi when I smell certain spices or hear certain songs. But I also have a favorite place to eat near school now, and friends who text me random things, and a version of myself that makes sense in this place.

The irony is that my inflexibility was what made me flexible. By refusing to change the small things, I had the stability to change the big things. Every routine morning, I had enough solid ground beneath me to step into something unknown. And now, looking back, I think that's maybe what it means to actually grow somewhere new. Not to abandon who you were, but to bring that person along steadily, habit by habit, until you're suddenly somewhere else entirely without having planned the journey.
