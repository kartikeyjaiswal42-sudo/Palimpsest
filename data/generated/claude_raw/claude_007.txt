Mr. Okafor set two rolls of quarters and a lottery ticket on the counter and said, the way he said it every single Thursday, "just put it on the tab, I'll square up Saturday," and I rang it through without writing it down, because it was seven in the evening, the line behind him was four people deep, and I had already worked six hours after school without a break.

I did not write it down. That's the whole failure, contained in that one sentence, though it took me two weeks to understand how much weight it carried. My family's store runs on a paper ledger my grandfather started twenty years ago, a spiral notebook behind the register where regulars who are short on a given day get marked down and settle up later, an arrangement built entirely on people being honest and my family remembering to ask. Mr. Okafor had been in that notebook, on and off, since before I was born. He'd been our upstairs neighbor's father for a while, then just a man who came in Thursdays for quarters and a ticket and stayed to talk about boxing with my dad, who doesn't even like boxing but likes Mr. Okafor.

Two weeks later my father noticed the register was short and pulled out the notebook, going line by line, and there was no entry for Mr. Okafor's twelve dollars and change. My father asked me if I remembered it. I did remember it — that was the strange part, I remembered his voice, the exact roll of quarters, the specific joke he made about his numbers — I had simply not written it down, in the rush of a Thursday evening, and had also, somehow, in all the busyness since, forgotten to tell my father it was owed.

My father didn't yell. He said, quietly, that the store isn't really a store, not the way I think of a store — it's a arrangement, held together by a notebook and a family's word and forty years of a neighborhood deciding we're trustworthy enough to run a tab with. He said one missed entry doesn't break that. He said it in a tone that made it clear he wasn't finished being disappointed, even as the sentence was meant to reassure me.

I never told Mr. Okafor. My father paid the twelve dollars out of his own pocket the following Saturday and marked the ledger settled, and Mr. Okafor never knew there'd been a gap at all, never knew his name had briefly gone missing from a book that has held his name, on and off, for longer than I've been alive. He still comes in every Thursday. He still says the same sentence about squaring up Saturday, and I still write it down now before he's even finished the ticket, twice, once in the notebook and once on a receipt I tuck under the register drawer, which is probably more caution than the job requires.

He asked me once, a few months after, why I seemed so serious about his little tab all of a sudden, like it was a bank loan and not two rolls of quarters. I told him I just liked to keep good records. He laughed at that, the big laugh he uses for things he thinks are funnier than they actually are, and bought his ticket, and left.
