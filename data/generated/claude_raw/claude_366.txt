I learned to swim when I was sixteen. This shouldn't be interesting to anyone, but there's a photograph of me in the community pool wearing goggles that are too big for my face, and every time I see it, I think about how convinced I was that I would fail.

The goggles were the first thing I bought. I was so nervous about starting that I made a whole purchase feel ceremonial. Goggles, swim cap, a one-piece suit even though everyone else wore bikinis. I showed up at the pool early and stood by the edge for fifteen minutes before the lesson started. My hands were actually shaking.

The instructor, Ms. Reid, took one look at me and said "You're nervous." I wanted to deny it, but she was right. She also didn't make a big deal out of it. She just said most people who learn to swim late are nervous, and then she held out her hand and pulled me into the water. I remember the shock of it—how much there was to think about. The temperature, the buoyancy, the way my body wanted to do everything wrong.

She started with the basics. How to float. How to move your arms. How to breathe without panicking. Everything felt impossible and then she would show me again and it would feel slightly less impossible. After three sessions, I could barely stay above the water. After five sessions, I could do a lap if I didn't think about it.

The photograph was from the day I swam my first full lap without stopping. I look terrified in it. The goggles had slipped down my nose. My wet hair was plastered to my head in a way that doesn't look flattering. But I had done it. I had completed the thing that seemed impossible.

What made it hard wasn't the swimming though. It was all the thirteen-year-olds who had been coming there since they were five, who belonged in the water in a way I didn't. It was having to be the worst one in the group. It was the fear that my body would betray me, that I would panic, that I would drown even though Ms. Reid was right there.

I believed I would fail because I had spent my whole life believing I wasn't the kind of person who did physical things. I was smart. I was good at school. But I was clumsy and weak and I had never been good at sports. So obviously I couldn't learn to swim at sixteen. Obviously that ship had sailed.

The thing that changed was the goggles. Not because they were magical, but because buying them meant I was committed. I had spent money and showed up and put on the goggles even though I felt ridiculous. And then once I was in the water, it turned out I wasn't failing. I was just learning. I was just slow at learning, but learning and failing aren't the same thing.

By the end of the summer, I could swim a mile. Not fast, but continuously. The goggles fit better now. My technique improved. I stopped looking like someone who was terrified and started looking like someone who was just swimming.

I keep that photograph on my bulletin board because I want to remember that moment when I thought I couldn't do something and then I did it. I want to remember the goggles that were too big. I want to remember Ms. Reid not making it dramatic, just holding out her hand and pulling me in.

The person I became after learning to swim was the same person as before. But I stopped believing that some things were simply impossible for me. I stopped assuming my limitations before I tried. That shift changed more than just my swimming.
