There's a cheap USB microphone sitting on my desk right now, the little foam windscreen gone gray with dust, and it recorded exactly four episodes of a podcast almost nobody heard before I quietly gave up on the whole thing.

I started it in February of last year with my friend Dario, an interview show about kids at our school with weird part-time jobs — a guy who trained falcons for a bird-control company, a girl who did voice acting for indie video games, that kind of thing. We had this whole plan. Weekly episodes, a real release schedule, maybe eventually some kind of local advertiser if we got good enough. Episode one took us six hours to record and edit for something that ended up eleven minutes long, mostly because neither of us knew what we were doing and we re-recorded the intro probably a dozen times trying to sound like actual radio people instead of two seventeen-year-olds in a bedroom.

Four episodes in, over about five weeks, we stopped. Not dramatically — no fight, no announcement, just a fifth week where neither of us scheduled a guest, and then a sixth, and eventually it was just quietly over, the way a lot of things end without anybody deciding out loud that they're ending.

Here's the thing about the microphone, though. Before every single one of those four episodes, I did the same small ritual, and I didn't even notice it was a ritual until later. I'd unwrap the cable, plug it in, tap the mic three times to check the levels, and then — every time, no exception — I'd say, quietly, just to myself, "okay, don't mess this up," before I hit record. Four episodes, four times, same exact phrase, not because I planned it but because apparently that's just what came out of my mouth when I got nervous.

The podcast itself didn't go anywhere. I'm not going to pretend it did. Maybe forty people ever listened to any given episode, mostly people who already knew us. But that little phrase, that tap-tap-tap and "don't mess this up," turned out to matter more than the show did, because I've caught myself doing some version of it ever since, in completely different situations — before a debate round, before walking into a college interview I did over video call in the fall, even once before asking a question in a lecture hall full of people at a summer program. Some small physical check, some quiet phrase to myself, right before the moment I'm nervous about.

I didn't build that habit on purpose. I built a podcast on purpose, and the podcast mostly failed, four episodes and done, microphone gathering dust on a desk that used to have ambitions attached to it. But somewhere in those four failed attempts at radio stardom, I picked up a small ritual for managing nerves that has outlasted the actual project by over a year now, still useful, still there when I need it, completely by accident. I think about that a lot — how the thing you're actually trying to build isn't always the thing you walk away with, and sometimes the real result was hiding in a habit you didn't even notice you were forming while you were busy failing at something bigger.
