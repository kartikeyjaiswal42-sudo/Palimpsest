I started collecting rainfall data in our backyard the summer before eighth grade, using a plastic gauge my uncle gave me as a joke gift, and three years later I'm still doing it, mostly because of a mistake I made in the first six months that I still haven't fully let myself off the hook for.

What captivates me about it, honestly, is hard to explain to people who ask why anyone would care about measuring rain in one specific backyard for years on end. It's not really about the rain itself. It's the accumulation — watching a number that means almost nothing on any single day slowly turn, over months and then years, into a shape you can actually see, a pattern that tells you something true about where you live that you couldn't have known any faster. I check the gauge most mornings before school, log the number in a spreadsheet, and some days that's the only quiet ten minutes I get before the day starts moving too fast to notice anything.

The mistake happened in that first autumn, and it's small, which is part of why it still bothers me. I missed four days in October, a work trip with my family that I hadn't planned around, and rather than leave the gap honest and empty in my log, I estimated. I looked at the regional weather station's public data online, which recorded rainfall about six miles from our house, and I filled in my four missing days with numbers adjusted slightly to account for the distance, presenting them in my own spreadsheet exactly the same way as every measurement I'd actually taken myself, with no note, no flag, nothing to distinguish real data from a guess dressed up as real data.

I told myself at the time this was reasonable, even careful — I wasn't inventing numbers out of nothing, I was extrapolating from a nearby, credible source. But six miles matters more than I wanted to admit, especially with the kind of scattered, localized storms that come through our area in fall, and I know now, from three years of watching my own gauge diverge from that same regional station on plenty of ordinary days, that those four days could easily have been badly wrong, and I would never know, because I never marked them as anything other than genuine.

I didn't discover this failure through some dramatic reckoning. I just kept collecting data, month after month, and at some point in year two, looking back over the full spreadsheet for a school project, I found those four entries again and felt a small, specific discomfort I hadn't expected, seeing them sitting there indistinguishable from three years of real measurements, quietly corrupting a record I'd otherwise built entirely on my own careful mornings with a plastic gauge.

I never fixed it, not exactly. I can't go back and un-invent four days from three years ago. What I did instead was start flagging every gap since then, honestly, in red text in the spreadsheet, rather than quietly patching it with someone else's approximate number. It's a small correction for a small mistake, and probably nobody but me will ever look closely enough at that spreadsheet to care about four red cells buried in year one. But I care, and I think that's actually the real reason I've kept doing this for three years past the point any reasonable person would call it a finished project — not because I love rain, particularly, but because I owe that spreadsheet an honesty I failed to give it once, and the only way I know how to pay that back is one accurately measured morning at a time.
