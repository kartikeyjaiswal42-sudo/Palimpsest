The shoulder went in February. Not dramatically. There was no moment where something tore and I screamed and a coach came running with ice. It just started hurting in the last twenty-five of the 200 fly, and then it started hurting in the first twenty-five too, and then it hurt when I reached for a mug on the top shelf.

I told nobody for eleven weeks.

That is the part I got wrong. Everything else that happened — the MRI, the labral tear, the surgeon saying the word irreparable about a piece of me that I had been assuming was permanent — all of that was luck, or genetics, or eleven years of a stroke that is bad for shoulders. The eleven weeks were mine.

Why did I do it? I have given about six different answers to that question depending on who is asking. The one I gave my mother was that I didn't want to worry her. The one I gave my coach was that I thought it was just tightness. Neither is true, or they're true the way a nice photograph is true.

Here's the real one. County trials were in April. I was ranked fourth in the age group and the third-place girl had moved to Leeds. I could see the whole thing — the qualifying time, the lane, the way people would say my name at the club dinner. And I had worked out, without ever letting myself finish the thought, that a shoulder which hurt could probably get me through April, and that after April I could deal with it.

So I swam on it. Six sessions a week. I changed my catch slightly to take pressure off, which any physio will tell you is how you turn one injury into two, and I started icing in the car so nobody saw.

I did not make the time in April. I was a second and a half off. And then in May a doctor put my arm through a range of motion and I heard myself make a noise I did not recognise.

What did I do next. That's the prompt, and I've noticed that the honest answer is boring. I did not find a new sport and become excellent at it. I did the rehab, badly at first, and I cried in the changing rooms at Fenham Leisure Centre more than once, and I quit the club in September because standing on a poolside smelling chlorine and not getting in was worse than not going.

The thing I actually did, the thing I'd want somebody to know about me, is smaller. I started telling people when something hurt.

That sounds like nothing. It isn't nothing. I now coach the eight-to-tens on Saturday mornings, and there is a boy called Rafi who is very fast and very stubborn and who told me in October that his knee was fine. I knew the exact tone of voice. I'd used it. I sat him out for two weeks and he hated me and his knee is fine now.

I still think about February. Not the surgery — February, and the eleven weeks, and how easy it was to lie to a room full of people who were paying attention to me. I don't think I've fully stopped being that person. I just watch for her now.
