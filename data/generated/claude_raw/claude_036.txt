The object is a plastic pill organizer, the long kind with four compartments for every day of the week, twenty-eight little lids in a row. It is pale blue and one of the Wednesday lids has been broken since 2021 and is held shut with a piece of medical tape that I replace when it goes grey.

My brother Anwar has Crohn's disease. He was diagnosed when he was nine and I was thirteen, and the organizer arrived in our house about two weeks after that, from the hospital pharmacy, along with a printed schedule that nobody could read because it was in eight-point type and used the chemical names instead of the names on the boxes.

What I taught myself was how to fill it. That sounds small. It was not small. There were six medications and they did not behave the same way — one had to be taken with food, one had to be taken two hours away from the one with food, one was a half tablet that had to be cut and the cutter always crushed the edge so you lost a little each time, one changed dose depending on what his last blood test said, which meant the whole grid rebuilt itself every six weeks.

Nobody taught me. My mother works nights. The nurse showed us once, quickly, in a corridor, and then we were on our own with a plastic box and a bad printout.

So I made mistakes. I put the evening dose in the morning slot for four days once and did not notice until Anwar said he felt strange after breakfast. I cried in the bathroom about that and then I went back out and I rebuilt the whole week from scratch, and after that I started doing it the same way every Sunday: all four rows empty on the table first, bottles lined up left to right in the order of the printout, and I say each one out loud as it goes in. Out loud is the part that fixed it. Saying it makes you slower and slower is the entire trick.

What learning it without a teacher showed me is that I trust systems more than I trust myself, and that this is not a weakness. I used to think being good at something meant not needing the checklist. Now I think the checklist is the skill. Anyone can be careful when they are rested. The question is whether you are still correct at eleven at night when you have a test in the morning and your brother is in pain in the next room and you want very badly to just get it done.

The organizer is on our kitchen shelf. It is scratched and the Wednesday lid is taped and the blue has gone chalky where the sun hits it. Anwar is fifteen now and fills it himself most weeks. I still check it. He knows I check it. Neither of us has ever said anything about that.
