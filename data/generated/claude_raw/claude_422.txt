I rode a bus for two hours every morning to get to school, and I spent three years convinced that this was evidence that my parents didn't care about me. The bus route was crowded and stopped at every corner and took a detour through neighborhoods that seemed to have nothing to do with the school. The drive straight there was twenty minutes. The bus made it an ordeal.

My parents had offered to drive me. They had offered to let me transfer to the closer school. I said no to both, which was the teenage equivalent of cutting off your nose to spite your face. Instead I took the bus and I was angry about it, this anger that was diffuse and didn't have a clear target but seemed to blame my parents for making me suffer for reasons I couldn't articulate.

The bus ride wasn't actually bad. It was just long. By sophomore year, I knew the driver's name. I knew the usual passengers, the ones who got on at the same stops every day. There was a guy in a maintenance uniform who always brought coffee. There was an elderly woman who read the same book for three months. There was a teenager who practiced violin in the back of the bus, which sounds obnoxious but was actually kind of beautiful.

I convinced myself that hating the bus ride was justified. Two hours a day that I could have spent sleeping, or doing homework, or literally anything else. But looking back, I don't think I actually hated the bus ride. I hated the idea of the bus ride, which is different. I hated what it represented, which was something about my family not being the kind of family where parents drove you to school, where you had a car, where things were easy.

One morning, six months into senior year, the bus broke down. We all got out in the rain and stood there while the driver called for a replacement. An older woman—the one with the book—stood next to me and said, "Well, this is inconvenient." And I said yes, and she said, "But it's not the worst thing, is it? At least we got farther than usual."

And something shifted. I realized I had spent three years riding this bus with all these people, building these small routines, and I had hated all of it without ever actually experiencing it. I had been too busy resenting the bus route to notice that the bus ride was fine. Boring sometimes, but fine.

I started actually being on the bus after that. I read. I talked to people. I started sitting by the window and watching the city unfold in a way I hadn't noticed before. There was a park that appeared around mile seven. A mural that changed every month. A building that was being demolished and rebuilt. None of this was new—it had all been there the whole time. I had just been too angry to see it.

I still think about the bus ride sometimes now. I think about how much time I wasted being resentful about something that was actually fine. The bus wasn't the problem. My assumption that difficulty meant something was wrong with my family was the problem.

When I got my license, I stopped taking the bus. But I never felt like I won. I felt like I had lost the opportunity to understand something, which took me a while to articulate. Which was that sometimes we make ourselves suffer by refusing to accept the shape our lives actually take. We convince ourselves that if things are hard, we're being victimized. We don't notice that hard and fine can be the same thing.

The bus ride taught me that, eventually. Took me three years to learn it, but it's one of those lessons I actually remember now.
