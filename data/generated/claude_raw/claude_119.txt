My parents opened the restaurant twenty-two years ago, before I was born, and it closed this past spring, and in between those two facts is basically the whole shape of my childhood. I grew up doing homework at a corner table while the dinner rush went on around me, learning to fold napkins before I learned to ride a bike, knowing the regulars by their orders before I knew most of their names. It closed because the lease wasn't renewed and the numbers had been getting harder every year since the highway construction cut off half our street parking, not because of any one dramatic thing. It just stopped being possible to keep it open.

The habit I want to write about is small enough that I never thought of it as anything until the last few months, when I started noticing it because I knew I was about to lose it. Every night at closing, once the last table had paid and left, it was my job to flip the sign in the front window from open to closed. I started doing this around when I was twelve, tall enough finally to reach it without a stool, and I did it almost every night we were open for the next five years, unless I was sick or away.

It's such a small thing that I'm almost embarrassed to build an essay around it. But here's what I noticed once it started ending: the regulars who lived on our block used to watch for that sign. Mr. Delgado, who ate there probably four nights a week for as long as I can remember, told me once, near the very end, that he used to time his evening walk so he'd pass our window right around when the sign flipped, because it meant the night was properly over and he could go home. I didn't know that until he told me. I'd been doing the same small motion for five years without knowing that anyone outside my own family was paying attention to it, that it meant something to someone who wasn't me.

On the actual last night, I flipped the sign for the final time, and my mother asked if she could do it instead, and I let her, and I think that was the right call even though I'd been the one doing it for years. It felt like it belonged to all of us by then, not just to whoever's hand happened to be on it. A few regulars stood outside on the sidewalk that night, which they didn't usually do, just to watch it happen one more time.

I think what this taught me about community is something I wouldn't have understood if I'd only ever thought about the restaurant in terms of the big things, the menu, the reviews, whether we made rent that month. The actual community was built out of something as small as a sign getting flipped at the same rough time most nights, reliably enough that people planned their evenings around it without ever mentioning it to me. I was part of that community not because I was especially good at anything in the restaurant, I wasn't a great server and I was a mediocre cook at best, but because I showed up and did the same small task consistently enough that other people could build a small piece of their own routine around mine.

I don't know exactly what I'll do with that now that the restaurant is gone. But I know I want to find something else worth doing reliably enough that someone else notices only once it stops.
