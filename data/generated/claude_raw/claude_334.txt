I keep bees on my school's rooftop. Two hives, small ones. They sit behind the HVAC equipment, tucked into a corner where no one really goes except me, every week, to check on them.

I got permission from the principal two years ago. It wasn't hard to convince her. Everyone wants to seem forward-thinking about sustainability. Urban beekeeping is a thing people approve of in theory. I bought the bees, bought the equipment, read a dozen books about their care, and started a small project that I thought would be straightforward. Bees are simple, I believed. They do their work. You just need to give them space.

But they're also fragile in ways I didn't anticipate, and my rooftop beekeeping has become a study in smallness and failure.

The first hive died in my second year. I didn't make a big mistake. That's what kills you about it. I followed the protocols. I fed them what I was supposed to feed them. I checked on them when I was supposed to check. But something went wrong anyway. A disease. A parasite. Something I couldn't see until it was too late. One week the hive was humming with life, and three weeks later it was empty except for the few hundred bees that had abandoned it.

I had to call the beekeeper who sold me the colony and admit what had happened. She was kind about it, which somehow made it worse. She said it happens to everyone. That failure is part of beekeeping. That some losses are beyond your control.

I've spent the two years since then trying to understand what I could have controlled and what I couldn't. There's a small philosophy that comes with keeping bees. You're working with creatures you don't entirely understand, in an ecosystem you're only partially aware of, trying to maintain a balance that's fragile and complex. You can do everything right and still lose.

The remaining hive is strong. It's grown. This past spring, I harvested enough honey to fill four small jars. It was golden and perfect and it tasted like the flowers that grow in the neighborhood around the school. I gave jars to my family, to the principal, to my biology teacher. Each jar was a success, a small proof that the work matters.

But I think about the lost colony more than I think about the successful one. I think about what I missed, what I didn't see, what I should have known. Every beekeeper I've talked to says this is normal, that learning beekeeping is learning to accept loss as part of the process.

The rhythm of checking on the bees has become meditative for me. I go up there every Saturday morning before school, and I spend time listening to them, watching their behavior, making small notes about what I see. I can't always tell if what I'm noticing is important or not. I've learned to live with uncertainty. To do the work without needing to understand it completely. To show up and pay attention and accept that sometimes things fail despite attention.

It's taught me something that I don't think I would have learned otherwise. That good intentions and careful work aren't guarantees. That sometimes you fail and it's not anyone's fault. That there's value in continuing to try even after failure, in showing up to the rooftop every Saturday knowing that loss is possible, that bees are vulnerable, that I don't have complete control.

Most people don't see my bees. They don't notice the hives or think about what's happening up there. But I do. And in that small, repeated act of attention, in the weekly climb to the rooftop to check on something I can't fully protect, I've found something that matters.
