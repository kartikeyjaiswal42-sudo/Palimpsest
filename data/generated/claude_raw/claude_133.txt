Every other Saturday at 7 a.m., I stand in Fox Run in hip waders two sizes too big, next to a retired chemistry teacher, a dairy farmer, and a man named Gene who has tested this creek's dissolved oxygen levels every month for eleven years and keeps every result in a green three-ring binder he will not let anyone photograph. That's the Fox Run Watershed Alliance. There are nine of us. I am the only one under fifty, and for the first four months I mostly held things and wrote down numbers other people called out to me.

I earned my place slowly. I learned titration in Gene's garage because the county extension office training happened on a school day. I built a spreadsheet that finally let the group compare five years of nitrate readings against rainfall data, which nobody had bothered to do before because nobody else there uses spreadsheets for fun. By spring, Gene handed me the clipboard without being asked, which in that group is close to a promotion. My place in that community is the person who turns other people's careful, patient fieldwork into numbers that actually mean something, and I take that seriously.

My science fair project used our own data, plus new samples I collected downstream of Riverside Poultry, the plant where my dad has worked for twenty-two years. The nitrate levels there are consistently the highest on the whole creek, high enough that I couldn't honestly leave it out of the project even though I knew exactly what that would mean at my own dinner table.

My dad is proud of that job. It put both my sister and me through braces and is the reason we have health insurance, and he has never once complained about the hours. When I told him what my numbers showed, he didn't yell. He just got quiet in a way that felt worse, and asked if I really had to name the plant specifically, or if I could say "an agricultural facility" instead, something vague and deniable. I said no. I said the whole point of the Alliance's work is that the data means nothing if you round off the parts that are inconvenient.

We have not resolved this. He came to my presentation, stood in the back, and told me afterward that he was proud of me and also that he wished I hadn't used the plant's actual name, both in the same sentence, like he hadn't noticed the contradiction or didn't know what to do with it. I don't think he's wrong to worry — people at his job have asked him about it, and he doesn't love explaining his daughter's science project to his coworkers. I don't think I'm wrong either. I love him, and I still turned in the project exactly as the numbers told me to.

Being part of the Alliance taught me that a community isn't only people who agree with each other. Gene once told me the creek doesn't care what any of us want it to say. I think about that a lot now, mostly at dinner, where my dad and I have quietly agreed not to bring it up again, which is not at all the same thing as agreeing.
