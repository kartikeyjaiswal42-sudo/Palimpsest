I made him coffee every morning even though he couldn't taste it anymore, which is how I know something fundamental about myself had shifted.

He came home from the hospital on a Tuesday with half his body recovered and half of it still not talking to him. The stroke had happened fast—early morning, my mother finding him confused and unable to move his left side. The neurologists were optimistic. He'd regain function. Time and physical therapy would heal most of it. The rest he'd have to accept.

The coffee became the thing I did while he was learning to use his body again. I'd wake up early, make it the way he'd always liked it, bring it to him while he was sitting in his chair trying to get his hand to close on the mug. Half the time, the coffee would spill because his grip wasn't there yet. I'd clean it up and make another cup.

I told myself I was doing it to help him practice using his hands. To build strength through repetition. That's what the physical therapist recommended, after all. Repetition. Practicing the small movements over and over until the pathways rebuilt.

But that wasn't why I was really doing it. I was doing it because my father couldn't do it anymore, and I needed to fix something. He was unfixable. The left side of his face was still slightly slack. His left arm would never be quite right. His speech had returned but he got tired easily and his words came slower. These were permanent. But I could make coffee. I could try to make the small thing work.

By week three, I realized I'd never noticed him making coffee before. It was just something that happened. He'd wake up before everyone else and the coffee would be made when we came downstairs. I'd taken it for granted. Now that it was gone, I understood it had been his daily small act of care for all of us. Making coffee was how he started the day. It was his ritual.

So I took it over. Every morning, even after my mother started making coffee again, even after he eventually recovered enough to make it himself. Every morning, I'd wake up and make his coffee exactly the way he used to, in the order he used to, at the time he used to. It became my ritual now. It became the way I told him that I'd noticed, that I understood what he'd been doing.

He never acknowledged that I was doing this. Or he did, but indirectly. He'd sit in his chair and wait for the coffee. He'd accept it from me when I brought it to him. After a few months, his hand could hold it without spilling. After six months, he could make it himself. But I kept making it anyway. Every single morning.

My mother asked once why I kept doing it if he could do it now. She meant well. She was pointing out that the help wasn't needed anymore, that I could go back to sleeping an extra twenty minutes. I didn't answer her. How could I explain that it wasn't about functionality? That it had become about something else entirely?
