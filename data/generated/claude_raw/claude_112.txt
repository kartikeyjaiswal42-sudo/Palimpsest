In eighth grade a boy named Tomas transferred into our school and he was deaf, and I learned sign language for him, and I have spent four years slowly understanding that I got the whole thing wrong.

I want to describe the wrongness accurately, because the story sounds good if you stop halfway. Here is the good-sounding half: I taught myself Indian Sign Language, badly at first, from videos and from a deaf woman's channel and from a photocopied dictionary I found at a library in Karol Bagh, an hour and a half of practice most nights for about eight months, until I could hold a real conversation. All of that is true. It is also the part that gets me a compliment when I tell it, which is why I no longer tell it that way.

The wrongness is this. I never asked him.

I decided, in the first week, that the reason Tomas ate lunch alone was that nobody could talk to him, and I decided that I would be the solution to this, and I began. I did not ask what he wanted. I did not ask whether he would rather I helped the class learn ten signs each than have one person learn four hundred. I did not ask whether he liked the interpreter he already had, or whether he was in fact lonely at all, or whether the thing he most needed was somebody to sit down without a project.

For eight months I practised at him. That is the phrase that arrived in my head at some point in tenth grade and has not left. Practised at him.

I found out what he actually thought in December of that first year, and not from him. His sister said it, in passing, at a school thing — she said, half laughing, that Tomas said I signed like a person reading a manual, and that he mostly went along with it because I was clearly having a very good time.

I was fourteen and I was humiliated for about a week, and then I did the thing I still think was correct, which was that I went to him and asked, four months late, what he wanted. It was an awkward conversation and my signing was not good enough for it and we ended up typing half of it on my phone.

What he wanted, it turned out, was for people to stop looking at his interpreter when they spoke to him. That was the main one. It was not a thing that required anyone to learn a language. It required people to move their eyes about fifteen degrees.

Tomas and I are still friends. My signing is much better now, and I use it, and I do not regret learning it. What I regret is the shape of the thinking — that I built a large private solution to a problem I had assumed the existence of, because assuming was faster and, if I am honest, because the solution was more flattering to me than asking would have been.

I still catch myself doing it. I caught myself doing it last month with my grandmother and her phone. I stopped, and asked, and the answer was not what I had built.
