I taught myself how to manage five hours of sleep and still function, which is something I've learned that my mother thinks is a moral failure.

It started because I needed money. College is coming, and my parents don't have much saved. I started looking for jobs junior year, expecting something like retail or food service, the standard teenage work. But the pay was terrible. Then I found the distribution warehouse posting: eleven dollars an hour, night shifts, ten p.m. to six a.m., plus the option to pick up extra hours.

Eleven dollars an hour was almost double what I'd earn at Target. I applied immediately.

My mother found out after I'd already been working there for a month. I'd been managing it okay—going to school, coming home, sleeping a few hours before work, working the night shift, coming home exhausted but functional. I had a system. It wasn't ideal, but it worked.

She was furious. Not just disappointed, furious. She said I was sacrificing my health, my education, my entire future for money that I didn't need that badly. She said my job was to be a student, to focus on school and college, and that working night shifts was a distraction that would tank my GPA. She said it was dangerous for a teenager to be driving home at 6 a.m. on the highway. She said I was being irresponsible.

I tried to explain that I understood the risks. I was getting decent grades. I'd learned to manage the sleep deprivation. I was earning money that I actually needed. I wasn't doing this for luxury or extra spending money—I was doing this because I wanted to go to college without my parents being crushed by debt, and this job made that more possible.

She didn't accept it. She said I should be enjoying being seventeen, not spending it exhausted. She said there were other ways to earn money that weren't night shift work. She said the warehouse was going to burn me out and I'd regret it.

We never actually resolved it. She eventually stopped actively fighting me about it, but there's still this tension. She disapproves but has accepted that she can't control it. When I come home from work tired, she gives me a look. When I mention that I'm managing on not much sleep, she says something about how that's not sustainable. She worries in this silent, constant way that makes me feel like I've disappointed her.

What I've learned, though, and what I haven't been able to articulate to her, is that this job has taught me something about work and survival that I don't think I would have learned any other way. I've learned that I'm capable of more than I thought. I've learned that you can do hard things that aren't fun or fulfilling because they need to be done. I've learned the specific economy of my own exhaustion—what I can still do well while tired, what I need to protect, how to prioritize.

I've also learned a lot about the world that night shift workers inhabit. The other people at the warehouse—my coworkers—are mostly people my mother would never encounter. Older guys trying to support families on warehouse wages, immigrants sending money back home, people cycling through because they have nowhere else to go. I've learned their names, their stories, what they're trying to do with their lives. I've learned that the gap between my world and their world is just luck. I'll go to college and get out of the warehouse. They probably won't, not because they're less capable but because they never had the choice I'm having.

That's something my mother doesn't see. She sees the night shift as dangerous and unnecessary. I see it as an education in how the world actually works, not how it works for people like us.

We still haven't talked about it directly. There's this unresolved frustration between us. She thinks I'm being reckless and self-destructive. I think she's being privileged about what she thinks is acceptable work for a teenager. Neither of us has managed to explain it to the other in a way that lands.

I'm going to quit after graduation. I'll go to college and the night shifts will end. In a way, my mother will have won that argument, even though she never actually convinced me. But I'll leave the warehouse knowing something about myself and about the world that I wouldn't have known if I'd been smarter about my future, more careful about my sleep, more obedient to what my mother needed me to be.
