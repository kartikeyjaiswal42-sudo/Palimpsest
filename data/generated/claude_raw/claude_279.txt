The object is a yellow legal pad, half used up, sitting in my desk drawer with about sixty pages of lists on it in three different pens. My aunt gave it to me in February and said, more or less, "you're organized, you handle the guest count," and by April I was handling a lot more than the guest count, because my cousin Reema was planning her wedding from another time zone and my aunt was working double shifts most of that spring.

I didn't ask for the job. It just landed on me because I was the one who answered the family group chat fastest and because I already kept lists for everything — practice schedules, homework, my own life — so it seemed natural that I'd keep the wedding's lists too. What I actually did, most days, was small. Every night before bed, for about four months, I opened that legal pad and went through the same three things: who had confirmed for the ceremony, who still hadn't paid their deposit for the shared hotel block, and what the caterer's headcount said compared to what my list said. Ten minutes, most nights. Some nights nothing had changed and I just wrote the date and a line saying so.

I almost stopped doing it in June, about three weeks out, because it felt pointless by then — the big decisions were made, the dress was ordered, the hall was booked, and checking the same three columns every night felt like busywork I kept out of habit more than necessity. My mom actually told me I could ease off. I kept doing it anyway, mostly because stopping felt like it would jinx something, which isn't a real reason but was the only one I had.

Two weeks before the wedding, the caterer's number and my number didn't match. The caterer had 142. My list, updated that same night, had 149 — seven guests who had confirmed through a cousin's side of the family that never made it into the official spreadsheet my aunt was using, because they'd RSVP'd directly to me in the group chat instead of through the form. If I hadn't been checking that column every single night, out of a habit that had stopped feeling useful weeks earlier, that gap wouldn't have surfaced until the day of, with seven relatives showing up to a headcount that didn't include them.

My aunt called the caterer the next morning and fixed it without much drama, and the wedding happened the way weddings are supposed to happen, which is to say almost nobody there ever knew there'd been a problem at all. I didn't get thanked for it specifically, not in any big way — my aunt said "good thing you caught that" and moved on to the next fire, because there were always more fires that month.

I kept the legal pad because it's the most boring object I own and also the only proof that a habit I almost quit out of pure tedium turned out to be the one thing standing between a wedding and a very bad Saturday. I don't know what I'll use next time I'm handed something that matters. Probably still a legal pad. Probably still checking it every night, long after it stops feeling necessary.
