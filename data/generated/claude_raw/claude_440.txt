When my mother told us she was going back to university, I was thirteen and I was embarrassed. I would like to have a better opening sentence than that, but it is the true one and everything else follows from it.

She was forty. She had left school at seventeen to work in a chemist's, and had done the books for my uncle's building firm for eleven years after that, and she was going to study nursing at the university across the city, which meant three years, which meant she would qualify at forty-three. The embarrassment was not about her. It was about what I imagined other people would think, which at thirteen is a distinction you are not capable of drawing. I remember worrying that somebody's mother would ask my mother what she did and my mother would have to say student.

For the first year I was quietly, consistently unhelpful. Not cruel, I hope. But I made it clear in small ways that this was an inconvenience. I would sigh when there was no dinner because she had a placement. When she asked me to test her on the cardiovascular system I did it in a flat voice, quickly, so that she knew it was a favour. Once, and this is the one I would take back, I said that it was a bit late to be doing all this. She was at the table with her folders. She said yes, probably, and went back to reading.

The moment it turned was in the November of her second year and it was not a conversation. It was one in the morning and I came downstairs for water and she was asleep at the kitchen table with her head on a pharmacology textbook, and there were flashcards laid out in front of her in rows, and I stood in the doorway for a while looking at them.

What got me was the handwriting. Her flashcards were written in exactly the same handwriting as the notes she used to put in my lunchbox, and as the labels on the boxes in the loft, and as the shopping list on the fridge. It was the handwriting of a person doing an ordinary domestic task. And she was using it to write out the mechanism of beta blockers, over and over, at one in the morning, at forty-one years old, because she had failed the pharmacology paper in June and had not told us.

I had genuinely never considered that she might be frightened. I had been treating the whole thing as an indulgence — something she wanted, that the rest of us were absorbing the cost of. Standing in that doorway I understood, in a way I could not have been argued into, that it was the opposite: it was the most exposed thing anyone in our family had done in years. She was doing it in front of people half her age who had all of it already in their heads. She had failed something and got up the next morning and carried on and had not mentioned it, because she had four other people in the house who she thought would take it as evidence.

I did not say anything to her that night. I put the flashcards into a pile, which she told me later she had found annoying because they were in an order. In the morning I asked her about beta blockers and she explained them for twenty minutes while I ate cereal, and she was very good at explaining, and I have never forgotten what a beta blocker does.

She qualified in the July I turned sixteen. There is a photograph of her in the gown looking mildly ridiculous and completely certain. She works nights on a respiratory ward now and comes home at eight in the morning and sleeps until three.

What I took from it is not the obvious thing about hard work. It is that I had a picture of my mother that had been assembled entirely when I was small, and I had gone on maintaining it out of habit long after it stopped matching the person in the kitchen. I try now to notice when I am doing that with people. I do not always manage it. But I know what it looks like from the doorway.
