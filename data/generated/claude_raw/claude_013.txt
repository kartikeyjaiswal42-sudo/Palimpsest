The object is a swim cap, navy blue, still faintly smelling of chlorine even though it hasn't touched a pool in fourteen months. I keep it in the top drawer of my desk, underneath a stack of loose pens, where I have to move things aside to get to it, which I think is intentional on my part even if I've never admitted that out loud until right now.

My shoulder gave out during a 200 fly at Sectionals the winter of my sophomore year. Not gave out like a dramatic pop, nothing you'd see in a movie — it was more like something inside just quietly stopped agreeing to do what I asked of it, mid-stroke, and I finished the race anyway because that's what you do, and then I couldn't lift my arm above my head for six weeks. The diagnosis had a long name involving my labrum that I've since memorized the way you memorize the name of a person who hurt you. Surgery, then months of a physical therapist named Deb pushing my arm through ranges of motion while I stared at the ceiling tiles and did math in my head to keep from crying. The short version is that the shoulder healed enough for regular life and nowhere near enough for competitive butterfly, and a doctor with kind eyes told me that at some point, the eleven years I'd spent in a pool were, medically speaking, over.

My father did not agree with this. He still doesn't, not really, not all the way, and this is the part I haven't figured out how to write about without it sounding like I'm blaming him, because I'm not, or I don't think I am.

He was my first coach. Before club teams, before anyone else timed me with anything more official than a kitchen stopwatch, it was him at the edge of our local pool at six in the morning, before his own workday started, and swimming was the thing we built between us instead of whatever fathers and daughters usually build. When the doctor said what she said, my dad's first instinct wasn't comfort. It was research. He found three more specialists. He asked about experimental treatments. He wanted a second surgery, a third opinion, a path back into the water that didn't involve the word "over."

I didn't want any of that. I wanted to be done, and I wanted him to let me be done, and those two things turned out to be much harder to say to him than I expected.

We had the fight — if you can call it a fight, since neither of us raised our voice, we just got quieter and quieter until the silence itself was the argument — in the car outside Deb's office. He said I was giving up too early. I said I wasn't giving anything up, I was choosing something, and those aren't the same, and he said choosing to quit is still quitting, and I said maybe quitting isn't always the wrong word for a thing.

We never really finished that conversation. We just stopped having it, the way you stop touching a bruise once you've learned exactly where it is. He still brings up swimming sometimes, carefully, like he's testing whether the door is unlocked. I still change the subject, carefully, like I'm not entirely sure it isn't.

I don't know if he thinks I resent him. I don't, not exactly, though I did for a while. What I think now, holding this cap that doesn't smell like much of anything anymore if I'm honest, chlorine doesn't last fourteen months, maybe I'm imagining it — what I think is that he loved the sport for both of us, and I only ever loved it for as long as my body agreed to let me, and neither of us has found the sentence yet that explains that difference in a way the other one can actually hear.
