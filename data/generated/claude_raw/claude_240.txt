The article ran on page three of the November issue, under a headline I had argued for in a meeting: "Where Does the Activity Fee Actually Go?" I had spent six weeks on it. I had pulled the budget summaries the school posts every spring, made a spreadsheet, and found that the line item labeled "student programming" had shrunk by nineteen percent over four years while the fee itself had risen twice. I quoted two students who said they had never seen a dollar of it. I did not quote anyone from the administration, because the assistant principal did not answer my second email and I decided that silence was itself a kind of answer.

Three days later I was sitting across from her. She had a printout of my article with sections underlined in green pen, which somehow felt worse than red. She did not tell me I was wrong. She told me that the "student programming" line had been split into three lines in 2019, that two of those lines lived in a different section of the report, and that the total had gone up, not down. She slid a page across the desk. She was right. My nineteen percent was an artifact of a formatting change I had not thought to check.

What I remember most is not the embarrassment, though there was plenty of that. It is the thing I said next, which I am still not proud of. I said that the overall point still stood — that students did not know where the money went, and that the confusion in the report proved it. And she said, mildly, "That might be true. But that isn't what you wrote."

I went home and argued with her in my head for two days. I had a whole case. Journalism is adversarial. Institutions do not volunteer their failures. If I had waited for a comment I would have waited forever, and the fee would have gone up again in the meantime. I believed that the value of a story like mine was in the pressure it created, and that pressure was a blunt instrument by nature. Getting a number slightly wrong seemed like a rounding error against that.

The moment my mind actually changed was small and happened on a Tuesday. A sophomore I barely knew stopped me by the vending machines and said the article was great, that the school was stealing from us, and did I know if anyone was going to sue. He was genuinely angry. He had read my number and built something in his head on top of it, and my number was made of nothing. I had wanted to create pressure and I had. It was pointing in a direction I could no longer defend.

I asked for space in the December issue. Not a correction buried at the bottom — a full column, in my name, explaining the error, showing the two reports side by side, and then asking the question I should have asked in the first place: why is a public budget written so that a motivated seventeen-year-old with a spreadsheet gets it wrong? That question turned out to be a better story. The business office sent someone to talk to me for it. She was not hostile. She said the format was inherited from a state template nobody liked. The piece ended up longer and less exciting than the original and I think it was the first real thing I ever wrote.

I still believe institutions should be pushed. I have not become gentler about that. What I gave up was the idea that being right in general excuses being wrong in particular, that a good cause launders a bad fact. Now, when I finish a draft, I go back through and mark every sentence that is a number or a claim about someone else, and I make myself find where it came from. Most of the time it holds. The times it doesn't are the reason I do it.
