The place is a kitchen on the ground floor of my building, with a window that faces the bins and a table with a wipe-clean cloth on it patterned with lemons. It belongs to Mrs. Halloran, who is eighty-one and who I was sent to help because my school required forty hours of community service and my mother knew somebody who knew her son.

I want to be honest about how I arrived there, because the honest version is the only part of this that I think is worth anything. I did not volunteer out of goodness. I signed up because the form needed signing. The task was described to me as helping an elderly neighbour learn her new smartphone, which her son had bought her so that she could see her grandchildren on video, and which had been sitting in its box on the sideboard for five months.

For the first three visits I was a person performing helpfulness. I sat where I was told to sit, I made my voice patient in a way I could hear myself doing, and I said things like "no, the other button" while thinking about how many hours I had left. She was slow. She pressed the screen too hard, as if it were a doorbell. She apologised constantly, which made it worse, because then I had to say it was fine, and I was not sure it was fine, and lying takes energy.

The fourth visit is the one that changed it.

She had asked me to write things down, so I had made her a list — open the green icon, press the picture of your daughter, wait. She had the list in front of her and she was going through it correctly and it still wasn't working, and I leaned over to take the phone off her and she moved it away from me. Not sharply. She just moved it out of my reach and kept looking at her list.

I sat back. I let her do it. It took her four minutes to do a thing I could have done in six seconds, and in those four minutes I understood that everything I had been doing for three weeks had been for me. I had been solving the phone. She was not a phone.

After that the kitchen became a different room. I stopped narrating and started waiting. She told me about the shop she and her husband ran on Kilbride Street, and about a fire in 1974, and about how she had not liked her daughter's first husband and had said so, once, and had regretted saying it for eleven years. I stopped counting hours somewhere in October and did the paperwork wrong at the end because I genuinely could not remember how many I had done.

I don't have a large claim to make about this. I am not a naturally patient person and I am not sure I have become one. But that table, with the lemons on the cloth and the bins outside the window and a very old woman refusing to let me take the phone out of her hands, is the place where I stopped performing and could not tell you exactly when it happened. Everywhere else I am still a little bit doing an impression of myself. There, I wasn't.
