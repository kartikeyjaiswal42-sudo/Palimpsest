The turbidity tube lives in a shoebox under my bed now, but for eleven months it lived in my backpack, wrapped in a hand towel so the acrylic wouldn't crack against my textbooks. It's a clear plastic cylinder about sixty centimeters long, open at the top, with a small drain valve at the bottom and a black-and-white pattern — like a tiny archery target — printed on the inside floor. You pour creek water in until the pattern disappears from view, drain a little out, look again, and keep going until you can just barely make out the rings. Whatever depth the water sits at when the pattern reappears is your turbidity reading, and that one number, more than anything else I measured that year, is what kept me up until midnight in a county extension office that smelled like pond water and printer toner.

I got the tube from Mr. Halloran, who ran the water quality program out of the extension office two towns over and who I called so many times about Sugar Creek that his assistant started recognizing my voice. My project started as something small — I wanted to know whether the creek behind the Tractor Supply, the one that ran orange after storms, was actually worse off than the creek near my grandmother's, which ran clear even in August. I figured I'd take a few samples, make a chart, be done in a month. Instead I found myself standing in that creek every Saturday from September to July, sometimes in waders that let in water anyway, holding the tube up to the light and squinting at a target pattern like it was going to tell me something about my own life.

In a way it did. The tube doesn't care what you want the water to say. You can hope the numbers are good, the way I hoped every week that Sugar Creek would clear up, and the tube will still show you thirty-eight centimeters of visibility after a hard rain, which is bad, full stop, no matter how much you were rooting for the creek. I think that's what captivated me about the whole project — not water chemistry exactly, though I did fall in love with nitrate test strips and dissolved oxygen kits along the way, but the particular honesty of a measurement. A number from a stream doesn't perform for you. It doesn't care if your hypothesis was elegant. It just sits there in the tube, at whatever centimeter it landed on, and you either report it accurately or you're not really doing science anymore.

By spring I had four sites, forty-one weeks of readings, and a working theory that the runoff from a hog operation upstream, not the parking lot everyone blamed, was the real driver of the worst turbidity spikes — a theory Mr. Halloran initially doubted and then, after I showed him three storms in a row where the numbers only made sense his way, started taking seriously enough to mention to the county. That felt bigger than any ribbon the project could have won, and it did win a ribbon, though by the time regionals came around I cared much less about the placard than about whether site three was going to clear up before the fair even happened, which it did, barely, the morning of.

I still have the tube. I take it out sometimes, not because I need it for anything anymore, but because turning it in my hands still makes me want to know what the water looked like the week before, and the week before that. Mr. Halloran is the person I call when I don't understand a reading. The tube is the thing that taught me to trust a number over a hope, and I don't think I've stopped being the kind of person who'd rather wade into a cold creek in October than guess at an answer from the bank.
