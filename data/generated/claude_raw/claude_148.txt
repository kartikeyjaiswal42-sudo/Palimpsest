I taught myself bookkeeping, which is a boring answer, and I taught it to myself for a reason my father and I still cannot discuss without one of us leaving the room.

He was made redundant in March of my first year of secondary school. He is a structural engineer, a good one, and for the first eight months he behaved exactly the way you are supposed to: suit, applications, a spreadsheet of contacts. Then the eight months became two years, and the two years became the situation we live in now, and somewhere in there the spreadsheet stopped being updated. He does the shopping. He fixes things that are not broken. He has read more books in the last three years than I will read in my life.

What I taught myself, at fourteen, was how to keep the household accounts. Nobody asked me to. I found a free course, watched a man in Ohio explain double-entry to an audience of nobody, and started copying our bank statements into a ledger I built badly and rebuilt three times. Debits on the left. Every number has to have a twin somewhere else or you have made an error. I liked that. It was the only part of the house with a rule in it.

My father found out in the second month and we had the argument we are still having. He said, kindly, that it was not my job. I said that somebody had to know what the standing orders came to. He said that knowing did not change them. I said that not knowing did not change them either, and that at least this way when Mum asked whether we could do the school trip, the answer would be a number instead of a mood.

That was the sentence that did it, I think. He did not shout. He said, "You've made me a line item," and went into the garden.

I have thought about that for three years and I am not sure he was wrong. That is the part I cannot resolve. Learning something without a teacher means nobody is standing there to tell you when your method is technically correct and humanly stupid. The course taught me that every transaction has a counterpart. It did not teach me that when you put your own father into a ledger, in a column called income with a zero in it, you have described something true and something cruel at once, and that the truth does not cancel the cruelty out. There is no double entry for that.

I still keep the accounts. He still does not look at them, and I have stopped offering. Twice a year, when the insurance renews, he asks me what I think, and I tell him, and we both act as though it is a normal question between two adults, which is its own kind of agreement.

What learning it alone showed me is that a method will happily give you an answer to a question you should have thought harder about asking. A teacher might have caught that. A video from Ohio never will. I know how to close a month now. I do not know how to sit in a kitchen with somebody I love and say the true number out loud without it landing as an accusation, and I have started to suspect that is the harder skill, and that nobody teaches it either.
