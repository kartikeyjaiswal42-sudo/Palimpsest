My finger landed on the wrong key again, and Dadu didn't stop pumping the bellows, just kept the drone going underneath my mistake like it hadn't happened. "Again," he said, not unkindly, and I pressed the same key, wrong again, and felt my face go hot in the small front room where the harmonium lived under a yellowing cloth when nobody was playing it.

I had asked to learn only because my mother mentioned, offhand, that nobody in the family could play it anymore once Dadu was gone, and something about that sentence had lodged in me. The harmonium itself was older than my mother, wood gone dark at the corners where three generations of hands had rested, keys slightly yellowed, one of the reeds sticking so you had to strike it a little harder than the others to get it to sound. Dadu had learned it from his own father in a village I had visited exactly twice, and he played it every evening after his tea, songs I didn't know the names of, words in a language I understood maybe a third of.

For the first two months I hated the lessons. I was fourteen and used to being good at things quickly, or at least competent, and here I was fumbling through scales that Dadu's hands moved through without looking, his eyes half closed, his foot tapping a rhythm I couldn't feel yet. He never raised his voice. He also never told me I was doing well when I wasn't, which somehow felt worse than if he had yelled. I kept coming back anyway, some mixture of stubbornness and guilt, and I told myself I was doing it for my mother, or for some vague idea of heritage I couldn't have explained if you'd asked me directly.

Then one Sunday in March he asked me to accompany him instead of playing alone, just the drone, just holding a single note steady while he sang over it, and I realized halfway through that I wasn't thinking about my fingers anymore. I was listening to where his voice was going and trying to feel it a half-second before it arrived, the way you learn to anticipate a wave instead of getting knocked down by it. My note held. It wasn't much, one sustained pitch under an old song about a river, but something in me reorganized itself in that half hour, from a kid doing a chore out of guilt into someone actually inside the music, even at the most minor possible position within it.

I go to his house every Sunday now, not because my mother suggested it once and I felt bad, but because I've started to understand what the room sounds like when I'm not in it. My aunts and uncles drift in during our practice, half-listening while they cook or argue about something on the news, and my youngest cousin has started asking to sit next to me on the bench, watching my hands instead of Dadu's, which feels like a kind of inheritance I didn't expect to receive this early. Nobody assigned me a role in that room. I found one, in the space between a note I could barely hold and a song I couldn't yet sing, and I keep going back to hold it a little longer each time.
