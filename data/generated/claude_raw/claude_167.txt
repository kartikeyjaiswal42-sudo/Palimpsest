There's a shelf in the Fairview branch library, second from the end in the 813s, where I spent most of the summer after ninth grade. Not because I loved American fiction especially. Because I'd made a dumb bet with myself in June — read every single book on one shelf, start to finish, in order, no skipping — and I picked that one because it was at eye level and I was tired of scrolling through recommendation lists that all said the same five books.

It took me eleven weeks. Some of the books were incredible. Some were, frankly, terrible, dated in ways that made me wince, and I read them anyway because the rule was the rule. I got a library card fine for one I kept too long. I got a weird look from the same librarian, Ms. Petrakis, roughly every Tuesday, because I always came in around four and always left with the next book in sequence, like I was working through a syllabus nobody had assigned me.

Here's the thing I got wrong, and I still think about it more than I'd like to admit. I told everyone — my parents, my friend Iris, a college counselor who asked about my summer during a mock interview in October — that the project was about discipline. About proving to myself I could finish something boring for the sake of finishing it. That was the story I told because it sounded like the kind of thing that belongs in an essay like this one.

It wasn't really true. Or it was true, but it wasn't the reason.

The actual reason, which I only admitted to myself much later, on a bus somewhere around November, staring out the window instead of doing precalc homework, was that I didn't want to choose. Choosing a book felt like a small failure waiting to happen — what if I picked wrong, what if I wasted a week on something forgettable when I could have read something that mattered. The shelf took the choosing away from me. It let me read forty-one books without ever being responsible for a single one of those decisions.

I've turned this over so many times since. Was that cowardice, dressed up as discipline? Some nights I think yes, obviously, and I feel a little embarrassed about the whole thing, about presenting a fear of choosing as some admirable feat of willpower. Other nights I think it doesn't matter what the motive was, because the eleven weeks happened regardless, and I did read a novel by an author I'd never have picked on my own that ended up mattering to me more than almost anything else I read that year — a slim, strange book about a lighthouse keeper that I still reread every August.

What bothers me isn't which version is true. It's that I can't tell anymore, and I'm not sure the two versions are even separable — that the discipline and the fear were doing the same work from two different directions, and I only get to see the direction I choose to look from on a given day.

I mention all this not because I've resolved it. I bring it up because I think the resolving isn't really the point. What I actually learned, sitting on the floor of the 813s in July with a book I didn't especially want to be reading, is that I don't always know why I do the things I commit myself to, and that not knowing doesn't disqualify the commitment. I finished the shelf. I can't fully tell you why. I've stopped needing to.
