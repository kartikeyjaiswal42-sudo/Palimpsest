The filing cabinet was beige, three drawers, and it sat against the wall behind my desk at the internship for the entire six weeks. Someone had put a strip of masking tape across the top drawer and written SERVICE TICKETS 1998–2004 on it in marker. There was a dent in the bottom drawer at about shin height, the kind of dent a person makes with a foot when a drawer sticks. That cabinet is the only thing from that summer I could draw from memory.

I want to be honest about what the internship was. I was told to come in at nine. I was given a desk, a login that did not work for the first four days, and a supervisor who was genuinely busy and genuinely apologetic and who, when I asked for work, would say he'd get me something and then not. I sat there. I read the company website twice. By the end of the first week I had done about ninety minutes of actual assigned tasks, and most of that was moving chairs for a meeting.

The cabinet was the thing I looked at. Around day eight I opened it, mostly out of boredom, and found that it was full — genuinely full, jammed — with carbon-copy service tickets, handwritten, going back to the year before I was born. Customer name, address, what broke, what the technician did, how long it took. Nobody had touched them in a long time. There was dust on the top edge of the folders in a way that dust does not get in three years.

I asked if I could do something with them. My supervisor said sure, in the tone of someone agreeing to a thing he did not expect to be asked about again.

So I taught myself a spreadsheet. Not a spreadsheet the way I had used one in school, where you type numbers in boxes. I mean I had to figure out how to turn six thousand handwritten pieces of paper into something a person could ask a question of. I typed tickets in for two days before I realized my columns were wrong, and that I had been entering "burner assembly" and "burner assy" and "brnr assembly" as three different things, and that everything I had done was going to be useless unless I fixed it. I started over. I learned what a dropdown list is because I needed to stop myself from typing the same thing three ways. I learned pivot tables from a video, badly, and then from another video, less badly. I learned that a date typed as text does not sort, which took me an embarrassing afternoon.

By the last week I had about eleven hundred tickets in and I could show a chart of which part failed most often in which month. I showed my supervisor. He was interested for maybe four minutes, which is a real four minutes and more than I expected, and he asked me to email it to him. I do not know if he opened it.

What learning it without a teacher showed me is smaller than what I would like to claim. It is this: nobody was going to tell me my columns were wrong. In every class I have ever taken, being wrong is a thing that gets caught for you, usually within a week, usually in red. In front of that cabinet the only person who could catch it was me, and I caught it late, and starting over cost me two days I could not get back. I did not enjoy that. I am not sure I would have chosen it.

But I have noticed since that I check my own work differently now. I look for the version of the mistake I cannot see, the one where everything is running fine and the categories are quietly wrong. That is what the cabinet taught me, and it was not trying to teach me anything.
