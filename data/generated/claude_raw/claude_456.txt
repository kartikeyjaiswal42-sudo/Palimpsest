I carry a bobbin case in my pencil box. It is smaller than a bottle cap, nickel-coloured, with a little steel arm on one side and a screw the size of a grain of rice on the other. It came out of a Singer that my mother has had since before I was born, and it is the first thing I ever fixed. Nobody taught me how.

The machine started skipping and eating the thread in the winter of ninth standard. The mechanic in Sadar Bazaar takes three hundred rupees and does not explain anything; he turns the machine towards himself, does something for four minutes, and turns it back. I asked him once what had been wrong and he said the machine was old. That answer annoyed me for weeks, which is probably the real beginning of this.

So I opened it myself. I watched videos where the man's hands were in the way of the part he was naming, and I read a scanned manual in English I half understood, and mostly I took things out and put them back and wrote in a notebook what happened. For eleven days that machine did not sew. My mother was patient in a way I did not deserve.

The bobbin case is where I finally understood something. All the trouble was in that screw. It sets how hard the case grips the thread coming off the bobbin, and the whole stitch is a negotiation between that grip and the tension from above. Too loose and loops come up under the cloth. Too tight and the top thread gets pulled down and the seam puckers. There is a test where you hold the case by its thread and let it hang, and give it a small jerk, and it should slip down about an inch and stop. Nobody can tell you what an inch feels like in your fingers. You have to be wrong about it maybe forty times.

That is the first thing learning it alone showed me. There is a large gap between knowing the name of a part and knowing the part, and a teacher can only carry you across the first half of it. The second thing is that I learned to work backwards from the symptom. A tailor does not say the timing is off; he says it is making a knot on the underside, or the needle is breaking on thick cloth, and you have to translate that. With no one to ask, the only way to be sure was to change one thing at a time, which is a slow, boring discipline I did not have before. The third thing is less comfortable. I broke a needle plate on a neighbour's machine by forcing a screw, and I had to go tell her, and pay for it out of my tuition money. Learning without a teacher means the mistakes belong to you completely, and so do the repairs after them.

Since then I have worked most Sundays at Iqbal chacha's tailoring shop in our lane, not sewing, just servicing. Cleaning lint out of the feed dogs, oiling, setting hook timing on the two industrial machines, arguing with him about whether a wobbling handwheel matters. He pays me in samosas and in letting me take apart anything a customer has abandoned. I still keep the old bobbin case, even though the machine it belongs to has a new one now. When I am stuck on something that is not a machine, I have started asking the same question I asked that screw, which is: what exactly is this holding, and how tightly.
