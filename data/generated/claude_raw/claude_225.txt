The dough splits along the same seam for the third time, right at the pleat where I've pinched it closed, and a thin thread of khoya filling leaks out into the oil and turns instantly, furiously black. I fish the ruined gujiya out with a slotted spoon before it can scorch the rest of the batch and set it on a paper towel next to its two failed siblings, and I stand there in my grandmother's kitchen, which is now just my kitchen, holding a spoon and trying not to cry about fried dough.

My phone is propped against the flour tin, open to a recipe some woman in Ohio posted with exact measurements, two hundred and forty grams of maida, one hundred grams of ghee, a photograph of dumplings so uniform they look machine-stamped. I have made this recipe twice this week and both times it has come out wrong, not badly wrong, just wrong in a way I can taste but can't name, missing the specific density Dadi's always had, the way hers gave slightly under your teeth before the crunch. She never once, in eleven years of watching her make these before every Holi, wrote down a single measurement. She'd tell me a fistful of this, enough of that to feel right, and I used to think that was carelessness, honestly. I used to think, standing at her elbow at nine years old, that a woman who loved her family as much as she clearly did would have taken twenty minutes at some point to write the recipe down so that this exact moment, three years after she died, would not be happening to me.

I call my mother in from the living room to taste the black one's replacement, and she chews for a second too long before she says it's close, not it, but close, and something in her face when she says it makes me understand she isn't grading my cooking. She's remembering. She tells me, while I start pleating a new one, that Dadi learned this from her own mother in a kitchen in Meerut with no written recipe either, that she used to complain about the exact same thing I'm complaining about now, a fistful of this, enough of that, and that somewhere along the way she stopped complaining and started measuring with her hands instead of her eyes, the way you learn a language by living inside it instead of by memorizing a dictionary.

I had wanted, this whole week, to solve my grandmother like a problem, to reverse-engineer her into two hundred and forty grams of maida so that nothing about her would ever be at risk of disappearing again. I am realizing, with my hands full of dough that will not behave exactly like the Ohio woman's dough, that this was never going to work, and that maybe it was never supposed to. My mother takes the spoon from me and shows me, not tells me, how much khoya to use, pinching it between two fingers, and it is more than the recipe says and somehow obviously correct the second I see it in her hand.

By the fifth batch the pleats hold. They're not identical to Dadi's, a little thicker on one side, filling slightly off-center, and when my mother bites into one she doesn't say it's exactly right, she just goes quiet in the particular way she goes quiet when something has actually worked, and keeps eating standing up at the counter instead of sitting down, the way you do when you're not thinking about whether it's good, you're just eating it.
