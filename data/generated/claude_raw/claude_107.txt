The confirmation email says congratulations to Wren Castellan, and I read it twice before remembering that Wren Castellan is me, or is supposed to be, for the next six weeks until the state judges announce winners under whatever name gets typed onto the certificate.

Ms. Okafor found out three days before the deadline, when I asked her to look over the essay one more time and used the name by accident in my own note to her. She didn't say anything for a second. Then she asked why, and I told her the truth, which was that the essay was about my father's year in county jail and I did not want four hundred people at my school reading my last name next to that story in the district newsletter where winners always got printed. She said she understood the fear. She said she still thought it was the wrong call, that an essay this honest deserved to be claimed, not smuggled in under a name I'd invented in about eleven seconds at my kitchen table, and that hiding the byline would quietly change what I let myself write, whether I noticed it happening or not.

I disagreed with her, out loud, more firmly than I usually disagree with adults I actually respect. I told her that owning the words and owning the byline were two different things, that I could be completely honest on the page and still protect myself off it. I believed that when I said it. I sent the essay in as Wren Castellan and didn't think about her warning again until the acceptance email showed up using a name that wasn't mine.

I won second place. Not first, second, and I've never been able to decide if that fact is meaningful or if I'm only asking because it gives me something to blame besides myself. To collect the award I had to submit my actual legal name to the district office anyway, so the anonymity bought me exactly six weeks and then dissolved on its own, quietly, in a form I filled out in the front office during lunch. The newsletter ran it under my real name in the end. Everyone read it exactly the way I'd built an elaborate fake name to avoid.

What I keep circling back to isn't the exposure, which turned out to be nothing, a few kind comments in the hallway and nothing worse. It's a smaller thing, something I only noticed rereading the essay months later. There's a paragraph near the end, about visiting him, that I'd softened without registering I was doing it, made a little cleaner and a little more forgivable than the actual afternoon had been. I don't know if I softened it because Wren Castellan wasn't real enough to need to tell the whole truth, or because some part of me was already writing for the newsletter version, the one with my actual name attached, the one Ms. Okafor said I was always going to have to face eventually.
