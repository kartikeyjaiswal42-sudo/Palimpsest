The harmonium sits on the floor of the back bedroom at my grandparents' flat in Southall, on a folded blanket because the legs of the case have gone, and the place where I feel most myself is sitting cross-legged behind it with my left hand on the bellows. My grandfather thinks this is a waste and we have never agreed about it.

It was his. It is a Monoj Kumar Sardar, bought in Delhi in 1971 and carried here in a crate, and three of the reeds are flat and the D above middle C sticks if the room is damp. He played it for forty years at the gurdwara and at home on Sunday mornings, and then his hands went — arthritis, both of them, the joints at the base of the fingers — and it went into the back bedroom in 2016 and stayed there.

I started playing it when I was thirteen because nobody else was going to. That is not a noble reason. It was sitting there and I was bored.

Here is the disagreement, and I want to set out his side properly, because he has a real argument and I get tired of people assuming a grandfather's position must be sentimental.

He says I am learning it wrong. Specifically, he says I am learning it as an instrument and not as an accompaniment. The harmonium in the tradition he learned it in is not a solo instrument; it exists under a voice. You play what the singer needs, you follow, you never lead, and the ornament belongs to the throat, not the keys. He says that what I do — sitting in that room working out the raag on the keyboard, playing it as a melody, no voice at all — is like learning to be a shadow with nobody standing in the light. He said that to me almost exactly, in Punjabi, and it is a better line than anything I could come up with.

My side is this. I do not sing. I cannot sing; I have tried and it is genuinely unpleasant to be in the room. So the choice he is describing is not available to me. It is not a choice between playing correctly and playing incorrectly. It is a choice between playing it wrong and it sitting under a blanket for another decade with three flat reeds, and I think an instrument that is played wrongly is more alive than an instrument that is preserved.

He does not accept this. He says a thing done wrong is not the same thing. He says I have taken a religious object and made it into a hobby.

I have started to think he might be partly right, which is annoying.

Because the reason that room is where I feel most myself is not devotional and I should not pretend it is. It is physical. It is the pump of the bellows with the left hand, which has to be continuous, which means your left arm is doing something rhythmic and slightly effortful the entire time and cannot stop, so there is no such thing as a pause. It is the smell — old wood and the particular dust of a room nobody vacuums. It is that the instrument is loud enough that I cannot hear my phone. I go in there on Sunday afternoons when we visit and I come out ninety minutes later and I have not thought about school once.

So he is right that I am not doing what he did. What I am doing is using his instrument for something he did not intend, which is to get out of my own head, and calling it inheritance because that is a more flattering word than escape.

We still argue. Two weeks ago I played him something and he listened all the way through and then said the left hand was uneven, which is true, and then said nothing else. My grandmother says that is approval. I do not think it is. I think he genuinely believes I am doing it wrong and has decided that I am going to do it anyway, and that being in the room while I do it is better than the alternative.

That is roughly where I have got to as well.
