Every Friday at ten past three for two years I emptied four blue bins into a wheeled cart and pushed it round the back of the sports hall to the compound. That is the whole activity. It took twenty minutes. It is the most useful thing I have ever done at school and the school has now cancelled it, and the belief I ended up questioning was not the one I expected.

The programme started because I read that our local authority had a 62% contamination rate on household recycling, which means that nearly two-thirds of what goes into the green bin gets pulled out and landfilled anyway. I found this outrageous and I found it outrageous in the specific way that seventeen-year-olds do, which is loudly and in an assembly. My proposal was four bins in the sixth-form block, one for paper, one for cans and plastic, one for glass, one for general. Mrs Aldous signed it off in September. I made posters.

The belief I was operating under was this: people do the wrong thing because they do not know the right thing. Give them information, give them the correct bin, and behaviour follows.

Six weeks in I emptied the paper bin and found a half-eaten pasta pot, two crisp packets, a can of Monster and, for reasons I have never resolved, a shoe.

I want to describe what happened next accurately, because it is the part that mattered. I did not give up and I did not launch a second poster campaign. I started sorting. Every Friday, in the compound, with gloves from the science prep room, I took the wrong things out of the right bins. It was disgusting for about the first month and then it was just a job. And because I was doing it by hand, week after week, I started to notice patterns that no amount of reading would have given me.

The general waste bin was almost always clean, because there is no wrong answer in a general waste bin. The paper bin was worst on Fridays after the bake sale. The glass bin was barely used and mostly contained plastic bottles, because from three feet away in a corridor, in the two seconds a person allocates to this decision, a clear plastic bottle looks exactly like glass. The bins were also in a bad spot — past the doors, so you had to turn back.

None of this was a knowledge problem. Nobody in that block believed that a shoe was paper. They were walking past a bin at speed with their hands full, making a half-second guess based on shape and colour, and my beautiful posters, which explained the recycling process, were addressed to a person who does not exist: someone standing still and reading.

So I changed things. I moved the bins to before the doors. I made the apertures different shapes — a slot for paper, a round hole for cans, and the wrong item physically will not go in a slot. I put pictures of actual objects on them, our objects, the specific brand of crisp packet our vending machine sells. Contamination went from about half to something I would guess at a fifth, though I only ever measured it by eye, which is a weakness in what I am telling you.

Then in March the school cancelled the programme. The waste contractor changed and the new one takes commingled recycling, so the sorting we were doing had nowhere to go, and the four bins became two. I was angry for a week. I am less angry now, though I do not think the decision was well explained to anyone.

What survived is the belief I actually changed. I used to think that changing behaviour meant changing minds. Now I think it mostly means changing the two seconds — the shape of the hole, the position of the door, the moment where a person is deciding without knowing they are deciding. That is a less flattering theory. It gives me a much smaller role. It also happens to be the one that worked.

I still notice bin apertures everywhere I go. My friends find this tedious. Twenty minutes a week for two years, pushing a cart past the sports hall, and the residue is that I cannot walk through a train station without evaluating the slots.
