The shelter listed Biscuit as a two-year-old shepherd mix, house-trained, good with children. Two of those three turned out to be guesses. He came home in the back of my mother's car in March, shaking hard enough that the seat belt buckle rattled against the door, and for four days he lived under the dining table and would not eat unless the kitchen was empty. I decided, almost immediately, that I would be the one who fixed him.

I had a system. I had watched maybe forty hours of training videos by then, and I wore a canvas pouch of freeze-dried liver on my belt like a tool. Sit, stay, come, place. Three sessions a day, ten minutes each, exactly as the videos prescribed. I logged them in a notebook: date, duration, treats used, success rate. For a while the numbers moved. He learned sit in a week and his name in two. I remember telling my sister that people who complained about rescue dogs were simply not consistent.

Then we hit the thing that would not move. Biscuit could not be left alone. Not for an hour, not for ten minutes. He would scream, which is the only accurate word, and chew the doorframe until his gums bled, and once he went through a window screen and was found four streets away by a man walking to work. I treated this like every other item on my list. I built a schedule of departures: thirty seconds, then a minute, then two. I bought a camera and counted the barks. When the graph refused to bend, I ran the sessions longer, because longer had always worked before.

It took a vet to tell me what I had actually been doing. She used the word threshold and explained that every time I pushed past the point where he had already begun to panic, I was not teaching him that I come back. I was teaching him that panic is the ordinary condition of an afternoon. Eight months of careful, well-documented work had made him measurably worse, and my notebook was the record of it. You can find the exact page where the percentages stop and I started writing only the dates.

What I did next is less impressive than I wish it were. I stopped training him. We put him on medication, which I had resisted because it felt like conceding that effort was not the answer. My mother took over the departures at a pace so slow it embarrassed me, thirty seconds for three weeks before we tried forty. Biscuit is six now. He can be left for about three hours. That is not a triumph, only a life. He sleeps by the radiator and still cannot ride in a car.

I think about the notebook more than I expected to. Not because I was lazy or cruel, since I was neither, but because I was certain, and the certainty was the whole problem. I had confused working hard at something with being right about it, and I had built an entire apparatus of measurement that could only tell me how faithfully I was repeating the mistake. It never occurred to me to ask whether the thing I was measuring was the thing that mattered. I try to check that now, in labs and in arguments and with people, and I am honest enough to say I am usually not fast enough about it.
