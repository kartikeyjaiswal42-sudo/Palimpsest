My mother has run a secondhand clothing stall at the Saturday market since I was eleven, the year after my dad lost his job at the plant and didn't find another one that paid the same. She calls it Second Chances, which is painted by hand on a piece of plywood she props against the folding table, and for the first two years of it I was, honestly, ashamed of the whole operation — the racks of somebody else's old coats, the hand-lettered sign, my mother steaming wrinkles out of donated blouses at our kitchen table on a Thursday night while I did homework six feet away and pretended I didn't notice the smell of other people's closets filling our kitchen.

The setback in this story isn't really the job loss, even though that was bad enough. It's a specific Saturday in ninth grade when a girl from my English class walked past the stall with her mom and stopped, and picked up a denim jacket, and said, delighted, "Oh my god, is this from Rue Home Goods, I swear I saw this exact jacket on their Instagram." My mother, who has an excellent memory for inventory, said cheerfully that yes, it had come out of a donation bag from a family two streets over from where that girl lived. The girl put the jacket down like it had bitten her. I stood behind the rack for the rest of the afternoon pretending to reorganize hangers so she wouldn't see whose daughter I was.

I brought it up with my mom that night, badly. I told her I didn't want to work the stall anymore, and when she asked why, I said something about how embarrassing it was, and she went very quiet in a way that was worse than yelling. She said she hadn't realized I thought what she did for a living was something to hide from people I went to school with. I said that wasn't what I meant, except it kind of was, and we both knew it, and the conversation ended with her saying, flatly, that this business had paid for my shoes, my field trip to Washington, half my braces, and she'd appreciate it if I found a way to be around it without flinching.

We never really finished that conversation. We had versions of it again over the next two years — me negotiating out of Saturday shifts, her pointing out, not unfairly, that I seemed to have no problem spending the money the stall brought in. What changed wasn't the argument resolving. What changed was smaller and slower than that. I started noticing, on the Saturdays I couldn't get out of working, that my mother knows more about fabric and construction and decade-specific tailoring than anyone I've met, that she can tell real vintage from a costume-shop knockoff at ten feet, that customers drive in from towns forty minutes away because they trust her eye specifically. I did the math, once, on a slow afternoon, on how many of the things in my closet and half the things in our house had come through that table, and it embarrassed me in a completely different direction than the denim jacket had.

I still haven't told her, in so many words, that I'm proud of what she built out of a bad year. I don't know exactly why the sentence won't come out of me, except that it feels late, three years late, arriving only after I stopped being the problem instead of being useful earlier when it would have cost me something. We don't fight about the stall anymore, not really, but we haven't resolved it either, not in the clean way stories like this are supposed to resolve. She still sometimes asks, lightly, almost like she's testing the water, whether I want to help out this weekend, and I still sometimes say I have plans when I don't, and neither of us calls it what it is.

What I learned isn't tidy enough for a single sentence, but it has something to do with how a person can build real dignity out of a circumstance you were embarrassed by, and how your own embarrassment says a lot more about you than it does about them, and how you can understand all of that clearly and still not manage to say the one thing that would fix it, not yet, maybe not for a while longer.
