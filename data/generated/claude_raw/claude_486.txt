The computer lab at our school is in the basement, in a room that used to be storage before someone decided it could be something more useful. It's small, poorly lit, with a radiator that clanks unpredictably and a window that looks out onto the parking lot. There's no air conditioning. In the summer, it's sweltering. In winter, the radiator overheats it. Most students avoid it when they can.

I found myself spending every free period there, which is where I learned that half the laptops the school had purchased were broken.

It started because I was hiding, to be honest. The main computer lab was where everyone hung out between classes, and I wasn't good at belonging to that scene. But the basement room was empty most of the time. I could work on assignments there without the noise and without the specific anxiety of being in a space full of people I didn't quite know how to be around.

One day I noticed that one of the laptops wouldn't turn on. I poked at it idly, not expecting anything, and the case fell open. Inside, I could see the problem immediately—a loose cable. I pushed it back in, and the laptop started up. It felt like a small magic trick.

After that, I couldn't stop noticing all the other broken ones. Keyboards with stuck keys, screens that wouldn't respond, machines that ran so slowly they were useless. I started bringing tools downstairs—a small screwdriver set, some cleaning supplies. I taught myself using YouTube videos and a lot of trial and error. Some things I fixed, some things I didn't. But slowly, the lab went from having maybe six working laptops to having twelve.

The school's IT person eventually noticed. Mrs. Chen came down one day, looked at what I'd done, and asked why. I told her I was just killing time, which was true and also not true. She offered to show me more. She didn't ask permission; she just started teaching me, showing me how to diagnose problems methodically, how to replace components, how to know when something was worth fixing and when it was time to order new equipment.

That basement became different after that. It went from being the place I hid to being the place I chose to be. Mrs. Chen would leave me a list of machines to work on, and I'd work through them during lunch. Other students started coming down because they'd heard the laptops actually worked now. The room filled up. The noise increased. But it was different noise than the main lab. It was quieter, more purposeful.

What I realized was that I don't feel most myself in crowds or in socially active spaces. I feel most myself when I'm fixing something. When my hands are working on a problem that has a solution. When I can see the exact moment something stops being broken and starts working again. When someone comes up to me with a laptop that won't turn on and leaves with one that does, I understand my purpose in that moment with complete clarity.

The basement computer lab is still poor, still hot in summer and too warm in winter, still has a radiator that sounds like someone's dying in the walls. But it's mine in a way that feels true. I'm there most days after school, working on machines, learning more every time I approach a new problem. The school ordered better equipment once the budget allowed it, but I still maintain the old ones. They're held together by small fixes, by the kind of care that doesn't look glamorous.

I feel most myself there because I'm not trying to be anyone. I'm just solving problems with my hands while other people use the machines I've fixed. It's simple and necessary and, for the first time in school, it feels like that's enough.
