The textbook is called The American Nation, eleventh edition, and my copy has a strip of yellow sticky tab on page 412 that's been there since October, going soft and gray at the edges now from being flipped past so many times. I could tell you the paragraph on that page by memory at this point. It's four sentences long, about the incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II, and it calls the policy "a difficult but understandable security measure given the fears of the time."

My great-grandmother was fourteen when she and her family were sent to Manzanar. I know this because my grandfather has her photographs, a few letters, and one page of a diary she kept for about six weeks before she stopped writing in it, which he showed me once and which I still think about more than almost anything else I've been shown by anyone in my family. So when I read "difficult but understandable" in a school-issued textbook, in a class of thirty-one students, most of whom were going to take that sentence as simply true because it was printed and assigned, I put the sticky tab on the page and went to talk to my teacher, Mr. Delacroix, after class.

He's a good teacher. I want to say that clearly, because what happened next wasn't a case of a bad teacher being dismissive. He listened to me the first time, actually, and agreed the wording was weak, and said he'd mention it to the department. I believed him. Then nothing changed, for about three weeks, and when I brought it up again, he told me, a little more tired this time, that he didn't control the textbook adoption cycle and that there were bigger fights to have with the district than one paragraph's phrasing.

I disagreed, in class, out loud, which I don't think either of us expected. I'd brought printed pages from an oral history archive — testimony from people who were actually there, describing armed guards and barbed wire and losing homes and farms in a matter of weeks, nothing that sounded like a "difficult but understandable" anything — and I read part of one out loud during a discussion that was supposed to be about something else entirely. Mr. Delacroix let me finish. Then he said, in front of everyone, that he took the point, but that my frustration with one sentence in one textbook wasn't the same as a plan, and if I wanted the sentence to actually change for the next class that came through his room, I needed to write it up properly, with sources, and bring it to the department chair myself, not just win an argument in front of thirty-one witnesses.

That stung, honestly, because it felt like being told my feelings didn't count until they were formatted correctly. But he was right, and I did it — wrote up two pages with the sources cited, met with the department chair in November, and by December the department had agreed to add a supplemental reading packet alongside that chapter for next year's classes, including my great-grandmother's diary page, which my grandfather let me include.

The textbook itself hasn't changed. Page 412 still says what it said in October. I kept the sticky tab on it anyway, not to remind myself to be angry, but because that argument is the reason people in that class started treating me differently — not as the difficult kid who fights with the teacher, but as the one who actually checks where a sentence comes from before accepting it. Two classmates have since asked me to look at other pages with them. I don't think of myself as someone who belongs at the center of that classroom community. I think of myself as someone who found a place in it by being the one who reads the footnotes, who flags the page, who won't let "understandable" sit unquestioned next to my own family's photographs.

I still have the printed testimony pages, folded into the back cover of that textbook, and I hand the book back at the end of the year with them still tucked inside, on purpose, for whoever gets it next.
