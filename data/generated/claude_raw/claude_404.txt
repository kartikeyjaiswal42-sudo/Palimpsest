For fourteen months I photographed every piece of jewellery I made on the same square of grey card, on the windowsill of the back bedroom, between four and five in the afternoon. That was the habit. Grey card, north light, four o'clock. I did it because a video told me consistency was what separated a shop that looked real from one that looked like a teenager's hobby, and I badly wanted mine to look real.

The shop was called Small Weather and it sold earrings I made from copper wire and glass beads, and it sold eleven of them. Eleven, in fourteen months, four of which were to my aunt. I am not going to pretend that number does not still make me wince. I had a spreadsheet where I tracked views and favourites and conversion rate, and I had read enough about pricing that I could explain, at length, to anyone who asked, why my prices were correct. The shop had one hundred and thirty items in it. It was not that people looked and did not buy. It was that almost nobody looked at all.

I closed it in the March of my sixteenth year. What I told people was that I needed to focus on school. What was actually true is that I had begun to feel a small physical dread every time I opened the app, and that the dread had started spreading to the making itself, which had been the only part I ever loved.

What I did next was nothing, for about two months, and I think that was correct. Then I did something small. I kept photographing. Not for the shop, which was gone, but because four o'clock on the windowsill had become the part of the day that was mine, and I did not want to give it up just because it had failed to earn anything. So I photographed the earrings I made for friends, and then I photographed other things — a cracked cup, my brother's football boots, a bunch of coriander from the market — always on the same grey card, always in the same light.

Somewhere in the sixth or seventh month of doing that, a woman who ran a small ceramics studio two streets over saw the photographs on my account and asked whether I would shoot her new glazes. She paid me forty pounds. Then she told someone else. I have now photographed products for four small businesses, and I am better at it than I ever was at making jewellery, and I would not have known that if the shop had worked.

The lesson I am supposed to draw here is that failure redirects you, and there is some truth in that, but it is not the part I actually think about. What I think about is that the habit outlived the ambition. I built the four o'clock ritual as a means to an end, and the end never arrived, and the means turned out to be the durable thing — a skill I had been practising six hundred times without ever once considering it the point.

I still have the grey card. It is warped at one corner from a spilled glass of water and there is a thumbprint of copper polish in the middle that will not come off. I have been told repeatedly that I could buy a new one for very little money. I am not going to.
