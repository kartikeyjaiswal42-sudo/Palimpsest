The 6:05 from our estate gets into the city at ten past seven, and then there is the 31 across town, and then eleven minutes walking, and if any part of that goes wrong you are late. Coming home is worse because of the traffic. Four hours a day, door to door, for five years, because when I was eleven I passed an exam for a school on the other side of the city and my parents decided that this was worth it.

On the way home, for the first hour, I sat with my eyes shut and went back through the day in order. Not revising. There was no book open. I simply started at the beginning — first period, Mr Vance, the board, what he had written on it — and walked forwards through the six lessons, retrieving whatever would come. When something would not come I sat with the gap for a bit and then moved on. By the time we hit the roundabout at Copse Hill I was usually somewhere in the afternoon.

I started doing it because the bus made me travel sick if I read, and because sleeping meant missing my stop. That is the entire origin. For two years I did not tell anyone I did it, because it sounded like nothing.

What I noticed, around Year Ten, was that I did not really revise the way other people did. My friends spoke about revision as an event — a week of it, a period of suffering before an exam. I did not have those weeks and I was not doing better than them because I was cleverer. I was doing better because I had accidentally been testing myself on everything, every single day, twelve hours after learning it, for four years.

I only understood why it worked when a teacher mentioned retrieval practice in a study-skills assembly and described, in about forty seconds, the thing I had been doing on the 31 since I was twelve. Trying to recall something without the notes in front of you is not a way of checking whether you know it. It is the act that makes you know it. All the years I had spent resenting that bus, I had been given a daily hour in which reading was impossible and my only entertainment was the inside of my own head.

The realisation changed less about my studying than about how I think of constraints. I had a fixed story about the commute: it was the tax my family paid for the school, four hours a day taken from me. Everything I did not do — clubs, the football team, being at anyone's house after five — sat on that ledger. And that story was not wrong, exactly. I did miss those things and I still mind. But it was incomplete, because the account had only a debit column, and I had been adding to it since I was eleven without ever once asking what was on the other side.

Now, when something is imposed on me and I catch myself narrating it as pure loss, I try to at least look. Not to be cheerful about it. Some things really are just loss. But I know now that I am not a reliable witness to my own circumstances, because I ran a four-hour-a-day experiment for five years and did not notice the result until somebody told me from a stage.

I take the 6:05 tomorrow. There is a man who gets on two stops after me and sleeps until the city, and I have never spoken to him, and I have been seeing him nearly every weekday for five years. On the way home I still shut my eyes at the same place, out of habit now rather than nausea, and go back to first period.
