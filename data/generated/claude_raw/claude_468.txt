My mother is a librarian, which is why the school asked me, and it is also why we are still not speaking about it properly a year later.

The school library had not been touched in fifteen years. Six thousand books, a Dewey system that had drifted so far from itself that the poetry sat in three separate places, and a card catalogue nobody had opened since before I was born. When Mr Adeyemi asked for volunteers to reorganise it over the summer I said yes before he finished the sentence, and I went home and told my mother, and she was delighted for about four minutes.

Then I told her my plan, which was to abandon Dewey entirely.

I had thought about it. Nobody in our school browses by number. I had watched people for a term: they come in wanting something like the last thing they read, or something a friend mentioned, then they wander, then they leave with nothing. Bookshops group by subject in ordinary words and people find things in bookshops constantly. So I proposed sections — Other Countries, People Who Actually Lived, How Things Work, Very Short Books — with a spreadsheet linking every title to its old number.

My mother said no. Not in an argument. She said it the way you tell someone something obvious. A classification system is not for the person browsing, she said. It is for the person who comes after you. You will leave school in two years and take the whole scheme with you in your head, and the next librarian will inherit six thousand books arranged according to the private taste of a sixteen-year-old.

I said that Dewey was also somebody's private taste, just an American man's from 1876, and that his taste had put every religion that was not Christianity into a single subdivision. She said that was true and beside the point. I said it was exactly the point. We went round like this at the kitchen table for most of an evening. It was the first time I had disagreed with her about the thing she has spent twenty-six years doing.

I did it my way. Eleven days, four volunteers, and by the end of August the library was resectioned, relabelled, the spreadsheet nine hundred rows long. Borrowing that autumn went up by a third. I am not going to pretend that number does not matter to me.

But last April our librarian left, and the woman who replaced her spent a fortnight unable to find anything. In June a Year Eight could not find a book on volcanoes, because I had shelved volcanoes under How Things Work and she had looked, reasonably, in Other Countries. My mother has never said I told you so. She asked in October how the library was going, and I said fine, and she nodded, and that was the whole exchange.

What I cannot resolve is that I still think I was right about the browsing and she was right about the inheritance, and I do not know how those two things are supposed to fit together. She believes a system should outlive the person who made it. I believe a system that nobody uses has already failed, whoever inherits it. We are both, I think, describing something true about what we are afraid of.

The spreadsheet is on the school server and also on a memory stick in my desk drawer. I check occasionally that it is still there.
