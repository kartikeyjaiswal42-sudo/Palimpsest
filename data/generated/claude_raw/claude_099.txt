My grandfather had a stroke the winter I was fifteen, and it left the right side of his mouth slower than the left, so that words he'd said his whole life without thinking now came out thick and delayed, sometimes wrong, a name swapped for another name, a year that didn't match the story. He grew up in a village called Barhona that doesn't exist anymore, swallowed decades ago when the coal operation on its border expanded and the whole settlement was relocated, houses and wells and a school and all, to make room for the pit. He used to tell me about it constantly when I was small, and I used to only half listen, the way you half listen to anything that seems like it will always be available to you later.

After the stroke, I was afraid it wouldn't be. That's the honest obstacle here, not the stroke itself, which he recovered from well enough, but the specific fear that the stories I'd never bothered to actually absorb were about to become permanently harder to reach, garbled by a mouth that didn't work the way it used to, and that I had wasted twelve years of easy access to something I could no longer take for granted.

So I started a habit that felt almost too small to count as anything. Every Sunday after lunch, I'd sit with him on the porch for exactly as long as he had energy for, which usually wasn't long, fifteen or twenty minutes before his face would start to look tired in a way I learned to recognize. I brought a small notebook, not a recorder, because I noticed early on that a recorder made him self-conscious about how slowly he spoke, and a notebook let me just write while he talked without either of us watching a machine measure the gaps. I gave myself one rule: write down one full sentence, just one, that felt true. Not a summary. An actual sentence, in something close to his words.

Some Sundays that was easy. Some Sundays he'd stall on a name for a full minute and I'd sit there not filling the silence for him, which was its own kind of discipline I had to teach myself, because my instinct was always to guess the word and finish his sentence, and I learned that finishing it for him usually meant losing whatever he'd actually been about to say.

I have sixty-one sentences now, one notebook, less than a page for some months and more for others. It doesn't read like a story. It reads like fragments: the well was deeper than any well after it. My mother sold milk to the overseer's wife on Tuesdays. The school bell was actually a piece of rail track someone hit with a hammer. None of it would mean much to a stranger. But when my younger cousin needed something for a family history project last spring, that notebook was the only written record of Barhona that exists anywhere, and I handed it to her without hesitation, sixty-one small true things, collected on purpose, one sentence a week, because I was scared of losing something I'd never actually thought to value until it was almost gone.

I still go on Sundays. I don't need the fear anymore to keep doing it. That part surprised me most.
