Channel fourteen on our lighting board sticks. Not broken, just stubborn—you push the fader up and it catches about two-thirds of the way, like it's thinking it over, and if you don't know to tap the side of the slider with your knuckle at exactly that point, the stage right wash never comes all the way up. Nobody outside the booth knows this. It is, genuinely, the single most important piece of information in our theater program, and it lives entirely in the heads of about six people who've run that board over the years.

I'm one of those six now. I run lights for our school's plays, which means during tech week I basically live in a booth the size of a closet at the back of the house, headset on, cue sheet in front of me covered in my own shorthand that would mean nothing to anyone else. GO on "the light," hold two counts, fade to black on her exit line. I've missed exactly one cue in two years, during Act Two of a musical nobody will remember, and I still think about it sometimes at random moments, which tells you something about how seriously I take a job most of the audience doesn't know exists.

Here's what I didn't expect when I signed up sophomore year mostly because the drama teacher needed a body and I needed an elective: I found an actual community back there. Not the kind people usually mean when they talk about theater—I'm not on stage, I've never once been on stage, I get stage fright just thinking about it. I mean the stage manager who taught me to tap channel fourteen without being asked twice. The sound board op next to me who eats an entire sleeve of crackers during every single tech rehearsal and somehow never gets crumbs in her console. The senior who ran lights before me and left me a binder full of notes on every show we'd ever done, cue numbers and warnings about actors who liked to speed up under pressure, like some kind of inheritance nobody talks about but everyone relies on.

My place in that group is specific and it's small and I like it that way. I'm not the loudest person back there. I'm the one who shows up an hour before call time to check every channel, including fourteen, because I'd rather lose sleep than lose a cue in front of three hundred people. During one show a fuse actually blew mid-scene, half the stage went dark for real, not as a choice, and I was the one who had to decide in about four seconds whether to kill everything or fake a blackout transition like we meant it. I faked it. Nobody in the audience noticed. Only the four people backstage who knew what almost happened understood what they'd just watched me do, and that's kind of the whole job, honestly—being essential and invisible at the same time.

I still think channel fourteen should probably get replaced. I've told our teacher this at least four times. But there's a small, stupid part of me that would miss it if it worked right the first time, because knowing exactly how to fix something nobody else even notices is broken is, weirdly, the closest thing I've felt to belonging somewhere. I don't need the audience to clap for me. I need the stage manager's voice in my headset saying "nice save" after a blackout nobody was supposed to see me catch.
