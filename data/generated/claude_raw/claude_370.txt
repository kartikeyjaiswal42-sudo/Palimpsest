I met Kai in a Discord server for people interested in digital art, which seemed like a safe way to make friends without the complications of actual proximity. He lived in Japan. I lived in the Midwest. The distance meant we could be friends without it being complicated by the social hierarchies of real life.

For the first year, it was exactly what I wanted. We'd talk about art, about school, about the specific experience of being a teenager in completely different contexts. He'd tell me about Japanese high school. I'd tell him about American high school. It was interesting precisely because it was removed. There was no possibility of this friendship becoming something more complicated.

But somewhere around month fourteen of knowing him, that distance stopped feeling like a feature and started feeling like a problem.

It started when he mentioned he was applying to American colleges. He asked me what my town was like, whether there were good schools nearby. He was thinking about California, he said, but he was also thinking about the Midwest. He'd researched it. It seemed like there were good options.

I think that's when I realized I'd fallen into the specific trap of online friendships where you can like someone without having to actually deal with them. Kai was perfect at a distance. But if he actually ended up an hour away from me, in the same geographic reality, I'd have to figure out whether I actually wanted a real friendship with him or whether I was just comfortable with the version of him I'd created in my head.

I became weird. I stopped responding to messages as quickly. I made excuses not to video call. I told myself it was because I was busy, but actually I was scared. Scared that if we became real-life friends, I wouldn't like him as much. Scared that he'd realize that the version of me I presented online was edited and curated and wasn't fully real.

He called me out on it, which was both the kindest and most difficult thing someone's ever done. He said he could feel me pulling away, and he wanted to know why. He asked if I didn't want to be friends anymore. He asked if I was embarrassed by him.

I had to tell him the truth, which was messy and complicated and made me look selfish. I told him I was scared of ruining something by making it real. I told him I'd gotten comfortable with the distance. I told him that if he actually came to the same continent, I wasn't sure I was brave enough to be his friend in a way that required actual showing up.

He didn't get mad. He just got quiet for a few days. When he came back, he'd decided not to apply to American colleges. He was going to stay in Japan. He said that if our friendship meant I had to be afraid, then it was better to keep it the way it was.

I don't know if that was the right call. I think about it sometimes, about whether I made a choice that was about protecting myself or about protecting our friendship. I think about the version of reality where he came to the same country and I was brave enough to show up, and I wonder what that friendship would have looked like.

What actually happened is that I changed my mind about what I wanted from friendship. I stopped trying to have relationships that were safe and started valuing relationships where I might get hurt or disappointed or have to be vulnerable. Kai stayed in Japan. We're still friends. But there's this specific thing between us that we never resolved, this acknowledgment that I chose to protect myself over choosing him.

I think that taught me something, but I'm not sure I can explain it clearly. Something about how love—all kinds of love, not just romantic—requires risk. Something about how you can't actually connect with another person if you're also always protecting yourself from connection. It's either real or it's not.
