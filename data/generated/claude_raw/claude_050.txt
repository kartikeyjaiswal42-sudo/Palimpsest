I had watched my mom cook for seventeen years before I decided I actually knew how to do it. This is a specific kind of arrogance, the kind that comes from seeing someone do something over and over and thinking you've absorbed the knowledge by osmosis. I hadn't. I was just confident in ways that weren't supported by evidence.

The night I decided to cook dinner for everyone was a Tuesday in March. My parents had gone out to see a movie, and I was left with my younger brother and the family's collective hunger. I had made pasta before—just pasta, boiled water and a jar of sauce—but this felt different. This felt like cooking, like I was going to produce something real and nourishing, not just heat something someone else had already made most of the way.

I decided on chicken with rice and vegetables, which I now know is an absurdly ambitious first real meal, but which seemed perfectly reasonable when I wasn't thinking about it carefully. I started by cooking the rice, and somewhere in the middle of that, I looked up a recipe on my phone, which should have been my first sign that this was going poorly. The second sign was when my brother came into the kitchen and asked what was burning.

Nothing was burning. I had just put the rice on, and I had also forgotten about some frozen peas I had put on the stove twenty minutes earlier. Those were definitely charred. I threw them out and started again.

The chicken was next. I had taken it out of the freezer that morning, assuming it would thaw, which is not actually how thawing works. I ended up trying to microwave it, which made it cook in weird uneven patches while other parts remained frozen. I told my brother to go away and stopped looking at my phone and just tried to do the thing that felt right, which meant cooking it longer, lower, hoping that eventually it would all be the same temperature.

It was around this point that I realized I had no idea what I was doing, and I also realized something else, which was that I didn't actually care. My brother was hungry. I was going to feed him. The chicken would not be perfect. The rice would be slightly mushy. The vegetables, which I eventually remembered to cook, would probably be overdone. But it would be food, and it would be food that I had made, and that felt like something.

The actual turning point wasn't when I served the meal or when my brother told me it was good or even when my parents came home and seemed genuinely impressed. It was earlier, when I stopped trying to cook the meal the way my mom would cook it and just started cooking it the way I could cook it, with my limited skills and my imperfect knowledge and my willingness to just keep moving forward even when things weren't going right.

My mom asked me the next morning if I wanted to cook dinner more often, and I said yes, which was surprising to me because I had spent most of that Tuesday evening wishing I had ordered food instead. But something had changed. I had done the thing even though I was bad at it. And doing the thing even when you're bad at it turns out to be the only way you ever stop being bad at it.

Now, six months later, I actually know how to cook. I mean, I still mess things up, but less often, and I do it with the confidence of someone who knows that failure is just part of how you get better at something that matters to you. That Tuesday night, I was just trying to feed my brother. I didn't know I was becoming the kind of person who actually knew how to do things.
