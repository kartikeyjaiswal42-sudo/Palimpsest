The summer I was fourteen I decided to read every book on a single shelf at the county library, the mystery section, second row from the bottom, roughly thirty-eight books by nine different authors. I don't remember exactly why I picked that shelf over any other one nearby. I think it was simply the shelf my eyes landed on first that day, and once I'd said the plan out loud to myself, I felt obligated to follow through on it, the way a promise becomes real the moment you say it, even to no one.

What actually made the summer work wasn't the ambition of the plan. It was a habit I built almost by accident and then couldn't stop: I went to the library every single morning, six days a week, and read for exactly one hour before doing anything else that day, sitting in the same chair by the window if it was free, a different one if it wasn't. I didn't decide on the hour in advance as some kind of discipline exercise. It just happened that the first few days I read for about an hour before I got hungry and went home, and by the second week the hour had become the shape of the whole summer, something I did before I checked whether I felt like doing it.

There were mornings I didn't want to go. I remember one in particular, after a late night at a friend's house, where I almost texted my mom to say I'd sleep in instead, and went anyway, mostly out of guilt about breaking a streak I'd started keeping track of on a sticky note taped inside my binder. I read badly that morning, barely absorbing two chapters, but I showed up, and I think that's the part that actually mattered more than any single book I finished that summer.

Some of the thirty-eight books were genuinely bad, plots that fell apart if you looked at them too closely, characters who existed only to be suspects. A few I skimmed toward the end, which felt like cheating on my own rule but which I let myself do anyway, telling myself the point was the habit, not perfect completion of every page. A few were much better than I expected, and I read those twice, once quickly and once slowly, taking actual notes on a legal pad about how the author had hidden the solution in plain sight the whole time, obvious only in hindsight.

I finished the shelf in late August, four days before school started again. Thirty-eight books, sixty-some mornings, one sticky note with a chain of check marks I still have somewhere in a drawer at the bottom of my closet. I didn't come out of it with some grand insight about mystery fiction as a genre, and I don't think that was ever really the point of the project, even if it's the part that sounds impressive when I describe it to people now, years later, at some party where the subject somehow comes up.

What I actually got was the hour itself, and the discovery that showing up at the same time in the same chair, whether or not I felt like it that particular morning, built something in me that no single motivated day ever could have. I've kept some version of that habit going since then, in different forms — an hour of something most mornings, not always reading, not always the same chair. It started because of a shelf of mystery novels I picked almost at random, and I still think the shelf itself mattered less than the sixty mornings it took me to get through it.
