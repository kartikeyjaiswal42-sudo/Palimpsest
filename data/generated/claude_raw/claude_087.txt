Coach Bell cut me from varsity three years straight. Freshman year I expected it—I was fine with that one, honestly, I was five foot two and hadn't grown into anything yet. Sophomore year stung more. Junior year, though, that one wrecked me for a while, because by then I actually thought I had a shot.

Here's the thing I did wrong, and I still cringe thinking about it. After the sophomore cut, I got so mad I skipped the spring skills camp Coach Bell ran for anyone who wanted extra reps, cut or not. It was optional. Free, even, on Tuesday afternoons in the small gym. I told myself I didn't need it, that showing up would look desperate, like I was begging for a spot instead of earning one. Really I just didn't want to stand in a gym with a man who'd told me no twice already. So I didn't go. Not once, all spring.

Junior year I got cut again, obviously, and this time I asked for a meeting, mostly to argue with him, if I'm being honest. I had a whole case prepared in my head about playing time and fairness and how Marcus Webb, who made the team, couldn't hit a free throw to save his life. I walked into his office ready for that fight.

He didn't fight back. He pulled out a folder—an actual paper folder, this man keeps handwritten notes on tryouts, which I did not expect—and he had specific stuff written down from all three years. My footwork off the dribble. My left hand, which is bad, still is honestly. And then he said something I wasn't ready for: that he'd noticed I stopped coming to the spring camp after sophomore year, and he'd wondered why, because the two kids who did come both made varsity as juniors, not because they were naturally better than me, but because they'd used the one door he'd left open.

I didn't say much back. I remember just sitting there, staring at a stat line from a scrimmage I didn't even remember being that bad in, feeling my face get hot in a way that had nothing to do with anger this time.

What got me wasn't the cut. I was numb to that by then. It was that he'd kept notes on a kid he never actually coached, for three years, and showed up to a meeting he didn't have to take with actual evidence instead of a brush-off. Coaches don't owe you that. Most don't give it. I went home and I don't think I said one word to my parents at dinner, and later that night I actually looked up whether the camp was still running that spring, which it was.

I went. Every Tuesday. I'm not going to pretend this turns into some story where I made the team senior year and hit a buzzer beater—I didn't even try out again, I ran cross country instead and liked it more than I expected to. But I think about that folder a lot. Somebody who had every reason to just tell me no and move on chose to tell me no and also show me exactly why, and hand me a door I'd already slammed shut myself. I skipped that camp out of pride, and it's probably the pettiest decision I've made that actually cost me something. I still don't fully know what to do with being grateful to the guy who cut me three years running. But I am. That's just true, whether it's a tidy thing to admit or not.
