"Tell her the shadow is probably nothing," the doctor says, and my mother is looking at me instead of at him, waiting for the Vietnamese to catch up to the English the way it always does, half a beat behind, like a dubbed movie.

I don't tell her that. I tell her that they found something small on the scan and the doctor wants to look again in six weeks, which is true, technically, in the sense that every word of it is accurate and none of the shape of it is. My mother nods. My father, sitting on the other side of her, watches my mouth the way he always does at these appointments, checking not the words but my face, because he's the one who actually asked me, in the car on the way over, to leave out the word "mass" if it came up.

I've been doing this since I was eleven, sitting in waiting rooms with my legs not reaching the floor, learning words like "biopsy" and "co-pay" before I learned them in my own language for myself. My parents' English is fine for the grocery store and fine for parent-teacher conferences where the stakes are a B-minus. It is not fine for a doctor talking fast about probabilities, and so I go, and I translate, and somewhere along the way I started doing something the doctors never asked me to do, which is decide how much my mother can carry in one sitting.

My father agrees with this. He has always agreed with it, has told me plainly, more than once, that his job is to earn the money and my job right now is to keep her calm, that a scared mother is a mother who cannot get out of bed, and none of us can afford that. I respect him. He built the two of us a life out of a language he still mispronounces on purpose sometimes, just to make the man at the hardware store laugh, and I have never once thought he was wrong about anything that mattered.

Except this. Except sitting in the car afterward while my mother looks out the window and asks me, in Vietnamese, whether I told her everything, and I say yes, and she believes me, because I am seventeen and she has decided to trust the seventeen-year-old more than she trusts her own instinct that something in the room felt withheld.

I told my father, once, that I thought we should just tell her straight, let her be scared if she needs to be scared, that being protected from your own body felt like something closer to being erased from your own life. He listened. He always listens. Then he said, in the flat voice he uses when a conversation is over whether or not I agree with the ending, that I didn't understand yet what it costs a person to watch someone they love be afraid, and that someday I would, and I would understand why he does it this way.

I don't know if he's right. I go back with them next month for the follow-up, and I already know I will sit in the same chair, watch the same doctor's mouth, and make the same small decisions about what crosses the room and what doesn't, my father's hand ready on the table in case I need reminding, my mother's face turned toward mine like I'm the only clear channel in a static-filled house.
