"You're guessing," my mother said, watching me squint up at the sky, one hand shading my eyes, trying to decide between two shutter speeds I was choosing basically at random. "Yes," I said, "that's the whole camera."

The camera was a Pentax K1000 that had belonged to my grandfather, no battery, no light meter, nothing but a mechanical body and a lens and whatever judgment I could bring to it, which in the beginning was none at all. I'd asked for it after finding it in a closet, mostly because it looked cool sitting on my desk, and I had no real plan to use it seriously until I loaded a roll of film out of stubbornness one Saturday and went out to shoot the empty lot behind our house, wanting to prove to myself I could figure it out without an app or a meter telling me what to do.

The first roll came back from the lab almost entirely ruined. Half the frames were black, overexposed into nothing, or so underexposed you could barely make out a shape. I remember standing at the counter of the drugstore, going through the prints one by one, feeling genuinely stupid, like I'd wasted eleven dollars and an afternoon proving I had no eye for this at all. I almost put the camera back in the closet that same week. It felt less like a hobby and more like a machine specifically built to embarrass me.

I didn't put it back, though I couldn't tell you exactly why, except that something in me wanted to understand what I'd gotten wrong rather than just accept that I had. I started reading about the Sunny 16 rule, about how film speed and aperture and shutter speed relate to each other, doing the math by hand before I even lifted the camera, treating exposure less like an art and more like a puzzle with a right answer I hadn't found yet. My second roll, shot two weeks later, had maybe four usable frames out of thirty-six. My third had closer to twenty.

The real shift happened on the fourth roll, on a gray, flat, overcast afternoon that gave me no obvious cues at all — no hard shadows to read, no strong sun to work off — the exact kind of light I would have guessed wrong on a month earlier. I stood in the backyard for a long moment, ran the calculation in my head, adjusted for the cloud cover, and took a photo of my little sister on the swing set that came back sharp, correctly exposed, genuinely good, the kind of photo where you can see individual strands of her hair against the gray sky. I remember standing at the drugstore counter with that print in my hand and feeling something click into place that hadn't clicked before — not just relief that I'd finally gotten one right, but the specific, quiet thrill of having reasoned my way to a correct answer with nothing but a lens and a bit of math and my own two eyes.

After that I stopped thinking of the missing light meter as a limitation and started thinking of it as the entire reason I liked the camera. Every other device I owned told me the answer before I'd had to think about the question. This one made me actually look at the sky, estimate, commit, and live with the result thirty-six frames at a time, with no undo button and no do-over until the next roll. I've shot maybe forty rolls since that gray afternoon in the backyard, and I still do the calculation by eye every time, even now that I could just buy a meter, because doing it by eye is the part I actually love.
