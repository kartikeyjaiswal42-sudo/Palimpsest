I wrote an article for the school newspaper about the athletic department's budget that got me called into the principal's office and almost got me expelled from the journalism program.

It seemed important at the time. I was a sophomore, newly on the paper's editorial board, and I'd been looking at the budget data that's publicly available. The athletic department had requested a significant funding increase while three academic programs were being cut. The disparity bothered me. I thought it was worth reporting on.

I spent weeks on the article. I interviewed the athletic director, who gave me the official explanation about how athletics builds school spirit and generates revenue. I interviewed the principal, who said the budget cuts were necessary and difficult but unavoidable. I looked at the actual numbers, which showed that the school was investing significantly more in sports than in academics per student.

I wrote the article as objectively as I could, presenting the facts and both sides of the argument. Or I thought I did. What I actually did was write an article that made a clear argument against the budget priorities, even though I tried to frame it neutrally. The facts I chose to highlight made the school look bad. The quotes I selected made the athletic department sound like they were prioritizing sports over education. Every piece was technically true, but the overall effect was damaging.

The article came out on a Friday. By Monday, there were parents calling the school. By Tuesday, the principal called me in.

She wasn't angry exactly, but she was disappointed in a way that felt worse. She said the article was inflammatory. She said I'd used my position as a journalist to make an argument that I was entitled to make as a student, but which I shouldn't have made in the school newspaper. She said the paper was supposed to represent the school fairly, and my article didn't do that.

I tried to argue that the facts were accurate. She said facts without context can lie. She said I'd presented the data in a way that made a specific argument, and that wasn't journalism, that was advocacy. She said if I wanted to advocate for academic programs, I could do that, but not under the banner of the school newspaper.

I was grounded from writing anything for the paper for the rest of the semester. The athletic director requested that I not be allowed to interview him or anyone in his department again. I became the girl who wrote the inflammatory article, and everyone looked at me differently.

The worst part was that she was right. I hadn't been neutral. I'd chosen the facts that supported my argument and presented them as if they were comprehensive. I'd selected quotes that made specific people look bad. I'd framed everything in a way that led readers to a conclusion I'd already reached. That wasn't journalism. That was propaganda with good sources.

I didn't grow from that experience in a simple way. I didn't learn "objectivity is important" and then become a perfectly objective writer. Instead, I learned something messier: that there's no such thing as purely objective reporting, but there is such a thing as honest reporting. My article wasn't dishonest—I didn't make anything up or misquote anyone. But it also wasn't honest about my argument.

What changed was how I thought about responsibility. I had power as someone who had access to a platform. Using that power to make an argument without being clear that I was making an argument was a violation of trust, even if everything I said was technically true.

I'm back to writing for the paper now. I cover sports mostly, which is a deliberate choice to work in an area where I'd be forced to represent both sides fairly because I don't care enough about the outcome to let my bias slip through. My advisor, Mrs. Chen, says I'm a better writer now than I was before the article, but that it cost me something to learn it.

That's the part I still think about. The growth came at a cost. Someone's reputation was damaged because of my lack of responsibility. The school's reputation was damaged. The athletic program felt attacked. Even though I didn't make anything up, I hurt people. And I couldn't take the article back. I couldn't undo that damage.

I think about that sometimes when I'm writing something now. I think about the power I have and whether I'm using it honestly. And most of the time, that stops me from writing things that are true but not fair.
