The binoculars are a hand-me-down from my grandfather, a chunky pair of Bushnells with a strap that's been re-stitched twice, and for two years now they have lived by my back door, next to my shoes, so I can grab them without thinking. Every school day that isn't raining, I leave the house at 6:20, walk the four minutes to the drainage creek behind Lindale Elementary, and stand there until 6:55 watching whatever shows up. I started doing this alone. Then I met Mr. Ferris.

Mr. Ferris is seventy-four and has been birding that same creek for over thirty years, since before the elementary school existed, when the land behind it was still an actual field. He noticed me out there in October of freshman year, said nothing for about a week, and then one morning just started talking to me like we'd already been mid-conversation for years — telling me the difference between a downy and a hairy woodpecker, how to tell a Cooper's hawk from a sharp-shinned by the way it flies, not just the way it looks. I started bringing a notebook because I didn't want to keep asking him the same things twice.

For a year and a half, Mr. Ferris was the only birding authority I had, and I trusted him completely. So when I disagreed with him, in April of this year, it actually mattered to me in a way that a disagreement with a teacher or a classmate wouldn't have.

What happened was this. I saw a warbler in the willows on the east bank, small, yellow-and-black, with a wing pattern I didn't recognize, and I was almost sure — ninety percent sure — that it was a Golden-winged Warbler, a bird that is genuinely rare in our county, maybe one or two confirmed sightings a decade. I wanted to log it on the county list, the shared spreadsheet real birders use, the kind of thing that gets checked by a review committee. Mr. Ferris looked at my photo, which was blurry because my hands were shaking, and said no. Not "I'm not sure." No. He said it was almost certainly a Blue-winged Warbler, which looks similar at a distance and is far more common, and that submitting an unclear photo as a rare bird does real harm to the data other researchers rely on.

I argued with him. I said ninety percent was still ninety percent. He said the county list isn't built on ninety percent, it's built on documentation, and that every birder who's ever submitted a maybe-sighting because they wanted it to be true has made the record worse for everyone who comes after them. I remember feeling something close to betrayed, like he was choosing a rule over what I had actually seen with my own eyes.

I didn't submit it. Not because he convinced me that morning — I walked to school still annoyed — but because I kept turning it over, and by third period I understood that his caution wasn't about doubting me specifically. It was about a discipline he had spent thirty years building, one blurry photo at a time, and I was asking him to let me skip the part where you earn certainty.

I still have the photo. It's probably a Blue-winged Warbler. I'll never fully know, and that used to bother me more than it does now. What I kept from that argument is the binoculars themselves. They weren't good enough to have settled it, and neither was I, and every morning I stand at the creek with them I'm reminded that wanting something to be rare doesn't make it rare. Mr. Ferris still meets me there some mornings. We haven't seen a Golden-winged Warbler since, but I check every willow on that east bank like we might.
