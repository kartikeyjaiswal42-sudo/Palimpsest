The basketball court behind the community center was demolished the summer I turned sixteen. It's gone now and there's just the empty lot, dirt and weeds, occasionally used for overflow parking. But for seven years before that, it was the place where I learned to play basketball, and more importantly, it was the place where I had a significant disagreement with my father that we never resolved.

I taught myself to play on that court. My father used to play when he was young, but he stopped before I was born. He didn't push me to play. I just started shooting around on the court one summer and I liked it. I kept going back. I watched YouTube videos about footwork and shooting form. I practiced the same moves over and over until they became natural. I taught myself the game from the ground up, learning through repetition and failure and gradual improvement.

My father came by the court sometimes and watched me play. He would give me tips, small corrections. He saw potential in me that I didn't see in myself. He started talking about high school basketball programs, about scouts, about the possibility that this casual thing I was doing could become something bigger. He wanted me to get serious about it.

Here's where the disagreement starts. I didn't want to get serious about it. I loved playing basketball on the court with my friends, but the moment it became something my father was invested in, something with expectations, I liked it less. I kept playing, but not with the intensity he wanted. I didn't join any traveling teams. I didn't work with a trainer. I just kept playing casual games on the court.

My father was frustrated by this. He would ask why I wasn't trying harder, why I wasn't joining competitive leagues, why I was wasting potential. I would tell him I was playing because I liked it, not because I wanted to be a basketball player. This made him angry in a way I didn't fully understand. He said I was being lazy. He said I was throwing away an opportunity.

What he couldn't see was that he was trying to make my thing into his thing. He had been a player and had to stop for reasons he never fully explained. I think he saw me as his second chance. But I didn't want to be anyone's second chance. I wanted to just play basketball on a neighborhood court with my friends on summer evenings. That was enough for me.

The court closure made this argument different. Suddenly it wasn't theoretical anymore. The court was actually gone. I couldn't just go back. The city decided the land was more valuable as parking. They took down the basketball hoop and the asphalt and made it a lot. My father seemed almost relieved. He suggested I join a gym, get a trainer, take it seriously now that I didn't have the distraction of casual play.

But I didn't. I was done playing basketball. That sounds dramatic, but it's true. Once the court was gone, I realized how much of my attachment was to that specific place. To the friends I played with there. To the freedom of just showing up and playing without it meaning anything beyond the game itself.

My father never said he was sorry for pushing me. I never said I was sorry for disappointing him. The argument just stopped being active because the condition that created it was gone. He wanted me to be a serious player and I wanted to play casually and now neither of those things was possible on the court that had started it all.

What I learned from teaching myself basketball on that court was something my father couldn't teach me. I learned that the best things are often the ones that happen without external pressure. I learned to play because I wanted to, and that was enough. The moment someone else wanted me to play, it became something else. Something I didn't love anymore.

I think about that court sometimes. I think about my father still wanting me to play competitively even after the court was gone. I think about how some disagreements don't get resolved because they're based on fundamentally different values. He values potential and achievement. I value freedom and pleasure. Both are fine. We just never agreed on which one should matter in this situation. And now the court is gone and we'll probably never resolve it, which is okay. It's just one of those things that remains unresolved between us.
