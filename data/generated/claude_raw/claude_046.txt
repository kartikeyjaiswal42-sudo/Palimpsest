The metronome is the thing I notice first when I open the box. It's small and red and still ticking faintly, even though I shut off all the power years ago. The batteries probably died inside it. I haven't opened this storage box in three years, not since I stopped piano lessons. I'm not sure why I opened it today, except my mom asked if I wanted to sell the pieces to make room in the garage.

I remember that metronome perfectly. It started clicking when I was six and never stopped. 240 beats per minute. I knew that number like I knew my own birthday. My teacher used to set it and say play through this twice and then we'll move on to the next piece, always forward, always faster. The clicking drove me insane by the time I was thirteen. I used to mute it by covering it with my hand or stuffing it under the piano bench. But it would still tick, muffled and determined, and I'd feel like it was mocking me for not being disciplined enough to just accept it.

Opening this box now, I pick up the metronome and it stops. The mechanism is finally dead. And I feel this strange thing in my chest that I'm not expecting—not quite relief, but not sadness either. It's something like recognition.

When I quit piano at sixteen, I told myself it was because I didn't love it anymore. That was true. But it was also because I was tired of measuring myself against that click. Tired of knowing exactly how I stacked up to professional performances I'd listened to. Tired of practicing scales while my friends were outside doing literally anything else. My teacher cried when I told her I was stopping, which made me feel guilty but also angry. She said I had real talent, real potential. I said I had other things I wanted to do. We were both right.

The thing is, quitting taught me something that the metronome never could have. When you stop measuring yourself against an external standard, you have to learn to listen to yourself. I spent the next two years doing almost nothing musical, which felt like betrayal. But then I started playing random things on my friend's ukulele, not because I was any good but because it was quiet and I could do it at my own speed. I started writing weird little songs that nobody would ever hear. I learned that I actually did care about music. I just didn't care about being perfect at it.

I'm taking piano lessons again now, actually. Just one a month, not the weekly performance prep that used to define my life. My new teacher doesn't use a metronome. We talk about pieces I actually want to learn. Sometimes I nail it, sometimes I don't. Nobody's keeping score.

I put the dead metronome back in the box. I don't think I'll sell the piano.
