The object is a paperback copy of Treasure Island, spine cracked in four places, that I read aloud to a hospice patient named Mr. Aldrich every Tuesday for about five months. I volunteered through a program at the hospital my mom works at, mostly reading to patients who wanted company more than they wanted the actual book, and Mr. Aldrich always wanted Treasure Island specifically, no substitutions, even though his memory had gotten bad enough that he rarely remembered where we'd left off from one week to the next.

I got careless because of that. Around February, tired one Tuesday and short on time before I had to leave for a track meet, I skipped ahead almost forty pages, past a section I found boring, telling myself it wouldn't matter since he never remembered the details anyway. I read him the part where Jim Hawkins finds the stockade, skipping the mutiny scene entirely, and got through my visit twenty minutes faster than usual.

He noticed. Not the plot exactly, but something was off to him, some rhythm of the story he could still feel even without holding the specific facts in place, and he asked me, more than once that afternoon, where the ship had gone, why we weren't still at sea, in a tone that wasn't confused so much as quietly unsettled. I told him we'd just moved along in the story, which was true and also not the honest answer, and he let it go because he didn't have the energy that day to push further.

He passed about six weeks later. I never told him what I'd actually done, never went back and read him the section I'd skipped, and I think about that more than almost anything else from that whole year. I had assumed his failing memory gave me a kind of permission to be sloppy, that if the details wouldn't stick anyway, the details didn't matter. What I got wrong is that the story mattering to him was never really about tracking plot points. It was about the specific, complete experience of being read to properly, in order, by someone taking it seriously, and I had quietly decided his disorientation meant that experience didn't need to be complete.

I still have the book. I finished reading it to another patient months later, cover to cover this time, no skipped chapters, partly as an apology I couldn't actually deliver to the person it was owed to. I don't know if it changed anything for that second patient, who probably never noticed the difference either way. It changed something for me, mostly the understanding that care isn't measured by whether someone can catch you cutting corners. It's measured by whether you cut them at all.
