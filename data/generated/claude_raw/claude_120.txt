I started birdwatching for a stupid reason, which is that I wanted a thing. I was fourteen, everyone around me seemed to have an identifiable interest, and I did not, and there was a pair of binoculars in the hall cupboard that had belonged to my late uncle. So I got up early one Saturday and went to the scrubby bit of ground behind the retail park where there is a pond, and I taught myself birds, alone, out of a secondhand Collins guide with somebody else's pencil marks in it.

For about two years I did this before school. Out at six, back by half seven, three or four mornings a week. And for about two years I was, I now realise, doing it wrong in a way that I was completely satisfied with at the time.

What I was doing was collecting. I had a list. I ticked things off. When I saw a bird, the question I asked was "what is it," and once I had an answer I stopped looking, because the transaction was complete. Reed bunting. Tick. I got to eighty-three species by the end of the first year and I was proud of that number the way you are proud of a high score. My knowledge was entirely a knowledge of names and of the four or five diagnostic features you need to attach a name to a shape. Wing bar. Eye stripe. Whether the tail is forked.

The morning it changed was in February of Year 11, and it was not dramatic. There was a heron at the pond, which was not a new bird, I had ticked heron in the first month. I had nothing to do because it was too cold and too early for anything else, so I just watched it, out of boredom, for about twenty-five minutes.

In twenty-five minutes it did not catch anything. It moved four times, each time a few steps, each time slower than I would have believed a living thing could move. Once it stopped mid-stride with a foot lifted and held it there for what I counted as fifty-one seconds. And at some point in there I understood that I had seen herons perhaps forty times and had never once seen a heron do anything, because I had always stopped watching at the moment of identification.

That was the turn. Not "I saw a rare bird." The opposite. I saw an extremely common bird properly for the first time and realised my whole method had been designed to prevent exactly that.

What teaching myself showed me, in the end, is that when there is nobody checking your work, you will unconsciously optimise for whatever you can measure. Nobody told me to keep a list. But a list gives you a number, and a number gives you the feeling of getting better, and so I built two years of practice around the one part of the activity that produced a score. A teacher would have asked me a question I never asked myself: what is this for? An expert would have said, on day three, forget the list, sit down and watch one bird for an hour. Self-teaching is enormously good at building competence and enormously bad at telling you whether the competence is pointed anywhere.

I still go out before school, though now it is two mornings a week because of coursework, and I still have the list, but it has not moved much. I have a second notebook now that is just behaviour — a sparrowhawk that hunts the same hedge on the same side every time, gulls that follow the bin lorry down Farrow Road on Thursdays and not Wednesdays, the fact that our local blackbirds start singing about eleven minutes earlier under the streetlight on the corner than they do fifty metres away in the dark.

None of that is in the Collins guide. That is the point of it.
