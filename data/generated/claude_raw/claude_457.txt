Mr. Aziz has run the tailoring shop on Denton Street for thirty-one years, and I've worked there after school for two of them, mostly repairing the sewing machines in the back room while he handles the actual tailoring up front. The disagreement I still think about started over a machine, a 1962 Singer with a bent needle bar, and it wasn't really about the machine at all.

He wanted to replace the bar with a modern reproduction part, cheap, easy to source, functionally identical for the customer's purposes. I argued we should track down an original replacement, even though it would take weeks and cost three times as much, because the machine was a genuine antique that a collector had brought in specifically hoping to preserve its authenticity, and using a modern part felt, to me, like a kind of quiet dishonesty toward what the owner actually wanted.

Mr. Aziz has forgotten more about sewing machines than I will probably ever learn, and I knew that going in, and I argued with him anyway, more forcefully than I'd argued with him about anything in two years of working there. He said the collector wanted a working machine, not a museum piece, and that I was overcomplicating a straightforward repair out of some romantic idea about authenticity that didn't actually serve the customer. I said the customer had used the word "original" three separate times when he dropped it off, and that ignoring that was a failure to actually listen to what he'd asked for.

We went back and forth on it for most of an afternoon, both of us more stubborn than usual, until he finally said, fine, do it your way, track down the part, but if it takes past the date we promised him, that conversation with the customer is mine to have, not his. I took that as a kind of victory at the time. I spent the better part of three weeks calling parts suppliers and antique-machine forums, found a bar from a salvage machine in Ohio, had it shipped, installed it myself under his supervision, and the collector was thrilled when he picked it up, said something about how rare it was to find a shop that still cared about that kind of detail.

I was right, in the sense that it worked out. But I still think about the argument itself, not the outcome, because I've turned it over enough times to notice something I didn't notice in the moment. Mr. Aziz wasn't wrong that I was overcomplicating it — I was, a little, driven as much by wanting to prove I understood the machine's value as by actually knowing what the customer needed. And I never really acknowledged that to him afterward, because the job succeeded and success has a way of ending an argument without anyone having to admit anything.

What sits with me is that I don't actually know, even now, whether I was right for the right reasons or right by accident, whether the "original part" instinct came from genuine respect for the machine and the customer's wishes or from wanting, at seventeen, to win an argument against a man who'd been doing this since before I was born. Mr. Aziz never brought it up again, and I never asked him what he really thought once it was over, and that unasked question sits in the back room with us most Saturdays now, unspoken, next to the machine itself, which still runs perfectly, original part and all, regardless of which one of us was actually right about why it mattered.
