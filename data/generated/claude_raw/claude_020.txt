I have been translating for my parents at doctors' appointments since I was nine, and for most of that time I thought of it as a chore, roughly on the level of taking out the bins but with more waiting.

The mechanics of it were always the same. We would sit in a room with pale walls and a poster about hand washing. The doctor would talk to me instead of to my mother, which I noticed early and did not have a word for. I would turn what he said into Gujarati, and I would turn her answer into English, and I would leave out the parts I judged unnecessary, and nobody ever checked my work.

That last part is the thing I want to write about, because I did not think about it at all for about six years.

I edited constantly. If my mother described a symptom in a way that sounded, to my nine-year-old ear or my thirteen-year-old ear, embarrassing or rambling, I compressed it. If she said something that I thought would make the doctor think less of her — if she mentioned a home remedy, or that she had waited four months before coming — I softened it or dropped it. I told myself I was being efficient. I was being protective, which felt like the same thing.

I changed my mind about all of it in a room at Sarvodaya Hospital when I was fifteen, in a consultation about my mother's hand.

She had had numbness in two fingers for a while. The doctor asked how long. She said, in Gujarati, a long sentence about how it had started around the time of Diwali when she was doing the cleaning, and how it was worse when she woke up, and how she had thought it was from the cold water, and how her sister in Rajkot had the same thing and it went away.

I said: about four months, worse in the mornings.

Which is a fine translation. It is arguably a better answer than the one she gave. It contains the medically relevant facts, and I was quietly pleased with myself for it.

The doctor nodded and wrote and asked something else, and my mother said something to me, quietly, not for translation — she said, tell him about my sister.

And I said, in English, that her sister had it too.

He put down the pen. He asked how old her sister was, and whether it had been diagnosed, and about six more questions I would never have thought to consider relevant, and the appointment went somewhere completely different from where it had been going.

I sat in that chair and understood that I had been doing this for six years. Six years of appointments where I had decided, at nine and eleven and thirteen, which of my mother's sentences deserved to reach the doctor. I had been standing in the middle of every medical conversation my parents had ever had in this country, editing, with no training and no oversight and total confidence.

I do not know what I lost them. That is the part I think about. There is no way to audit it. There were probably appointments where nothing was missed and there was possibly one where something was.

What changed after that day is that I translate everything now. All of it, including the rambling, including the parts I find embarrassing, including the sister in Rajkot. If it takes longer, it takes longer. I have said, out loud, to a doctor who was looking at his screen, that my mother is not finished.

She is fine, by the way. It was carpal tunnel. The sister in Rajkot had it too.
