I set up my stall at the weekend market at six-thirty in the morning, before the sun was fully up and before most of the other vendors had arrived. I was selling jewelry I'd made myself—mostly beaded necklaces and bracelets, some earrings. I'd been making jewelry for about a year by that point, and I thought I was ready to actually sell it to strangers.

I was very, very wrong.

The first person who came to my stall asked how much for the big necklace. I'd spent six hours beading that necklace. I'd chosen each bead carefully and arranged them in a specific pattern. The materials had cost me maybe eight hundred rupees. I'd never actually calculated what I should charge for the labor and the expertise and the time. I just knew I wanted to make some money, so I said, "Five hundred rupees."

The man immediately left. I didn't sell anything to him.

Over the course of the three-hour market, I sold three pieces. I made 1,200 rupees total, which sounds decent until you realize that two of those sales were at prices I'd set randomly and one was to a friend's mother who was being nice to me. By the time I packed up, I was exhausted and demoralized. I'd done all this work, spent six hours just on one necklace, and I'd essentially made two hundred rupees per hour for the time I'd actually spent selling things. That was lower than minimum wage. That was worse than my regular babysitting gig.

I spent that whole week thinking about how I'd failed at this. Everyone talks about entrepreneurs starting with a product and a market, and I'd had both, and it hadn't worked. Or I hadn't worked, which seemed to be the actual problem.

So I went back the next Saturday determined to do better.

The first thing I did was actually cost out my pieces properly. I looked at how much time I'd spent, how much materials had cost me, and I decided on prices that were realistic. The necklace that I'd originally priced at five hundred rupees? It should have been 1,200 rupees at least. My earrings should have been 300 rupees per pair, not 150.

I thought if I was going to fail, at least I'd fail at a fair price.

The second thing I did was make my stall look better. I got a small backdrop and arranged my pieces more carefully. I made a sign that said the pieces were handmade. I didn't have any capital to invest in this, so I got creative with things I had at home.

I sold eight pieces that Saturday at the new prices. One person actually bargained with me instead of just accepting my prices, which made me feel like I was doing something right. Two people bought multiple things. One woman asked if I did custom orders.

By the fourth week, I was actually making decent money. Not a fortune, but enough that it was worth my time, and enough that I could reinvest in better materials and supplies.

What I'm still realizing, even now, is how much of that initial failure was on me. I had come to the market unprepared. I had underpriced my work. I hadn't thought about what I was actually doing from a business perspective—I'd just thought about it as me trying to sell jewelry. Once I actually treated it like a real endeavor, it worked better. Not perfectly, but better.

The other thing is that I'd quit after one bad day. I'd let that failure convince me that I wasn't good at this, when what actually happened was that I wasn't good at pricing and presentation. Those are learnable things. Once I learned them, the whole thing got better.

I haven't continued with the market stall—I got busy with school and other things. But I've continued making jewelry, and I've continued thinking about what that experience taught me about failing at something and then actually examining why you failed instead of just accepting the failure as final.
