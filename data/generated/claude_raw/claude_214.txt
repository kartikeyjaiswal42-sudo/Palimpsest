I spent a month in the hospital when I was fourteen, and the only meaningful thing I did there was practice drawing in a sketchbook that my mother left on my bedside table.

I was admitted with an infection that had gone systemic, which is a polite way of saying I was really sick and they wanted to monitor me constantly while pumping antibiotics through an IV. The first week was the worst—I was feverish and confused and spent most of the time sleeping or waking up confused about where I was. But by the second week, I was lucid and well enough to be aware of how bored I was.

The hospital tried to keep me entertained. They brought me books, which I couldn't focus on. They brought me movies, which felt pointless. They offered video games, but my dad's laptop wasn't set up for that. My mother, trying to solve the boredom problem, brought me a sketchbook and a set of pencils.

I'd never been a visual person. I liked to read and write. I'd taken one art class and been mediocre at it. I had no reason to think I'd be any good at drawing. But I was stuck in a hospital bed for a month with nothing but time, so I started drawing.

The first sketches were terrible. They were the kind of drawings that you can tell came from someone who doesn't actually understand how things are proportioned. But I kept doing it. Every day. My hand would get tired from the IV, but I'd draw with my other hand or shift my position and keep going.

What happened was that I started to see. Not just with my eyes but with my actual perception. I'd look at the light coming through the hospital window and try to draw it. I'd fail, but then I'd look again and understand something new about how light actually works. I'd try to draw my own hand—the cliché sketch-class exercise—and realize that I couldn't do it because I didn't actually understand how hands are constructed.

Drawing became a way of paying attention to things I'd never actually seen, even though I'd looked at them a thousand times. Every sketch taught me something. I got incrementally better. By the fourth week, I could draw something that looked like a thing instead of just vaguely resembling a thing.

The recovery from the infection was boring and painful and frustrating. The hospital food was bad. The IV in my arm hurt and made everything uncomfortable. I was missing school and feeling isolated from my friends who were moving on without me. But the drawing kept me tethered to something. It gave me a purpose within the purposelessness of lying in a hospital bed.

When I got discharged, I kept drawing. It became my habit, the thing I did instead of wasting time on my phone. I got better. I took actual art classes and discovered that I was actually kind of good at it. Drawing became part of my identity in a way I never expected.

What's weird is that I'm grateful for that month. Which sounds strange because I was in the hospital and that was terrible. But I'm genuinely grateful because I found something there that I wouldn't have found otherwise. In the enforced boredom and the physical limitation, I discovered that I could make something with my hands. That I could look at the world and translate it into an image. That I could see differently.

The hospital stay was the worst experience I've had, and it's also the thing that gave me something important. I think about that sometimes when things are hard. I think about how growth doesn't always come from good circumstances. Sometimes it comes from being stuck somewhere uncomfortable and finding something to do there anyway.
