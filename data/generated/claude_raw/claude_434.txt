Every morning for six months I would wake up and check the sales. It was literally the first thing I did before I even got out of bed or brushed my teeth or had any coffee. Pull up my phone, open the Shopify app, scroll to the orders section, see how many orders came in overnight. Usually it was zero. Sometimes it was one. Once it was three, which I genuinely thought was my breakthrough moment. Like maybe this was the day things turned around. It wasn't.

The phone case business was supposed to change everything. I'd watched some YouTube videos about print-on-demand, found a supplier, designed a few basic cases with clichéd motivational slogans that I thought were clever. "Be yourself, everyone else is taken." "Do what makes you happy." Standard stuff that you see on a thousand Instagram posts. I'd uploaded them and set the price at a markup that seemed reasonable but probably wasn't. Then I'd waited for people to buy them, which they mostly didn't.

This small repeated habit—checking the sales first thing—it became almost meditative. I'd lie in bed and just stare at the number. Zero. I'd think about what that meant. I'd think about changing the listings, adding new designs, marketing differently. I'd make a mental note to fix things. And then I'd get out of bed and go to school and completely forget about it until the next morning.

Six months of that. Six months of a slow accumulation of not-happening. I sold maybe twenty cases total, which after the supplier cost me money instead of making money. My parents didn't say I told you so, but I could see them thinking it. My friends asked how the business was going and I'd say fine, great, building it up, knowing they could tell I was lying.

What's strange is that those six months taught me more about myself than any successful business could ever have taught me. Not in a motivational way where I'm saying "failure is learning" or some clichéd thing like that. That's the kind of inspirational sentiment that makes me want to throw up. It's true, probably, but it's also the most obvious way to put it and the most false-sounding when you're actually living it.

What I mean is that the repetition of checking the sales and seeing nothing, day after day, slowly wore away my resistance to the idea that maybe I wasn't an entrepreneur. Maybe I wasn't going to be the person who built something. Maybe I was just a person. Just me. And that person liked doing homework and making art and talking to friends, not constantly trying to figure out how to sell phone cases to strangers.

At six months I just stopped. I closed the shop. I stopped checking the sales. And for like two weeks I felt sad about it, like I'd given up on myself. But then I realized what I was actually grieving was the idea of myself as someone who was doing this thing. Not the actual thing. Just the idea.

It sounds depressing but it was actually liberating. I spent so much energy maintaining the story that I was an entrepreneur when I could have been using that energy to do things I actually cared about. I could have been making art that I made for myself instead of art I was trying to sell. I could have been learning things because I wanted to learn them, not because they looked good on a business plan.

The small repeated habit of checking the sales every morning was the real thing I was building, and what I was building was resilience to not having what I thought I wanted. Or maybe I was building self-knowledge. Every morning I was confirming: this is not for you. This is not who you are. And that confirmation, repeated enough times, finally stuck.

Now I do things that matter to me without needing them to look like a business or a side hustle or an achievement. I make art when I want to. I write things just for myself. I think about things deeply without rushing to monetize the thinking. I'm not an entrepreneur and I've made peace with that. That six months of morning phone checks taught me something fundamental—it's infinitely better to do nothing than to keep performing at something that doesn't align with who you actually are. The performance is the real waste of time.
