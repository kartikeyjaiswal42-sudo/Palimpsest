My mother wanted me to learn traditional dance for the Navratri festival. This was not a suggestion. This was something that was going to happen, whether I wanted it to or not. The festival was in three months. There was a local dance teacher. My mother had already enrolled me in the classes.

I was absolutely not interested.

I was fifteen and I had approximately zero interest in wearing a costume and dancing on stage in front of people. It seemed humiliating. It seemed like a waste of time. It seemed like the kind of thing that happened to girls who wanted to participate in cultural events, and I was not that girl. I made this very clear to my mother.

She made it clear that my opinion was noted and overruled.

So I went to the classes, and I was angry about it. I went through the motions. I learned the steps. I showed up to rehearsals because I had to. I was the sullen teenager who clearly didn't want to be there, and everyone could see it. The dance teacher would try to encourage me to get more enthusiastic about the choreography, and I'd just do the minimum required.

A week before the festival, we were doing the final full-run rehearsal. I was standing in position with the other dancers, waiting for the music to start. The teacher came over and said something that I think I needed to hear, even though I was annoyed about it at the time. She said, "You know, everyone can tell that you're here because you have to be. But you're also talented. You're moving well. You could actually do this really well if you cared about doing it well."

I don't think she was trying to shame me. I think she was actually trying to invite me into the experience instead of leaving me standing outside of it, resenting it.

Something about her comment actually landed. During that full-run rehearsal, I decided to actually try. I put energy into the movements. I paid attention to the timing and the precision instead of just hitting the basic steps. I danced like I actually wanted to be there.

And it felt completely different.

The physicality of the dance became something I could appreciate. The coordination with the other dancers felt like something we were creating together. The costume stopped feeling like humiliation and started feeling like I was part of something larger than myself. The music, which I'd barely been hearing before, suddenly seemed to be the thing that everything else was organized around.

For that rehearsal, I actually had fun.

The actual performance at the festival was chaotic and there were definitely technical problems and not everyone in the audience was paying attention. But I was dancing, not performing for people who weren't watching. The difference was that I'd finally chosen to be present in what I was doing.

Afterward, my mother was so proud. She kept saying she'd known I'd be good at it if I gave it a chance. My father said I moved with confidence. People who came to the festival told me I was the most engaged dancer they watched.

The thing that changed my mind wasn't my mother forcing me to do it. It was the teacher reflecting back to me that I was capable of doing something well, even if I'd been approaching it with resentment. That reflection made me reconsider whether I actually wanted to be miserable about the whole thing or whether I could choose to engage differently.

I'm not a dancer. I'm not taking classes anymore or performing in other festivals. But that experience taught me something about how my own resistance and resentment gets in the way of things I might actually enjoy if I approached them differently. It taught me that sometimes the barrier to enjoying something is just my own attitude about it, and I have more control over that than I thought.
