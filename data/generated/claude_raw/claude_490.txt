I couldn't sleep for a year, and that year fundamentally changed how I understand myself and what makes me who I am.

It started junior year with ordinary stress—tests, friend drama, the general anxiety of being sixteen in a world that demands you figure out your future. I'd lie in bed and my mind would just start racing. Not about anything specific, just a constant low-level anxiety that made sleep impossible. I'd lie there for hours, watching the clock, getting more frustrated as it approached midnight and then one a.m. and then two.

I tried all the standard things. No screens before bed. Exercise. Meditation. Melatonin. Warm milk. Chamomile tea. I'd read about sleep hygiene and implement it perfectly and still lie awake, my brain running at full speed while my body was exhausted.

By month three, the insomnia had become its own kind of anxiety. I was anxious about not sleeping, which made sleeping impossible. It was a cycle. I'd go to bed, lie awake for hours, get frustrated, get more anxious, sleep stay impossible. During the day, I was exhausted and irritable. I could barely focus in school. I was falling apart.

My parents took me to doctors. There was nothing physically wrong with me. A psychiatrist wanted to prescribe medication, but I resisted. I don't know why exactly, except that I felt like fixing the insomnia with medication would mean I was giving up on figuring out the actual problem. I felt like there was something I was supposed to learn from the inability to sleep.

By month six, I'd stopped fighting it. I accepted that I wasn't going to sleep eight hours a night. On good nights, I got four. On bad nights, I got two. I started structuring my life around what I could do on two hours of sleep. I lowered my expectations. I stopped trying so hard. I stopped seeing myself as someone who was broken and needed fixing.

That's when everything shifted.

Once I accepted that I was awake, the anxiety about being awake went away. I could be awake without the narrative that this was something wrong. I'd lie in bed and instead of fighting it, I'd just... think. About my life. About who I was. About what mattered to me. It was like the constant noise of the day went away and I could finally hear my own thoughts.

I realized during one of those sleepless nights that I'd been living my life based on what I thought I was supposed to want rather than what I actually wanted. I was supposed to be ambitious, to know my future, to be working toward college. And I did want some of those things. But I also wanted other things that seemed less impressive or important—time to think, time to create, time to just be without doing.

I realized I'd been anxious about sleep because I'd been anxious about everything. And the insomnia was actually my mind's way of forcing me to slow down and pay attention to things I'd been ignoring.

By month ten, my sleep had started to gradually improve. I wasn't "fixed," exactly. I still only got five or six hours on average. But it was better. And more importantly, I wasn't suffering about it anymore. I wasn't seeing myself as broken.

The year of insomnia taught me something about myself that I think is important. I'm someone who needs quiet and space to process. I'm someone whose mind works better when I'm not fighting my own nature. I'm someone who needed permission to not be constantly productive.

Now, a year later, I sleep mostly normally. Sometimes the old patterns come back for a week or two, and I panic briefly before reminding myself that I survived a year without sleep. One week is nothing.

But the person I became during that year is still here. That's my baseline now. I'm someone who understands that anxiety about being anxious is often worse than the actual anxiety. I'm someone who believes that sometimes our minds know something our conscious minds don't, and it's worth listening to that.

My identity now includes the fact that I went through that and came out the other side different. It's not a tragedy that scarred me. It's not a triumph that I overcame. It's just something that happened that changed how I understand myself. And that turned out to be necessary.
