The spokeshave chattered and the cedar came off in a torn crescent instead of a curl. I blew the dust away, turned the tool around, tried pulling instead of pushing, and it chattered again.

Uncle Deb took it out of my hands without asking. He turned it over, ran his thumb across the edge of the blade the way you test a knife you don't trust, and gave it back.

"It's sharp," I said. "I honed it Tuesday."

"It's sharp," he agreed. "You're pushing it."

That was the whole lesson. Then he went back to the strongback, where he was fairing the fourth station by eye, taking a shaving off here, a shaving off there, standing back, crouching, sighting down the row of molds like a man looking down a rifle barrel.

I had brought the plans. That was my contribution to the canoe: a library book of tables, offsets in feet and inches and eighths, half-breadths listed at every station, and a printout I made in the school computer lab where I had converted all of it into millimetres, because millimetres do not require you to add fractions with unlike denominators. I believed in that printout the way some people believe in scripture. A boat, I thought, was a mathematical object that happened to be made of wood. Be careful enough with the numbers and the shape simply arrives.

Uncle Deb had built four canoes and had never read a table of offsets in his life.

On the second Saturday I measured station four with the steel rule and found it two millimetres proud. I told him. He said, fine, take it off, and handed me the block plane, and I took it off, and I felt the specific pleasure of being right in a way that could be verified.

Then we sprang a batten along the molds. It's a thin strip of spruce, twelve feet of it, and you bend it around the frames and it finds the shape a plank would want to lie in. Where the batten touched, the curve was true. Where it lifted, it wasn't. It lifted at station four. My two millimetres had put a flat spot in the sheer that no number in the book had predicted and no measurement could have found, because the book gave me points and the boat wanted a line.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. He shimmed the mold back with a strip of cereal box and sighted down it again and grunted, and we went on.

I kept the printout. I still used it. But after that I checked it against the batten, and when they disagreed I stopped assuming the batten was wrong, which took me most of July.

In August the spokeshave finally did what it was supposed to do. I stopped forcing it, let the bevel find its own angle, and a shaving came off the gunwale a full arm's length long, curling over my wrist, thin enough that the workshop light came through it green.

Uncle Deb looked over. He didn't say anything about that either.
