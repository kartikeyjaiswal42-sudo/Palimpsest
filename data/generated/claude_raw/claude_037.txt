"You didn't even ask me," Wren said, and she said it quietly, which was somehow worse than if she'd yelled, because Wren yells about everything, Wren has yelled about socks being the wrong shade of white, and this time she just sat on the edge of her bed with the port access kit still open on the nightstand and said it like she was too tired to yell.

I had called the nurse line without telling her. Wren has cystic fibrosis, and that week her oxygen sats had been dipping in a way that scared me — not scared her, she insisted, but scared me — and I'd waited until she was in the shower and called our care coordinator, a woman named Deb who I'd spoken to probably two hundred times over six years, and asked whether we should push up her clinic visit. Deb said yes. I told my parents. By the time Wren got out of the shower, there was already a plan in motion that she hadn't been part of making.

She's fourteen. I'm seventeen. In the specific math of our house, that three-year gap has meant I've been half a sister and half something else for most of her life — the one who learned to do chest physio before I learned to parallel park, the one who can recognize a bad cough from a normal one through a closed door. I have never resented this, not really, not in a way I could point to and name. But somewhere in those six years I also started making decisions about her body without asking her first, and I told myself this was love, and mostly I still think it was, except for the part where I never checked whether it was also something else.

Our home health aide, Marisol, has worked with our family for four years, and she's the one whose opinion I trust more than almost anyone's outside my parents. She was there the day Wren found out about the call. I expected Marisol to back me up — I'd done the responsible thing, hadn't I, caught something early, gotten ahead of it — and instead she pulled me into the kitchen and told me, not unkindly, that I'd made a mistake.

"She's not a a problem you're managing," Marisol said. "She's a person whose lungs happen to be the thing you're managing. Those aren't the same, and you're old enough now that the difference matters."

I argued with her. I actually argued, standing in our kitchen with the kettle going, saying that Wren doesn't always make the safest choice for herself, that someone has to be the one who calls it in, that if I'd waited to ask permission we might have lost days we didn't have to lose. Marisol didn't say I was wrong about the sats. She said I was wrong about the asking. She said the whole architecture of Wren's illness, from diagnosis at two years old onward, has been built by adults deciding things about her body for her, doctors and nurses and parents and, now, me, and that somewhere in all that necessary managing, nobody had left her a single decision that was actually hers to make about her own health.

I didn't agree with her in the kitchen. I still don't know if I fully agree with her now. Some part of me still believes that if I'd asked Wren first and she'd said no, and something had gone wrong that week, I would never have forgiven myself, and I don't think that fear is unreasonable given what I've watched her body do to her over the years, given what CF actually does when it moves fast.

But I keep coming back to her face on the edge of the bed. Not angry. Just tired in a way that had nothing to do with her lungs.

I asked her, a few weeks later, carefully, what she would have wanted instead. She thought about it longer than I expected. She said she didn't know exactly — that she probably would have said yes to the call anyway, that this wasn't really about the call at all. She said what she wanted was to have been in the room when the decision got made, even if the decision ended up the same. Even if I was right about the sats. Even if speed mattered.

I still don't fully know what to do with that. Marisol was right about something I can name and can't yet undo, and I think about it most when Wren's oxygen monitor beeps in the next room and my hand is already reaching for my phone before I've thought to knock on her door first.
