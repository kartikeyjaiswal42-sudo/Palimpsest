I realized something about myself in the middle of an argument with my history teacher that I still haven't recovered from. It was a small thing, really, an argument about a textbook, about whether a particular historical narrative was accurate. But it revealed something about how I approach learning and how that approach sometimes breaks people's trust in me.

Mr. Patterson was teaching us about the British colonial period in India, and the textbook we were using presented the colonization as this neat narrative of progress and civilization. It talked about the railways the British built and the education system they introduced, and it treated these things like gifts. There was a sentence that really bothered me: "The British brought modernity to India." I raised my hand and asked if that was true, and Mr. Patterson said yes, essentially, that was the historical consensus.

I wasn't satisfied with that answer. So I went home and I did my own research. I found sources that contradicted the textbook. I found Indian historians writing about extractive colonialism and cultural destruction and the deliberate underdevelopment that made India dependent on British goods. I found data about how the colonization had actually impoverished the region, even as it built railways.

The next day, I came to class with my sources, and I told Mr. Patterson that the textbook was wrong. I didn't ask if it was possible that the textbook was wrong. I said it was wrong, and here was my evidence. I was seventeen years old, and I had done one afternoon of research, and I had decided that my textbook—written by professional historians, vetted by academic publishers—was simply incorrect.

He didn't handle it perfectly. He got defensive. He said that the textbook represented the standard historical interpretation, and while I was welcome to my opinion, opinion wasn't the same as scholarship. I told him that his opinion was literally just the opinion of the textbook writers, and what was the difference? It became an argument very quickly.

I won the argument, if we're being technical about it. He agreed that the textbook presented a particular perspective, that there were other valid historical interpretations, that my sources were legitimate. But I realized afterward that I had lost something in the winning. He stopped engaging with me the way he had before. He would answer my questions but wouldn't expand or discuss. Something had broken.

What I learned—and I'm still learning this—is that being right and being someone who learns well are not the same thing. I had the facts on my side, probably. But I didn't have the humility to ask questions, to be genuinely curious about why the textbook said what it said, to assume good faith in my teacher's instruction. I just wanted to be the person who was smart enough to catch the mistake.

I think about that a lot now. I think about how I approached learning as this combative thing, like education was something you had to win rather than something you participated in with other people. I think about what I might have learned from Mr. Patterson if I had approached him differently. If I had asked "What do you think about this?" instead of declaring him wrong.

The thing I still think about is that he was never quite the same with me again. He would help me, but something about the relationship had changed. And I get it now in a way I didn't then. I had made him feel small, and it didn't matter that I was right about the history. I had learned something from his mistake, but what I had really done was humiliate him in front of his class.

I don't regret learning history more accurately. But I regret how I went about it. I regret treating my education like a conquest instead of a conversation.
