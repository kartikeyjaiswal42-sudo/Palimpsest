My grandmother's tailoring shop is the only place I feel like I know what I'm doing, which is why my mother hates that I spend so much time there.

I started going after school when I was thirteen, mostly because my grandmother was lonely and because my mother was always working. Nani's shop was small, crammed into the ground floor of a building that had seen better decades. It had sewing machines from three different eras, bolts of fabric stacked floor to ceiling, and a smell that was all hers—old fabric, machine oil, and the faint smell of the tea she drank constantly.

Nani taught me to sew, to use the industrial machines, to understand how seams work and why a straight stitch is more important than it looks. But after a few months of that, I got distracted by the machines themselves. One of them had a tension problem that made her angry. Another made a sound that suggested something was about to break. I started asking her if I could fix them instead of sewing on them.

By the time I was fifteen, I was the person who maintained the machines. I learned to oil them properly, to replace needles, to understand the difference between a mechanical problem and a threading problem. I became the person Nani called when something wasn't working right. Customers started asking for me. "Is the machine boy here?" they'd ask, not knowing my name but knowing that I was the one who could make their broken machines work.

My mother started getting angry about it when I was in tenth grade. She said I was wasting time. She said I should be focused on school, on college prep, on something that had a future. The tailoring shop was dying, she said. Everyone bought cheap clothes now. Nani was only keeping it open because she was stubborn and nostalgic. Why would I invest my energy there?

We'd fight about it, not big fights, but steady ones. She'd say something, I'd defend myself, and nothing would change. She thought I should be volunteering at a law firm or shadowing a doctor or doing something that looked good on college applications. Instead, I was getting machine oil stains on my clothes and coming home smelling like old fabric.

What she didn't understand was that the tailoring shop was the only place I felt competent. At school, I was okay. I got decent grades, wasn't unpopular, wasn't popular. But I was never the best at anything. In my mother's eyes, I was supposed to be ambitious, which meant I was supposed to want something big and impressive. A lawyer, a doctor, something that impressed people at dinner parties.

But at Nani's shop, I was the expert. Customers trusted me with machines they cared about. Nani relied on me. I knew exactly what to do when a machine wasn't working, and I could make it work. That feeling of competence, of being genuinely needed and actually useful, was addictive.

The fight came to a head last year. My mother said I had to choose: spend my time on something with a real future, or accept that I was wasting my potential. She said she hadn't worked this hard to give me opportunities so I could end up like Nani, running a dying shop in a dying business.

She didn't say it to be cruel. She said it because she loves me and because she believes success looks a certain way. But what she was saying, underneath that, was that my competence in Nani's shop didn't count as real.

I didn't choose, actually. Or I chose by continuing to do what I was doing. I kept working with the machines. I'm still getting into college, and my grades are still fine. But my mother and I don't talk about the shop anymore. There's an agreement we've reached where I just don't mention it, and she doesn't ask.

Nani is seventy-eight now. The machines keep breaking down. I'm the only person young enough and skilled enough to fix them. But I don't know what happens after. I don't know if I'll take over the shop, or if she'll close it, or if I'll have to choose between my mother's definition of success and my own. All I know is that when I'm in that shop, with my hands on a machine and the sound of needles running in the background, I know exactly who I'm supposed to be. And I don't feel like that anywhere else.
