My grandmother used to grow chillies in Guwahati, on a strip of land behind the house she left when she moved here to live with us, and she talks about that garden more than she talks about almost anything else from her old life. Two years ago, for her seventy-fourth birthday, I decided I was going to grow her a full crop of chillies on our apartment balcony as a surprise, ready by the time she came back from a month visiting my uncle. I had never grown anything in my life. I figured how hard could it actually be, and that confidence is basically the whole story of what went wrong.

I bought six pots, some Kashmiri chilli seeds and some bird's eye seeds I found online because I wanted variety, and I started them in March, giving myself what I thought was plenty of time. I watered them every single day without fail, which I now know was the first real mistake, because chilli plants apparently prefer to dry out somewhat between waterings, and what I was doing was slowly drowning the roots while feeling proud of my consistency. I also used regular potting soil from the hardware store instead of anything with proper drainage, so the water had nowhere to go, and by May the leaves on four of the six plants had started curling and yellowing in a way I assumed, at the time, meant they needed more water, so I gave them more.

By the time my grandmother came home, two plants were dead outright and the other four were struggling, pale and stunted, with maybe a dozen small green chillies between all of them combined, nothing like the abundant, almost aggressive growth she'd described from her own garden. I hadn't told her about the project beforehand, so the surprise I revealed on the balcony that first evening was six sad pots that I had to explain were supposed to be a birthday gift, three months in the making, that had mostly failed.

She didn't laugh at me, though I think a part of me expected her to. She walked along the pots, picked up a handful of the soil, let it fall through her fingers, and said, quietly, that I'd drowned them, the way you'd say it about a fact rather than a failing. Then she asked why I hadn't asked her for help in the first place, since she'd grown these exact plants for probably fifty years before I was even born. I didn't have a good answer. I think I wanted it to be entirely mine, some kind of gift that didn't need her input to work, which is a strange thing to want when the whole point of the gift was to remind her of something she already knew everything about.

We replanted that same week, together this time, in terracotta pots with drainage holes she picked out herself, watering on a schedule she set based on how the soil actually felt rather than a fixed daily habit. The second attempt worked. We had chillies by August, not a huge crop, but real ones, and she used a handful of them in a curry that I thought tasted almost exactly like what she'd described from Guwahati, though I have no real way of knowing that for certain.

I still think about the six dead pots more than I think about the ones that survived. Not because the eventual chillies didn't matter, but because the failure taught me something the success never could have: that wanting to give someone a piece of their own life back requires actually asking them how it worked, not assuming I could reconstruct it out of confidence and an internet search. I got that wrong first. I don't think I'll get it wrong that particular way again.
