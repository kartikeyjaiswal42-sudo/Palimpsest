I started the recycling program in ninth grade because I was angry. Not angry at any one person or thing in particular, but just generally angry at the world for being wasteful and stupid and headed toward environmental collapse. I was that kid who'd watched one too many documentaries about the Great Pacific garbage patch and the rate of species extinction. And I had this rock-solid conviction that I, personally, was going to fix this through the power of my own passion and some blue recycling bins that I could convince the administration to buy.

The school's existing recycling setup was nonexistent. We threw everything in the trash. Plastic, paper, aluminum—all of it went to the landfill. It seemed like the most easily solvable problem in the world. So I did what every activist teenager does. I made a PowerPoint. I researched local waste management companies. I calculated the amount of garbage our school produced annually and translated it into environmental impact. I was going to present this to the administration and they were going to be so impressed that they'd immediately implement my recycling program.

The presentation was actually good. I knew that at the time and I know it now. I had the numbers. I had the plan. I had sources and citations and a timeline. The assistant principal listened to me politely and said they'd consider it. Three weeks later, I got approval to start a pilot program in the cafeteria. Three blue bins. That's what they gave me.

I was euphoric. This was happening. This was real.

For about a month, I was completely into it. I cleaned out the bins multiple times a day. I made laminated signs about what goes where—detailed signs with pictures and everything. I talked to the cafeteria manager about sorting and contamination. I set up an actual system where the recycling got picked up weekly and actually transported to a facility instead of just sitting in a closet accumulating smell. It was working. The bins were getting genuinely full with recyclables. People were using them. Students I didn't even know were walking past and putting things in the right bins. It felt like proof of concept.

Then I got busy. Sophomore year hit with classes and clubs and actual schoolwork and I had less time to babysit the bins. And that's when the system broke down. People started putting trash in the recycling. The bins were overflowing. Without someone actively managing it, nobody cared enough to recycle correctly. And if it's contaminated, the facility won't actually recycle it anyway. It all goes to the landfill. My whole system failed because I wasn't there to enforce it.

I didn't notice at first. I was in physics and struggling to understand waves and I just kind of forgot about the recycling bins. When I finally went back to check on them, months had passed. They were disgusting. Clearly nobody had sorted properly since I stopped paying attention. And the administration decided that since the pilot program had failed, they weren't going to expand it. They weren't even going to keep the pilot going. The bins disappeared.

I should have just owned it completely. I should have gone to the administration and said "I started this and I failed to maintain it and I need help making it work" and asked for actual support or resources. But instead I got angry. I got angry at students for not taking it seriously enough. I got angry at the administration for not caring enough. I got angry at the entire system itself for being designed in a way that required an individual's constant effort and attention to function properly.

The anger was misplaced. The truth is that I hadn't actually solved a problem. I'd created a system that couldn't function without me, and then I abandoned it. That's not activism. That's just performance. I wanted to be the person who saved the environment. What I actually was the person who made a mess and then left.

I'm not against environmental action or sustainability. But I learned something real about how change actually works. Systems need buy-in from multiple people and infrastructure and actual institutional support and funding. One passionate teenager with a PowerPoint and good intentions cannot fix anything sustainable on their own. That's a genuinely hard lesson but it's absolutely true. I wish I'd learned it sooner, and I definitely wish I'd learned it with less collateral damage to the program and the school's willingness to try other initiatives.
