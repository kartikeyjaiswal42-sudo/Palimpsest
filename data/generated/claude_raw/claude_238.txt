I have a collection of forty-two subway maps from cities around the world. They live in a portfolio folder under my bed, and my dad thinks they should mean something about my future, like they're evidence that I'm going to be an urban planner or a geographer or something that makes sense. He's wrong. They mean that I got obsessed with something and nobody in my family knew how to talk to me about it.

The obsession started with the Tokyo Metro map, which my friend Priya brought back from her family trip. It was beautiful in a way I didn't expect a map to be. The lines were color-coded perfectly. The geometry of it was almost perfect—not perfectly symmetrical, but organized in a way that felt intentional. I asked her if I could keep it, and she said yes, probably assuming I would look at it once and forget about it.

I looked at it every day. I studied it. I tried to understand the logic of why certain stations existed, why the lines crossed the way they did, why Tokyo had solved the problem of urban transit in a particular way. Then I started collecting maps from other cities. London, Berlin, Paris, Seoul, Singapore, São Paulo. Each one had a different solution to the same problem: how do you move people through a city efficiently and beautifully?

My dad saw the collection growing and said, "Oh, you're interested in city planning?" And I said no, and then he looked confused because he couldn't understand why I would care about something that wasn't leading anywhere. My mom asked if I wanted to learn about architecture, as if that might be a gateway to understanding what I was doing. My little brother just said they were cool and left it at that, which was the most honest response.

The thing is, I couldn't explain it to them because I couldn't explain it to myself. It wasn't about planning or architecture or travel. It was about seeing all these different ways that humans had solved the same problem, and each solution reflected something about the city's values and history and priorities. The Tokyo Metro was elegant and efficiency-focused. The London Underground was historical and sprawling. The Paris Metro was art deco and romantic. The Seoul Metro was modern and aggressively practical.

But I was also looking at them because they were beautiful, and I was lonely, and these maps of distant cities felt like permission to think about something else, something that didn't have anything to do with high school or friendship groups or whether anyone thought I was weird. I could spend an hour studying the Reykjavik Bus System and nobody could tell me I was wasting my time because it was clearly not something I should be wasting time on, which meant it was private, which meant it was safe.

My parents eventually had a conversation where they decided this was either a sign of genius or a sign that I needed to get out more, and they weren't sure which. They tried to be supportive by enrolling me in a summer course about urban development, which I hated because now my hobby had become a Thing I Was Doing and it stopped being mine.

I still have the forty-two maps. I haven't added any new ones in over a year, which probably means the obsession is dying, which is sad because I had hoped it meant something, that there was some version of my future where this mattered. But mostly it just means I got very interested in something and then moved on, which is what people do.

What I wish is that my dad had just asked me why I liked them instead of trying to turn it into evidence that I was destined for something. Sometimes people are just interested in things because those things are interesting, and that's allowed to be enough.
