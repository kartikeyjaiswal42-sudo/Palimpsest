There is a cardboard box in my garage with the words THANK YOU EASTSIDE! written on it in green marker, in my handwriting, in letters that get smaller toward the right because I ran out of room. The exclamation mark is doing a lot of work. I made forty of those boxes over a weekend in October, cutting the tops off and lettering each one at the kitchen table while my sister complained about the smell of the marker. Thirty-nine of them came back with something in them. Most came back with less than a third.

I had promised four thousand items. I had said the number out loud, at a school assembly, into a microphone, with a slide behind me. We collected eleven hundred and sixty-two.

The food drive was mine in the way that a thing is yours when nobody stops you. Our town's pantry had put out a notice in September that their shelves were the emptiest they'd been in six years, and I read it and thought: this is arithmetic. Twelve hundred students. Four items each. Four thousand eight hundred. I rounded down to four thousand because I wanted to sound reasonable. I built a spreadsheet. I assigned boxes to homerooms. I designed a leaderboard. I genuinely believed the hard part was logistics.

The hard part was not logistics. The hard part was that a fourteen-year-old has to remember on a Tuesday morning, while also remembering a gym kit and a permission slip, to have asked their parent on Monday night to buy an extra can of something, and then to carry that can on a bus. I had built a system that assumed everyone's home life was a small warehouse with a willing quartermaster. About a fifth of our school qualifies for free lunch. I did not think about that when I made the leaderboard. I put the leaderboard up in the main corridor. Homeroom 9C, whose catchment is mostly the estate behind the rail line, was last for three weeks in letters two inches high.

That is the thing I actually carry. Not the number I missed. The leaderboard.

I took it down in the fourth week, after a teacher named Ms. Adeyemi asked me, without any particular edge in her voice, what I thought a kid in 9C felt walking past it. I said I hadn't thought about it. She said, that's the honest answer, and left it there, which was worse than being told off.

What I did next is the part I would want anyone to know about me. I did not abandon the drive and I did not pretend it had gone well. I rewrote it. We stopped counting by homeroom and started counting as one school total against one bar drawn on butcher paper. We added a thing where you could donate an hour of sorting instead of an item, and about seventy people did, including six from 9C. We set up a table where the pantry itself could tell us what it actually needed, which turned out to be nothing like what people give — not beans, which they drown in, but cooking oil, nappies, tinned fish, shampoo. I had spent a month collecting things that were easy to give rather than things that were needed. Nobody had asked me to check. I hadn't checked.

The second drive, in March, collected two thousand nine hundred, and about six hundred of those were on the pantry's actual list. Still short of four thousand. I stopped promising numbers.

I keep the one box that came back empty. Somebody had written under my green letters, in pencil, sorry, in small handwriting, no name. I do not know who it was and I have decided not to try to find out. It sits in the garage with the flaps folded in.

I want to study public policy, and the reason is not that I am good at organising things. It is that I now know exactly how it feels to build something well-intentioned that quietly humiliates the people it was meant to help, and to not notice for a month, and to have to be told. That is not a talent. It is closer to a scar. But I would rather apply somewhere with it than without it, because it is the only reason I now ask, before anything else: who does this make feel worse, and have I bothered to look?
