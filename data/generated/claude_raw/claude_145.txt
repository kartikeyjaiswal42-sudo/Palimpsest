My dad was out of work for fourteen months after the plant where he'd been a maintenance supervisor for nineteen years closed down with about six weeks' notice. I was in ninth grade when it happened, and for a long stretch of that first year I was mostly just embarrassed by it, in a way I'm not proud of now. I stopped inviting friends over on weekday afternoons because he'd be home, in the garage usually, and I'd invented some vague sense that a dad being home in the middle of a Tuesday was evidence of something wrong with our family that I didn't want anyone else to see.

He bought a dead 1974 Honda CB360 off a classified ad about two months into the unemployment, for almost nothing, because the guy selling it just wanted it out of his garage. I thought it was a bad idea at the time — we needed money, not a broken motorcycle taking up space — and I said so, more than once, in the kind of blunt way fourteen-year-olds say things they haven't thought all the way through. He didn't really argue back. He just started taking the engine apart on a folding table, piece by piece, cataloging everything in labeled containers, and kept doing it whether or not I approved.

I started actually paying attention around month five, mostly by accident. I'd gone out to the garage to find a tool for something unrelated and ended up watching him explain, half to me and half to himself, how the valve timing worked, why the carburetor needed rebuilding before anything else made sense, what each gasket was actually sealing against. I didn't plan to stay. I ended up out there for two hours that first afternoon, and I genuinely didn't notice the time passing until my mom called us both in for dinner and I realized it had gotten dark outside without me registering it at all.

That became the turn, though I didn't recognize it as one at the time. I started going out to the garage most days after school, not because I felt sorry for him or because I thought I should support the family project, but because I actually wanted to understand how an engine that hadn't run in a decade could be brought back to functioning, piece by piece, through nothing but patience and correct sequence. I read manuals I didn't understand half of at first. I asked questions that annoyed him because they interrupted whatever he was doing. Somewhere in there, my whole understanding of what those fourteen months were flipped over. I stopped seeing my dad's unemployment as this shameful gap I needed to hide from friends and started seeing it as the specific stretch of time that handed me the one subject I have never once gotten bored of since.

The bike ran again in month twelve, backfiring badly the first three tries and then, on the fourth, catching and idling rough but steady in our driveway while my dad and I both just stood there not saying anything for a solid minute. He found a new job two months after that, doing similar work at a different company, and the CB360 has sat mostly finished in our garage ever since, though I've since pulled the carburetor apart and rebuilt it myself twice more, once because it needed it and once because I wanted an excuse to do it again.

I think about those fourteen months completely differently now than I did while they were happening. At the time I experienced my dad's joblessness as something to be embarrassed about, something that made our family visibly, temporarily broken in a way I wanted to hide. What I understand now is that it was also the only period in my life with enough unstructured time in it for a fourteen-year-old to accidentally discover, over a folding table in a cold garage, the one thing that can make an entire evening disappear without me noticing. I still don't fully know how to hold both of those truths about the same fourteen months. I just know I wouldn't trade the second one to undo the first.
