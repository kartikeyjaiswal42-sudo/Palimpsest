The place is a corridor. Specifically it is the third-floor corridor outside room 3.12, between the fire door and the noticeboard nobody has updated since the year seven trip to Bruges, and the reason I feel most myself there is that it is where I have spent two years arguing with Mr Halloran about a textbook.

The textbook is Bennett and Shaw, Britain and the Wider World 1750–1900, fourth edition, and the argument started in the second week of Year Twelve over one sentence on page 84. The sentence said that the East India Company "gradually assumed administrative responsibilities in Bengal." I put my hand up and said that assumed was doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence. Mr Halloran said, go on. I said that you assume a debt or a name, you do not assume a province, and the word was hiding a war. He said that was a fair point and we should move on because there were forty minutes and three centuries left.

That should have been the end of it. Instead I found him at the end of the lesson and said the thing I had actually wanted to say, which was that if the book was wrong about that it was probably wrong about other things and how were we supposed to know which. He looked at his watch, which I thought meant I was being dismissed, and then he sat on the edge of a desk and talked for eleven minutes about why textbooks are written in the passive voice.

After that it became a habit. Not a scheduled thing. I would catch him in the corridor between periods, four minutes at most, and say: page 112, why does it say tensions arose, whose tensions, arose from where. He would say something like, all right, but if you write whose tensions in the exam you will need to name them and you have not read enough to name them, so go and read Robins and come back. So I would go and read Robins and come back. Then it was Tharoor, and then Mr Halloran told me to read someone who disagreed with Tharoor, which annoyed me, and which turned out to be the more useful of the two.

It was never a fight. I want to be clear about that, because people hear "arguing with a teacher" and imagine defiance, and this was closer to a standing appointment. He disagreed with me about half the time and he did not soften it. When I said the book should just be replaced he said that every textbook is a compromise between a syllabus, a publisher, and the number of pages a fourteen-year-old will tolerate, and that learning to read around a compromise was a more transferable skill than finding a better book.

I did not notice it was doing anything to me. It felt like a mild obsession, four minutes at a time, roughly twice a week, for two years. But somewhere in there I stopped reading anything as though it had simply arrived. I read my chemistry textbook now and notice where it says "it was found that." I read news articles and notice which sentences have no subject. My sister says this makes me exhausting, and she is not entirely wrong.

The corridor is where I feel most myself because it is where I am least performing. There is no mark attached to it. I am not being assessed on whether page 84 is defensible. It is just two people who have both read the same paragraph, standing under a broken strip light, taking a sentence apart because taking it apart is interesting. I do not get that in lessons, where I am half thinking about the exam, or at home, where I am mostly thinking about food.

Mr Halloran retires next summer. I will probably just tell him that the four minutes were the actual course, and that the rest was revision.
