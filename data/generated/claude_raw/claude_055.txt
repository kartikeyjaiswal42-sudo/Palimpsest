The object is a cardboard thermometer, the kind you draw by hand and tape to a wall, the red marker line supposed to climb toward a goal. I still have mine, folded into quarters in the bottom of my desk drawer, because I could not make myself throw it away after the drive ended and the line had stopped less than a third of the way up.

I organized the food drive in October, the kind of project that sounds simple when you pitch it to a teacher. Every homeroom competes, whoever brings in the most canned goods gets a pizza party, all proceeds go to the county food bank before Thanksgiving. I stood in front of the whole school at an assembly and said we would collect two thousand items. I picked that number because it sounded ambitious and because the food bank director had mentioned, almost in passing, that two thousand items would cover about three weeks of the emergency pantry. I wanted to be the kind of person who did something that mattered, and mattering, in my head, meant a big number.

We collected about six hundred and ten. I know the exact figure because I counted them myself, twice, sitting on the gym floor at eight in the evening with a girl named Priya who had stayed to help me box everything, both of us hoping the second count would be higher than the first. It was not.

For a while I was furious, the way you get furious when you've built something around your own effort and the effort turns out not to be the thing that was missing. I blamed the homerooms that hadn't participated, the timing, the fact that the assembly ran short and I never got to make my second point about why it mattered. I drew up a list of what I'd do differently and it was full of the word "more" — more posters, more announcements, more homeroom competitions, more guilt.

The turn happened at the food bank itself, not at school. I drove the boxes over with my mom on a Saturday, and the director, a woman named Carla who had clearly done this a hundred times with a hundred disappointed teenagers, walked me through the intake room where volunteers were sorting donations onto shelves. She thanked me. She meant it, I could tell, in the plain unbothered way adults thank you when they are not performing gratitude for your benefit. Six hundred and ten items was six hundred and ten meals that existed because I had asked, in writing, on a piece of cardboard, for people to bring something.

I had built the whole project around a number I invented to sound impressive, and it took standing in a room full of actual food, actual shelves being filled by actual hands, to understand that the number was never really the point. The point was that a kid who forgot his lunch every other Tuesday might eat because someone else's mother remembered to grab an extra can of soup on her way to the register.

I kept the thermometer because the failed line on it is more honest than a full one would have been. It reminds me that I set the goal for myself, not for the people who needed the food, and that the difference between those two things is not always obvious until you're standing in the room where the difference actually lands. I don't know exactly what I'll do next time. I know I'll ask someone like Carla before I ever pick a number again.
