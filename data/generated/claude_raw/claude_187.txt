"That's not what the buoy data says," I told Priya, tapping my laptop screen hard enough that the trackpad clicked under my finger, "we can't just write 'more acidic,' that's not a number, that's a feeling." She had her marker uncapped over the poster board, red for danger zones, and she held it there in the air between us for a second before she recapped it and set it down without saying anything, which from Priya was worse than yelling.

It was February of tenth grade, and our biology group had drawn ocean acidification out of a hat, four names on the packet, but by week two it was really just me and Priya, because Dev had mono and Sam had quietly stopped showing up to lunch meetings. I had found the NOAA Pacific mooring data, hourly pH readings going back six years, and I wanted to build the whole project around it, a real graph, a real trend line, the kind of thing that would actually mean something instead of a poster with a sad cartoon coral turning gray. Priya wanted the sad cartoon coral. Not because she was lazy, which I told myself at the time and do not believe anymore, but because she had three other majors due that week and a little sister she picked up from daycare every day at four, and a graph with error bars was not a thing she had room for.

I did not ask her what she had room for. I asked her, in front of Dev, who had dragged himself in for one meeting despite the mono, whether she actually wanted to learn anything or just wanted the grade, and I said it like a joke, the way you say a mean thing and then laugh first so the other person feels stupid for being hurt by it. She did laugh, a little. Then she went back to the poster and didn't ask my opinion on anything else for the rest of the project, and I filled the silence by doing more of the actual analysis myself, which I told myself was generous and now understand was its own kind of insult.

We got an A minus. The teacher wrote "very thorough!" on my half of the rubric and "nice visuals" on hers, and I remember feeling, for about four seconds, pleased with that division of labor, before I understood what it meant that our one project had come back with two separate handwritings of feedback on it at all.

Priya and I still say hi in the hallway. We are not enemies, there was no scene, no blowup anyone else would have noticed, which somehow makes it harder to explain, because there is no single incident to point to, just a specific Tuesday when I decided being right about pH data mattered more than being someone she'd want to work with again. I think about the buoy data more than I probably should, not the numbers themselves but the version of me who thought producing them justified how I got there. I still read ocean chemistry papers some nights, actual peer-reviewed ones, long after midnight, long past the point where I'm absorbing anything useful, and I don't fully know anymore if that's curiosity or something closer to penance, or whether those two things are even different once you've gone far enough into either one. Some nights I get to a footnote, a citation trail, a dataset I haven't seen before, and I think about texting her the link before I remember what texting her a link would even mean now, three years and one apology I never sent later.
