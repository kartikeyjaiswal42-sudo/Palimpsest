That summer we decided to build a canoe. My father and I. This wasn't something we had discussed for years or planned carefully. He saw a video online and decided we should do it. I said okay because I didn't have anything better to do and because saying okay to his sudden projects was the way we had always operated. He gets ideas and I go along with them.

The canoe plan was take scrap wood and instructions from an old book and create something that would actually float and carry people. My father was excited in that way of his where excitement means he immediately starts buying supplies without thinking through the full scope of what he's committed to. He would come home from the hardware store with twenty dollars of materials when we needed two hundred dollars worth. This happened multiple times. I mentioned this multiple times.

What frustrated me is that I wanted to build it right. I had spent the previous summer learning about carpentry. I understood the principles of construction and stress distribution. I knew that some of his shortcuts wouldn't hold up. When I tried to point this out, he would listen politely and then ignore what I said. He had a specific vision and I was questioning it.

About halfway through the project, we had a real argument. I had tried to fix something he had built poorly, and he saw me taking it apart and told me to stop. He said I was overthinking it. He said the whole point was to do it fast and see if it worked, not to engineer it perfectly. I said the whole point was for it to actually work, which meant taking the time to build it right. He said I was sucking the fun out of it.

This disagreement never really resolved. We kept working on it through August, but there was tension. I would do a part carefully and he would make quick fixes over my work. He would do a part carelessly and I would worry it would fail. We didn't fight about it again, but the argument hung there between us. He thought I was overly cautious. I thought he was reckless.

In early September, we finished the canoe. We took it to the lake to test it. I was terrified it would sink. He was confident it would float. Both of our predictions were partially right. The canoe did float. But it was heavy and hard to paddle and not balanced properly. We took it out for maybe twenty minutes and then came back to shore. My father seemed disappointed but not crushed. I felt vindicated but not pleased.

What I didn't expect is what happened over the following months. He wanted to fix it. Not in a quick way, but methodically. He looked at balance and weight distribution. He asked me for advice about some of the structural issues. Together we made modifications. It took the rest of the fall and into winter, but by next spring, the canoe was actually good. We went out on it and it handled beautifully.

The thing is we never actually discussed what had changed. He didn't say I was right about taking time. I didn't say I was wrong about his approach. We just both started doing the work the way I had wanted to do it from the beginning. It was like the argument got resolved by action instead of by conversation.

What this taught me is that people you love can disagree with you fundamentally about how to approach something, and you can still end up in the same place. My father and I see things differently. He's an idea person and I'm a details person. In that moment, both ways of being had value. His excitement got us started. My attention to detail made it work. We couldn't have done it alone.

I think about that summer a lot when I'm doing other things. When people disagree with me. When I want to insist on my way. The canoe reminds me that the disagreement doesn't have to be resolved for the work to succeed. Sometimes you just keep moving and at the end you're at a place you didn't expect, and you realize you were both right the whole time, just in different ways.
