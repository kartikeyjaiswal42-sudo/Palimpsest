The notebook is blue and narrow-ruled. I've been using it for two years as a scribe for a blind student named Pradeep who is in three of my classes. The notebook is where I write down what he tells me to write, which is his way of taking notes. It's simple in concept—I write, he listens, he remembers—but it has meant something to both of us that goes beyond the practical.

Pradeep didn't ask me to be his scribe. The school provided an official scribe who was there during tests and in some of his classes. But he asked me one day if I would take notes in his math class because the official scribe wasn't available that day. I said yes. I just did it. The next day he asked if I would do it again. I said yes. Eventually the official scribe became backup and I became the regular scribe.

What I realized is that I'm not just writing down the material he needs to know. I'm writing down his understanding of it. When he tells me to take a note, he's already processed the information. He's already decided what matters and what doesn't. I'm not just transcribing. I'm recording the way his mind works.

This changed how I took notes. I had to listen more carefully because he needed clarity, not just speed. I couldn't scribble shortcuts. Everything I wrote had to make sense. This meant I was actually learning the material better while I was writing it for him. I was thinking about what mattered while I was writing. I was engaging with the content instead of just creating a record of it.

The blue notebook sits on my desk now as a completed thing. It's full of notes from this past school year, and I'm starting a new one for next year. Looking at it, I can see the progression of how I write. Early pages are messy, full of abbreviations and unclear phrases. By the middle, my handwriting has settled. By the end, I've developed a system where everything is clear and organized.

What changed for me is that I understood what attention means. Pradeep couldn't check the notes immediately like other students could. He had to trust that what I wrote was accurate and clear. That responsibility made me a better student, not just for him but for myself. If I'm going to write something down, it needs to be worth writing. It needs to be true and clear and useful.

Pradeep has never told me that this meant a lot to him. We don't talk about it much. I just show up and take notes and I hand him the notebook and he goes home and studies. It's practical. It's also something else—a relationship based on trust and attention. He's trusting me to care about the accuracy of what I write. I'm paying attention to his education in a way I wouldn't if I wasn't responsible for his notes.

I think often about how small this thing is. It's one class, one notebook, one student. But it's taught me something important about what it means to be useful to someone. It's not about grand gestures. It's about showing up consistently and caring about doing the work right. It's about understanding that the thing you think is simple might be essential to someone else's life.

The notebook is just paper and ink. But it's also a record of learning together, of paying attention, of being responsible to someone else's future. That's something worth showing up for.
