The water is down almost five feet this year, and my grandfather is already unlacing his shoes before I've finished parking the car. "Kohlua is close today," he says, like the village is a person who sometimes comes near the shore and sometimes doesn't, and by then I'd heard him say the name Kohlua so many times across so many dinners that I'd stopped actually hearing it, the way you stop hearing a clock once you've lived with it long enough.

He'd told the stories my whole life. The mango tree outside his father's house that took four children holding hands to circle. The well that never ran dry even in the worst summers, right up until the year the engineers came with their surveying poles and told everyone in the valley they had eighteen months. The temple with the cracked bell that rang wrong, a half-tone flat, and how the whole village claimed to like it that way better than a bell that rang true. I'd heard the mango tree story probably forty times. I used to time how long it took him to get to the part about the bell, and I am ashamed, now, of how bored I let myself look while he told it.

That afternoon, the reservoir was low enough that you could see it. Not imagine it, not take his word for it. See it. A ridge of stone poking through brown water that he said, without any drama in his voice at all, was the temple's outer wall. We stood there for maybe twenty minutes while he pointed at flat, unremarkable patches of water and told me what used to be under each one, the well, the courtyard, the lane his mother walked to the river every morning, and I realized somewhere in those twenty minutes that I was the only other person standing on that shore who had ever heard any of it. Not the only person who could have. The only person who had.

There is no village left to visit that isn't underwater nine months a year. There is no archive, no marker, no plaque the state ever put up, because a submerged village of eleven hundred people from sixty years ago isn't the kind of history anyone thought worth funding. There is a ninety-one-year-old man who still unlaces his shoes at the shoreline like he might wade in, and there is me, and after that afternoon there is a phone in my pocket I now actually use, recording him telling the mango tree story for the forty-first time, and the bell story, and one about a flood in nineteen-sixty-something that I'd genuinely never heard before because I'd always tuned out before he got that far.

I have eleven hours of him now. Some of it repeats. He tells the mango tree story slightly differently each time, the number of children changes, six once, four another time, and I've stopped trying to correct it into a single fixed version, because I think the drift is part of what's true about it. A place that only exists in memory doesn't hold as still as a place you can still walk through.

What I want to bring with me is not a finished archive. It's the habit itself, the recorder in the pocket, the willingness to sit through a story for the forty-second time in case this is the telling where a new detail surfaces, in case this is the last year the water goes down far enough to see the wall at all.
