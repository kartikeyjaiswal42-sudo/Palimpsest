The surveyor's spray can hisses twice before it catches, and then there's a red X on the mango tree's trunk, right at chest height, right over the spot where thirteen years of pencil notches are still faintly visible if you know where to look. My uncle is telling my father the number again, like saying it slower will make it hurt less, and my father isn't answering him, just standing there with his hand flat against the bark like he's checking the tree's pulse.

The notches are mine. Every year on my birthday, since I was four, my grandfather stood me against that trunk and cut a small line at the top of my head with his pocketknife, and then wrote the date next to it in pencil that has long since worn away, leaving just the cuts, a crooked ladder climbing the bark from somewhere around my grandfather's knee to somewhere past my own shoulder now. He used to say the tree and I were growing up together, since he'd planted it the spring my father was born and I was, in some sense he never fully explained, the second child it was raising.

My uncle wants to sell four acres to a developer building flats along the highway, enough money to cover what his son's surgery cost and what the loan he took out for it is now costing on top of that. My father wants to keep the land whole, the way it's been in the family for three generations, and every time this argument happens, which is often now, it ends the same way: my uncle asking what exactly my father thinks pride is going to pay for, and my father not answering, because there isn't a good answer, only a stubborn one.

I used to think my father would win just by refusing to lose. He's good at that, at outlasting a conversation instead of resolving it. But the surveyor was standing in our field with a can of spray paint because the bank had already sent a second notice, and no amount of my father's hand flat against bark was going to change what the four acres closest to the road were now worth on paper.

They sold it. Not all of it — my father held the line on the two acres nearest the house, including the tree, in a compromise that satisfied nobody completely, my uncle getting less than he needed and my father losing more than he wanted. The X stayed on the trunk for almost a year after, through two monsoons, fading from red to a rust-colored ghost of itself, until my father finally scrubbed it off one Sunday with a wire brush, not saying much while he did it, just working at the bark like it owed him something.

I climbed up to check the notches again before I left for school this fall. They're still there, thirteen crooked lines and a fourteenth my grandfather cut just weeks before he died, my father holding me steady that year since my grandfather's hands had started shaking too much to trust with a knife near my scalp. The tree stands maybe eighty feet from where the new boundary fence now runs, close enough that I can still see the flats going up on what used to be ours, three stories of scaffolding where the wheat used to come in gold by April.

My uncle and my father still don't really talk about it, not directly, though they sit at the same table every Sunday and pass dishes to each other like nothing happened. I keep waiting for one of them to say something that ends it properly. Neither of them ever does.
