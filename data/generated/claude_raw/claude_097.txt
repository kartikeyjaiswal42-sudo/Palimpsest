My grandfather kept a small tin box of photographs from a village called Rasoolpur, which does not exist anymore. It was submerged when a dam was built in the seventies, and the family that had lived there for generations scattered into three or four different towns, my grandfather's branch of it ending up two hundred kilometers away in the city where I was born. He talked about the village more than he talked about anything else. The well in the middle of the courtyard. The mango tree that supposedly gave fruit twice a year, which I now understand is probably not true, or at least not the way he told it. The exact bend in the river where he learned to swim.

I believed all of it, uncritically, for most of my childhood. I used to tell people at school that my family's village was at the bottom of a reservoir, like it was a fact I owned, a piece of drama that made me interesting. I never asked him a single real question about it. I never asked what it felt like to leave, or whether he'd gone back to see the water rise, or what happened to the people who didn't have anywhere else to go. I just collected the pretty details, the mango tree, the well, the river bend, the way you collect a story for later use, without thinking about the person who actually lived inside it.

He died two years ago. Going through his things, I found the tin box, and behind the photographs, a folded clipping about the dam's construction, with dates. The dates didn't match what he'd told me. The village had been abandoned four years before the reservoir finished filling, which meant there had been years in between, years of it half-empty, half-flooded fields, people leaving in stages instead of all at once in some single dramatic morning I had pictured. I had built the story around an ending cleaner than what actually happened, because that was the version I liked telling.

I think about that a lot now, the gap between what I chose to hear and what he was probably trying to tell me. I don't think he was lying. I think I simplified him, the way you simplify anyone whose story is more trouble to carry than the highlight version. He gave me a village and a river and a mango tree, and what he needed was someone to ask what it cost him to lose it. I didn't do that. I can't go back now.

The tin box sits on my desk. I don't know that I've earned the right to feel sentimental about it, given how little I actually listened while he was alive to be listened to. But I keep it there, as a kind of correction I owe him, even now, even late.
