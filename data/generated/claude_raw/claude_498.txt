We lost every single game that season.

Our baseball team went 0-14. Zero wins. Fourteen losses. The kind of record that becomes a legend at the school in a way that's not exactly flattering. People made jokes. "Are you trying to set a record for worst team?" that kind of thing. By the end of the season, we'd stopped showing up expecting to win. We showed up expecting to lose, and then we'd lose, and that was the story.

The thing nobody talks about is how demoralizing it is to be part of a team that loses constantly. It's not inspirational. It's not something you laugh about the way adults sometimes claim you will later. It's just disheartening. Every game feels pointless. Every practice feels pointless. You show up because the schedule says you have to show up, but the actual hope that things will be different is long gone.

The last game of the season, we were playing against the team that was in first place. We were supposed to lose. We'd probably lose by ten runs. We went into that game with basically zero expectations.

And then my father had the audacity to tell me, the night before that last game, that he was proud of me for sticking with the team through the whole season.

I remember being genuinely angry at him. I said, "What are you proud of? We won nothing. We didn't accomplish anything. I quit at some point—I've been going through the motions for weeks." I felt like he was patronizing me by acting like just showing up was worthy of pride.

He said something like, "You're right. You lost every game. And you kept coming back. Not because you thought you'd win, but because you'd made a commitment to be there for your teammates. That takes more character than winning when you're the best team."

I wanted to tell him he was wrong. I wanted to tell him that character was for people who won something. Character was for people who actually mattered. Character wasn't what we had. We just had failure.

But then the last game happened and something strange occurred.

We were up by three runs in the final inning, which has never happened before in our entire season. The other team started scoring, and suddenly we were in a position where the game could actually go either way. The pressure completely changed. We were no longer the team that was supposed to lose. We were the team that had a shot.

They ended up beating us by one run. We lost our last game just like we'd lost all the others. But the margins mattered that time in a way they hadn't before, because we'd actually been in position to win.

Walking off the field, I felt something different than I had after the other fourteen losses. I didn't feel crushed. I felt like something had shifted in how I understood our season. We'd been terrible. But we'd shown up. We'd tried. And in that last game, we'd actually competed.

My father's comment made more sense to me after that game than it had before. He wasn't saying losing was good. He was saying that my character wasn't measured by my wins and losses. My character was measured by whether I showed up when things were difficult, whether I could keep caring when caring didn't immediately produce results.

That's an argument I think we're still actually having, not just about baseball but about bigger things. Whether effort matters if the outcome isn't successful. Whether commitment to something is valuable even if you fail. I still think success matters. But I think I understand better now that it's not the only thing that matters.
