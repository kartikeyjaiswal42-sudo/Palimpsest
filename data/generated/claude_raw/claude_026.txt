The email came on a Tuesday in March with an automated response. Nobody had subscribed. I sat in the empty broadcast room staring at the mic, which I'd polished so many times the reflection was starting to smudge. My first episode had been perfect in my head—three hours of carefully selected indie tracks, live weather updates, my own rambling thoughts about why we should care about cloud formations. I'd recorded it the week before and uploaded it to every platform I could find. Zero listeners. Zero comments. Zero reason to keep going.

But there was this one thing: my grandmother's old radio. Not the equipment in the broadcast room, but the actual radio from her apartment, the one she'd brought from 1983 and never replaced. She'd given it to me six months earlier, right before we cleared out her place. It was brown plastic, heavy, with a dial that stuck and a sound so tinny it made AM stations sound like they were calling from underwater. She used to let me mess with it as a kid, turning the dial between stations just to hear the static.

I brought it into the broadcast room one afternoon and sat it on the control console where I could see the face of it, really see it. The dials. The ancient preset buttons that still had pencil marks from the 1980s with station names faded to ghosts. I thought about her turning that dial every evening, listening to the same three stations, the same evening news at six, the same host's voice for fifteen years. She never once needed anyone else to listen. She didn't need an audience. She just wanted the voice there.

So I started over. Same studio, same setup, same terrible equipment. But different. I stopped trying to make it perfect. I started talking about things I actually thought about—why my school's cafeteria smelled like burnt plastic, what it feels like to walk home on a day when the light hits the buildings exactly right, whether anyone else felt like they were pretending in geometry class. I played whatever I wanted. Sometimes I played nothing and just read from a book I liked. I gave it a stupid name. I told exactly three people about the stream.

One of them actually listened. Then two more. By the end of the year we had maybe fifteen people who tuned in on Thursday nights when they got bored. Some of them never talked to me at all. But they were there. The numbers never got big. The show never went viral. But that wasn't the point anymore. The point was that my grandmother's radio is sitting next to mine now, and I don't need permission to keep going. I don't need thousands. I don't need validation. I just need the person turning the dial to know someone is speaking.
