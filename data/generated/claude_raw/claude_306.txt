There's a book in my room that I can't read anymore. It's a collection of Agatha Christie short stories, the kind with the beat-up dust jacket and pages that smell like whatever nursing home smells like. Mr. Patterson's book. He marked it up—underlined passages, made notes in the margins about which detective he preferred, changed his mind halfway through about who the murderer was. I kept the book after he died because I didn't know what else to do with it.

I volunteered at the hospice when I was sixteen. Mostly I just sat in rooms and read to people. The hospice coordinator said it was one of the most needed things we could do—read aloud, just be present, let people hear a human voice. It seemed straightforward. You show up, you read, you leave. How hard could that be?

Mr. Patterson was in his room for three weeks before I met him. He was thin in the way dying people get thin, not from weight loss exactly but from an absence. He still had all his thoughts but his body had started the process of leaving. He asked if I could read Agatha Christie because his eyesight was going. I said sure. I'd never read Agatha Christie before. I picked up the book from his nightstand and started reading.

The problem was that I wasn't good at reading aloud. I would rush through parts. I'd mispronounce words. I'd lose my place because I wasn't paying attention. Mr. Patterson would stop me sometimes and ask me to go back because he'd missed something. Most of the time he just listened to me butcher the text without saying anything.

I went back every few days. We made it through maybe six stories. He asked questions about the plots even though he'd probably read them fifty times. He told me I was mispronouncing "Hercule" the whole time and we both just accepted it. He fell asleep once while I was reading and I kept going anyway because I wasn't sure if I was supposed to stop or keep going. When he woke up he asked me to read the part again because he'd missed it, so maybe the right choice was to keep reading.

The day before he died, I read three stories straight because he asked me to. My throat was sore. I definitely wasn't pronouncing anything correctly anymore. He listened to all of it. In between stories he would talk about his wife who'd died five years before, or his kids who never visited, or nothing at all. Mostly nothing. We just sat there, him lying in the bed and me reading badly about people getting murdered.

He died the next morning. I didn't find out until I showed up the next day and his room was empty. The hospice coordinator said I should take the book as a keepsake. I'm still not sure if that was the right call or if I was supposed to leave it there.

The thing I got wrong is that I thought I was failing at the job because my reading was bad. I thought I was supposed to read perfectly, to be the voice that distracted him from dying, to make everything better somehow. But I think what he actually wanted was just for someone to sit there and try. To care enough to show up and mispronounce things and keep going anyway.

The book stays on my shelf because taking it down would mean accepting that it's over, that there's no Mr. Patterson waiting to hear how the story ends. As long as the book's there, some part of him is still listening.
