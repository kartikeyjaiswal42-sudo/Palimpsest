I worked picking peaches one summer, which sounds like it should be a simple story but ended up being about something much more complicated that I still haven't resolved with my father.

He arranged the job. He had a connection to a farm, and they needed workers for the summer harvest. It was the kind of opportunity he valued—honest work, decent money for a teenager, something that would build character. He presented it to me as though he was giving me something, some kind of gift. Work ethic, he said. Responsibility. These were things that mattered.

I went. I spent six weeks picking peaches, starting at 5 a.m., working until the heat got unbearable in the early afternoon. My hands got blistered. My back hurt constantly. The money was decent, maybe three thousand dollars by the end, which seemed like real money to me.

But it was also miserable. The work was repetitive and exhausting and dull. The other workers were mostly older men or migrant families. I was the only teenager doing it for the summer, and I was obviously doing it as a character-building exercise, not because I needed to survive on the wages. That made it harder to relate to people for whom this wasn't a summer project. For whom this was just life.

When I told my father I wanted to quit halfway through July, he was disappointed. He said I was giving up, that I wasn't capable of following through on commitments. He said the hard thing about work is that sometimes you have to do it even when you don't want to, and that quitting was the easy way out.

I didn't quit. I finished the summer. But it was while actively resenting him for pushing me to do something that was teaching me a lesson I didn't want to learn.

Here's where it gets complicated: the experience did teach me something. It taught me about my own capacity. I realized halfway through July that I could endure things I didn't like and still function. The misery didn't kill me. The exhaustion didn't make me incapable. I could be uncomfortable and keep showing up anyway.

But what I also learned was that my father didn't actually understand what the work felt like from the inside. He had framed it as character building, but he was doing that from a position of privilege. This job had an endpoint for me. I had college coming. I had a future that didn't involve picking fruit. For the workers who were there year-round or season-round, the framing of "character building" felt insulting. They were doing it because they needed to. The character building was a luxury of my perspective.

When I came home, I didn't talk to him much about the experience. He asked if I'd learned anything. I said yes. He seemed satisfied that his lesson had worked. But what I'd actually learned was that we didn't agree on what the experience meant, and I wasn't going to convince him of my perspective.

He still brings it up sometimes. He'll mention a time I complained about something and say, "Well, at least you're not picking fruit," as if the summer crystallized my understanding of what I should be grateful for. And I find myself wanting to argue, to explain that I learned something different than what he thought he was teaching me.

The argument that never happened is still sitting between us. He thinks the summer was good for me and that I learned to be tough. I think the summer was necessary but also that my father used it to prove a point to himself about parenting rather than genuinely thinking about what the experience was like for me. I think he was right that I needed to learn endurance, but I'm not sure his framing of why that lesson mattered was correct.

I'm not resentful about it anymore. I'm seventeen now, and I'm old enough to see that my father was trying to raise me into someone resilient. That's actually a good thing. But I also know that his idea of growth through struggle came from a place of not quite understanding the privilege that allowed me to experience struggle as a learning opportunity rather than as survival necessity.

We've never had the conversation that would resolve it. I don't think we will. But the summer sits between us as evidence of something we fundamentally disagree about, even though we're both too polite to say it directly.
