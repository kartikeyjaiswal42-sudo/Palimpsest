The bench is still in our garden and it is still wrong, and every time I sit on it I can feel exactly where I stopped paying attention.

I started building things from pallets at fourteen, mostly because pallets are free. The builders' merchant at the end of our road stacked them behind the fence and would let you take them if you asked, and a pallet is a remarkable object: nine boards, three bearers, forty nails, and somebody else has already done the sawing. Over two summers I made a shoe rack, a planter, a low table my mother still uses, and a compost bin that fell apart in eight months.

The bench was the fourth thing and it was meant to be the good one. My father's birthday. I had drawn it on graph paper. Two metres long, a back at a slight angle, arms wide enough to put a cup on, and — this was the bit I was proud of — the boards on the seat running the long way rather than across, so the grain would look continuous. I bought proper screws with my own money instead of using the nails I had pulled out.

Here is what I got wrong. The pallets had been sitting outdoors behind that fence for I do not know how long, and the boards were wet. Not visibly wet. Wet the way wood is wet when it has been rained on for a year and then had a dry fortnight. I knew, in a distant way, that you are supposed to let timber dry and acclimatise before you build with it. But it was the eleventh of June and his birthday was the nineteenth, and drying properly would have meant a month indoors, and I told myself that these were pallets, not furniture timber, and that it probably did not apply.

The bench was beautiful for about five weeks. Then the seat boards began to shrink, which they do across the grain, not along it, and because I had run them the long way and screwed them down hard at both ends with no gaps, they had nowhere to go. Two of them split. One cupped so badly that water now collects in it and sits there after rain. The joint at the left arm opened up about four millimetres and I have never been able to close it.

What I got wrong was not really the wood. I want to be honest about that, because for a long time I told the story as though it were a technical mistake, a fact about moisture content that I did not know. I did know. What I did not do was let the knowledge inconvenience me. I had a date, a design I liked, and a strong feeling that I understood what I was doing, and when a piece of information arrived that would have cost me a month, I found a reason it did not apply to my particular case. That is a different kind of error and it is much harder to fix, because it does not feel like ignorance from the inside. It feels like judgement.

My father sits on it most evenings in summer. He has said several times that he likes it, and I think he means it, and that makes it worse rather than better. I have offered twice to rebuild it. He says no.

So it stays there, splitting slowly, with a puddle in the third board from the left. I have built five things since and every one of them has spent a month in the garage first, doing nothing, while I got on with something else. That is the only part of this I would call growth: I have learned to wait, and I still resent it every time.
