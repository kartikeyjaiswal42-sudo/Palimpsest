My dad and I still don't agree about this and I don't think we're going to.

Three years. Freshman year, sophomore year, junior year. Same gym, same list taped to the same door outside the athletics office, same feeling in my stomach at 3:40 on a Thursday. Basketball. I'm five foot ten, which is nothing, and I have hands that are honestly kind of small, and I can shoot from the corner if nobody is standing near me. That last part turned out to matter a lot.

The first year I cried in the car. My dad didn't say much, he just drove, and then when we got to the light on Chester he said, "next year." That was the whole conversation. Next year.

The second year he had a plan. Six a.m. at the rec center before school, three days a week, and he came with me every single time, which is the part I want to be clear about, because he was working a double most weeks and he still got up. He'd rebound for me. He'd count out loud. Two hundred shots and then we'd go home and he'd shower and go to work. In March I got cut again, and this time he was angry, not at me, at Coach Delgado, and he wanted to go talk to him, and I begged him not to, and he didn't, and I think he's still a little bit sorry that he listened to me.

The third year is the one we fight about.

Because by then I knew. I'd played summer league and I could see it, plainly, the way you can see the weather. Devon and Marcus and the sophomore kid whose name I never learned were just better than me. Not by attitude. Not by hours. Their bodies did things mine wasn't going to do, and I had spent two years of six a.m.s buying about four inches of vertical and a decent left hand, and it wasn't enough and it wasn't going to be enough. So I went to tryouts anyway, because I said I would, and I got cut, and I came home and I told my dad I was done and I was going to do something else.

He said I was quitting.

He didn't say it meanly. He said it like it was a fact about the world he was informing me of. And I said no, I'm not quitting, I'm reading the situation correctly, and he said that's what quitting sounds like from the inside, and that landed on me hard enough that I've thought about it probably once a week since.

Here's where I ended up. I started keeping stats for the team instead. Which sounds pathetic when I write it down, the kid who couldn't make it hanging around with a clipboard, and yeah, it felt like that in November. But I got interested. I built a spreadsheet that tracked where every shot came from, and by January I could tell Coach Delgado that our second-quarter offense was generating almost nothing from the left wing, and he actually changed something because of it. We went 16-6. I'm not saying that was me. I'm saying I was in the room.

My dad came to games. He's proud of me, he says so. But there's a thing he does where he'll mention someone from his old job whose son walked on somewhere, and it's not pointed, he's just talking, and I hear it anyway.

What I think now is that we're arguing about two different questions and calling it one. He thinks the question is whether you keep going. I think the question is whether you're honest about what you're good at. Both of those are real. Neither of us has convinced the other.

He'd want me to add that I could still try out senior year.

I'm not going to. But I love him for the six a.m.s, and I'd like him to know that the thing he was actually teaching me stuck, just not in the shape he wanted.
