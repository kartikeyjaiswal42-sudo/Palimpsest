I'd spent my entire childhood in a religious school. Catholic education, which meant uniforms, mandatory chapel, religion classes mixed into every subject. By ninth grade, I'd started asking questions. By tenth grade, I was certain that I didn't believe the things the school was teaching me. By eleventh grade, I was sure I needed to leave.

The decision to actually leave was harder than I expected.

My parents were shocked. They'd chosen this school specifically because it was religious, because they wanted me to have that education and that foundation. My mother actually cried when I told her. My father said I was throwing away the education they'd worked to provide. My little sister thought I was making a huge mistake. The school's administration tried to convince me to stay, said that leaving in the middle of high school would hurt my record.

Everyone seemed invested in me not leaving.

But I felt like I was suffocating. Every religion class felt like being forced to accept something I didn't believe. Every chapel service felt like pretending. I was spending six hours a day in a place that I'd outgrown, and I was angry about it in a way that was affecting everything else. I wasn't focusing in my actual classes. I wasn't participating the way I used to. I was just waiting for the day to end.

So I pushed to transfer schools. I found a public school that would take me mid-year. I filled out the paperwork. I told my parents this was happening whether they were happy about it or not.

What I didn't expect was that once I left, I'd miss it.

Not the religion classes. Not chapel. Not the uniforms. But I missed my friends, who all stayed at the religious school. I missed the structure and the clear expectations. I missed knowing exactly what the rules were and what would happen if I broke them. The public school was bigger and more chaotic and had so many more social hierarchies that I hadn't anticipated.

More than that, I realized that I'd been so focused on what I was leaving that I hadn't thought about what I was moving toward.

The first month at the new school was disorienting. The second month was actively lonely. By the third month, I'd made some friends and I was adjusting, but I kept having this weird feeling that I'd made a mistake. Or not a mistake exactly, but I'd made a choice without fully understanding it.

Looking back now, I think that moment of wanting to leave, the thing that felt so clear and obvious and necessary at the time, was also the moment I stopped believing I had to have everything figured out. I left the religious school because I was certain I was right about needing to leave. But I left not really knowing what came next, and that uncertainty was actually okay. I adapted. I found a different path. My life didn't end because I made a big change.

What changed my mind about the whole thing is that I realized the leaving was actually the right choice, even though the transition was harder than I'd anticipated. The religious education wasn't for me. The religious school wasn't for me. And that was fine. Other people should have religious education if they want it. But I didn't. I needed something different.

That feels obvious now, but it wasn't obvious at the time, when everyone around me seemed to think I was making a terrible mistake.
