Six weeks after Priya Anand stopped speaking to me, I was sitting in the back of Mr. Halloran's room during a free period when he said, not even looking up from the stack he was grading, "You know she could have torched you on that evaluation and she didn't." I asked him what he was talking about, and he seemed surprised I didn't already know, and then he wouldn't say anything else, like he'd already told me more than he meant to. It took me the rest of the day to figure out he meant the peer contribution forms from our Envirothon project, the ones we'd each filled out alone back in March, right around the time everything between us had gone quiet.

Priya and I had been best friends since the start of freshman year, when we got seated alphabetically next to each other in biology and bonded over both hating the teacher's habit of cold-calling. By junior year we did everything together, including, unfortunately, signing up as partners for the county Envirothon regional, where our assignment was to design and build a working rainwater filtration prototype on a budget of forty dollars, using whatever we could scavenge out of her dad's garage. She was better at the engineering. I was better at explaining things to judges. It should have worked.

It didn't, not because the filter itself failed, but because we spent weeks disagreeing about whose idea the core design actually was. She said the layered charcoal-and-sand approach had been hers from the beginning. I remembered it differently, remembered sketching it on the back of a worksheet at lunch while she was still stuck on a completely different model that needed a pump we couldn't afford. By the week before the competition we were barely speaking except to argue about credit, and two days before we presented, she said, loud enough that people in the hallway turned to look, that I didn't even understand my own part of the project. Maybe I didn't, not entirely. But it was the kind of thing you don't forgive fast, and neither of us tried to.

We stopped sitting together. We stopped texting. I wrote maybe four different drafts of an apology I wasn't sure I owed and never sent a single one. In March, when Mr. Halloran handed out the individual contribution forms, the ones where you rate your partner's effort and describe what they actually did, I filled mine out honestly but carefully, giving her full credit for the design even though it still stung, because I didn't want to be someone who let a fight rewrite the truth.

I assumed she wouldn't return the favor. I assumed, if I'm being honest, that she'd use the form to write down some version of what she'd said in the hallway, this time somewhere it would count toward my actual grade. So when Mr. Halloran let that comment slip, I asked to see my own file, which technically I wasn't supposed to do, and he let me anyway. Priya had written two sentences naming the exact piece I'd built, the water-clarity testing rig nobody else had noticed because it wasn't the flashy part, and said I'd done it without being asked and without complaining once. She'd dated the form March 9th. I checked that against my own memory and realized it was four days after the hallway, four days into a silence she had no reason to end, and she'd had every opportunity to hurt me on paper and had chosen, instead, to sit there and write something true and generous about a person she wasn't speaking to.

I don't know exactly what made her choose that. I've pictured the scene more times than I probably should, her at her kitchen table with the form and a pen, still angry, still not answering my texts, deciding to be fair anyway, for reasons that had nothing to do with me finding out. I never got an explanation, because we never really talked again, not properly, not the way we used to before all this. But something in me shifted after Mr. Halloran's comment, some assumption I hadn't known I was carrying about what people owe each other once a friendship is over. I started paying closer attention to my own smallness in situations like that one, the instinct to protect my version of events even when it costs someone else something real. I started trying, in whatever form that takes outside of a classroom, to rate people the way Priya rated me instead of the way I'd been ready to rate her.

It didn't fix anything between us. Some friendships end and just stay ended, no matter how the story resolves itself later. But it changed what I thought fairness was worth when nobody's forcing you to practice it, and that lesson has outlasted the fight by years now, longer than the friendship itself lasted after it.
