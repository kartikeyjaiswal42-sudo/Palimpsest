The object is a shoebox, the lid gone soft at the corners, that has moved with me across six schools in nine years. Inside it are the things too small to count as real possessions but too important to leave behind in any of the moves — a subway token from Boston, a pressed leaf from a tree outside my school in Ohio, a laminated hall pass from Georgia that a teacher let me keep as a joke because I'd lost so many real ones adjusting to a new building's rules, a folded seating chart from a fourth-grade class in Denver where a girl named Priya had drawn a small sun next to my name because I'd made her laugh on my first day. My father's job moved us for work, six times, sometimes with a year of warning and once with eleven days, which meant packing my entire room into boxes labeled by a moving company that never remembered my name from one relocation to the next.

Each move followed roughly the same shape. There was the week of dread beforehand, when I already knew what the new hallway would smell like even though I'd never seen it — floor wax and cafeteria food and something specific to whichever region we were headed to. There was the first morning, always the worst, walking into a classroom where everyone already had a group and a shorthand and a set of inside jokes I hadn't earned. And then there was the strange, almost mechanical process of rebuilding, which I got faster at every time, until by the sixth school I could locate the kid most likely to become my actual friend within about two days, just from watching who laughed at what during a lull in class.

I used to think the box was just clutter, the kind of thing a parent tells you to throw out before a move because it "takes up space you don't have." My mother told me that exactly once, before the Georgia move, and I refused, and we didn't fight about it again. What I understand now, packing it for the seventh time this spring as we prepare for one more relocation before college, is that the box is the only physical record I have of continuity in a life that otherwise reads, from the outside, as constant rupture. Six different friend groups I had to build from nothing. Six different accents I half-picked-up and half-performed to fit in faster. Six sets of teachers who knew a slightly different version of me because I'd learned, by the third move, to introduce myself a little differently each time, testing which version got me liked fastest.

The token and the leaf and the hall pass don't matter individually. What matters is that they're all in the same box, which means somewhere underneath all that adaptation there was one kid collecting small evidence that he'd actually been somewhere, that the version of him in Boston and the version in Georgia were connected by more than a shared last name on a school roster.

What I would bring to a campus that wouldn't be there without me is something related to that box, though it took me years to see it as a strength instead of a scar. I am extremely good, faster than almost anyone I know, at reading a new room. I can walk into a dining hall or a dorm floor or a study group on day one and figure out, within an hour, who the quiet ones are who need to be drawn out and who the loud ones are who need to be gently checked, because I have done this, cold, six separate times, with no home-field advantage ever. I don't experience a new environment as threatening. I experience it as familiar, in the specific and strange way that only genuine repetition can produce.

I also carry, from all that moving, an allergy to the idea that belonging has to be given to you by circumstance — by growing up somewhere, by having roots that were planted before you were old enough to have a say in it. I've had to build belonging out of nothing, quickly, over and over, in strip malls and church basements and unfamiliar cafeterias, and I know now that it's buildable, which most people don't get to learn until much later in life, if ever, and some never learn at all because they've never needed to. I would bring that specific, hard-won knowledge to a campus full of people who, many of them, have never had to build a community from scratch before, who assume the friend group they arrive with freshman week is roughly the one they'll keep, and who might be quietly terrified, underneath the confidence, of ever having to start over the way I've started over six separate times.

I don't say any of this to make the moving sound easy, because it wasn't. I have cried in five different bedrooms over five different goodbyes, and there is a version of this essay where I write about the friends I lost touch with instead of the skill I gained, and that version would also be true. But I would bring, to whatever campus takes me, a genuine comfort with being new, a working knowledge of how communities actually form instead of how they're assumed to form, and a shoebox, still soft at the corners, that would sit on a dorm room shelf as proof that I've done this seven times now and I am still, somehow, intact.
