Before I learned sign language, I thought deafness was something people managed around. When Marcus joined our class sophomore year, I expected the school would handle everything—interpreters, accommodations, whatever deaf students needed. I didn't think about what it meant to actually communicate with him beyond the formal structures in place.

Then one day Marcus tried to ask me about an assignment and had to wait ten minutes for an interpreter to show up. I watched him growing frustrated, watching the clock, watching the rest of us move on. I realized the system wasn't really solving the problem. It was just making it visible.

I started that week with a YouTube video on basic ASL. I thought I'd learn a few phrases, enough to say hello or goodbye. But the more I learned, the more I understood how much I didn't know. The grammar works differently. The speed matters. Your facial expressions aren't decoration—they're part of what you're saying. I made mistakes constantly. I'd sign something in the wrong tense or use the wrong hand shape and Marcus would politely correct me, patient with my fumbling.

What surprised me most was that Marcus started seeking me out. Not because I was fluent—I wasn't—but because I was trying. Because I showed up without waiting for the school to arrange it. Every morning before homeroom, we'd spend ten minutes practicing. He'd teach me slang I'd never find in the tutorial videos. I learned about Deaf culture, about the difference between signed English and ASL, about things he noticed that nobody had ever asked him about.

By junior year, I was fluent enough to interpret for him in some classes. Not officially, not instead of the real interpreter, but in ways that mattered. When teachers asked questions, I could repeat them quickly in sign instead of waiting. When the interpreter called in sick, I could help him get through the class. When he wanted to contribute something in discussion and the timing was off, I could carry his idea forward.

But here's what kept changing for me: I started to see how much I'd taken for granted. I'd thought about my own ability to hear as neutral, just the default. Learning to sign made me realize it was a privilege. Teachers don't wait for their questions to be interpreted for me. I can pick up a conversation happening across the room. I can hear music without it being translated for me. None of that is normal or neutral—it's just luck, or biology, or whatever you want to call it.

I questioned, for the first time, whether the accommodations we had for Marcus were actually equal to what the rest of us got. An interpreter is good. But it's a patch. Why shouldn't his education include direct communication with his peers? Why should he have to rely on technology or specialized staff to participate in the everyday social aspects of school that the rest of us take for granted?

I don't have an answer to that question. But I think about it differently now because of one small habit. Every morning, ten minutes before anyone else arrived. We never planned it formally or made a big deal out of it. But somewhere in those conversations, my understanding shifted. I stopped thinking about deaf accommodation as something the school should provide and started thinking about it as something we all needed to participate in. It wasn't a dramatic realization or a moment of heroic growth. It was gradual and small and necessary.

Marcus graduates this year. I'll probably forget some of the sign language eventually—not all of it, but the fluency will fade if I don't keep using it. But that habit of checking my assumptions about what's normal, what's accessible, what's fair—that stayed with me. It's still there.
