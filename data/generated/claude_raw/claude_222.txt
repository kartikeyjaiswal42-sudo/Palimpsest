I volunteered to tutor math at my school starting sophomore year, and what I didn't understand at the time is that I was actually setting myself up to fail in a way that would stay with me for years. I thought I was being generous. I was being arrogant.

I was good at math. I had taken precalculus early and always got As. So when my school put out a call for peer tutors, it seemed like a natural thing to do. I would help students who were struggling, and I would be the kind of person who helped, which felt important to who I wanted to be. I didn't really think about what tutoring actually meant or whether I had any particular talent for teaching.

The students they matched me with were failing. Not just struggling—actually failing, getting Ds and Fs, in real danger of not passing the class. My job was to help them turn that around. I was confident I could do it. I was smart at math. Therefore, I could explain math. That's basically how I approached the entire thing.

It was wrong. I couldn't explain math. I could do math, which is entirely different. When a student asked me why something worked a certain way, I would just demonstrate the procedure again, assuming they had misunderstood my demonstration the first time. When they asked questions that seemed basic to me, I found myself getting frustrated. How did they not see this? It was so obvious.

I had one student, Derek, who came twice a week for an entire semester and made almost no progress. He was a sophomore taking freshman algebra because he had failed it the year before. He wasn't stupid. He just thought about numbers differently than I did, and because his way was unfamiliar to me, I assumed it was wrong. I would correct him and show him the right way, which was my way, which didn't actually help because his brain wasn't wired the same way mine was.

By the end of that semester, Derek wasn't getting better grades. He was getting worse because now he was more confused, trying to force his thinking into a shape that didn't fit. And I was frustrated because I had done everything right—I had shown up, I had tried hard, I had been patient. What more could I have done?

What I didn't do was ask him how he thought. What I didn't do was try to meet him where he was instead of trying to drag him to where I was. What I didn't do was admit that maybe I wasn't good at this. I just blamed him for not trying hard enough to understand.

I quit tutoring after that year. I told myself it was because I was too busy with school, which was partly true but not the whole truth. The whole truth is that Derek made me confront something about myself that I didn't like. I was only kind to people who could learn the way I learned. I was only patient with people whose brains worked like mine. When someone couldn't make that adjustment, I blamed them.

That was seven years ago now, and I still think about it. I think about Derek especially. I think about whether my tutoring actually set back his understanding of math by confusing him with bad pedagogy. I think about the fact that I never asked if he got better after I left, never followed up, never tried to fix what I had broken.

I'm better at asking now. I'm better at admitting when I don't know something. I'm better at understanding that my way of learning is not the only way. But that required me to be really wrong first, and to really know that I was wrong, and to not be able to explain it away as the other person's failure.

I got wrong how to be kind to someone who doesn't think like me. I'm still thinking about it.
