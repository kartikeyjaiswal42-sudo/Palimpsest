The ice storm took the lines down on a Thursday and the power did not come back until the following Wednesday night. I was fourteen. What I remember is that I loved it, and that loving it is the part I got wrong.

For me it was a holiday. School closed. We cooked on the camp stove in the garage with the door cracked, and my father showed me how to bleed the water heater so the pipes would not split, and at night we played cards by a lantern and my mother told a story about her cousin in Ohio that I had never heard and have never heard since. I thought: this is what a family is when you take the screens out. I actually said something like that out loud, on maybe the third night, in the pleased voice of a boy who has recently discovered an idea. My father agreed with me. My grandmother, who lived with us then, did not say anything.

She was eighty-one and on oxygen. Her concentrator ran on electricity. What I did not know at the time, because nobody told me and because I did not ask, was that my parents had spent Friday morning calling around for a generator, and Saturday driving to my aunt's in Millbrook with two cylinders of backup oxygen in the trunk that were rated for about thirty-six hours, and Sunday deciding whether to move her to the hospital, which would have meant a bed she might not have gotten back. I found this out two years later, at a Thanksgiving, when my aunt mentioned it the way you mention weather.

I had been playing cards. I had been having the best week of that winter.

What I still turn over is not that I failed to help. There was nothing a fourteen-year-old could have done about a generator. It is that I built a whole small philosophy out of that week — about simplicity, about how we do not really need all of this — and I built it standing on a floor that other people were holding up. The candlelight was charming because it was temporary and because somebody else was tracking the hours of oxygen left. Every romantic idea I had about going without depended on the fact that I was not the one keeping anybody alive.

I think about it now whenever I hear the word resilient. People use it about neighborhoods after storms, and they mean it kindly, and sometimes it is true. But I have learned to ask who is doing the work that the word is covering for. When our school ran a canned-food drive after the floods last year, I was on the committee, and I pushed us to ask the shelter what they actually needed instead of guessing. They said batteries and extension cords and, oddly, phone chargers. Not romantic. Not simple.

My grandmother died in 2022, of something unrelated. She never brought that week up with me. I do not know if she was frightened. I have decided not to ask my mother, which may itself be a thing I am getting wrong. But I no longer trust an experience of hardship that felt good to the person having it.
