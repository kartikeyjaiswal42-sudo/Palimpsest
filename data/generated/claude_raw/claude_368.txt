I learned to swim at sixteen, in the shallow end of the municipal pool on Ferndale Road, on Tuesday mornings at seven, in a class that was otherwise entirely adults. There was a retired postman named Colin, two women in their forties who came together and talked the whole time, and me. The instructor was twenty-three and called all of us "folks."

My mother does not know I did it until it was finished, and that is the disagreement.

She cannot swim either. Neither can her mother. This is not an accident of our family, it is a fact about where she grew up, where the river took two boys from her school in one summer and the response of every adult in that town was to teach the children to fear water rather than to enter it. She taught me the same thing and she taught it well. Until I was fifteen I genuinely believed I had a body that did not float, that some people are made denser, that this was physics and not fear.

What changed was stupid and small. My cousin's wedding was at a hotel with a pool and I sat in a plastic chair in jeans for four hours while everyone I am related to under the age of thirty was in the water. Nobody said anything to me about it. That was worse. I went home and looked up adult beginner classes on my phone that night, and the first one I found was Ferndale, and it cost forty-two pounds for eight weeks, and I paid for it out of the money I make at the garden centre.

The first three weeks I did not put my face in. Week four I did, for about a second, and came up making a sound I did not know I could make. Week six I swam the width. It took me eleven strokes and I was not breathing correctly and Colin clapped, which was mortifying, and it remains one of the four or five best things that has ever happened to me. I can now swim about two hundred metres badly. I go on Sundays.

I told my mother in August. I had expected — I don't know what I expected. Pride, maybe, or the version of relief where someone says they always wanted you to. What I got was quiet for a long moment and then, "Who did you go with?"

Nobody. That was the wrong answer. She was not angry that I had learned. She was frightened that I had been in water for eight weeks and she had not known, that if something had happened she would have found out from a phone call. And underneath that, which she has said out loud twice since, is her belief that the sensible response to a dangerous thing is distance, and that what I have done is not competence, it is a slightly better-informed way of drowning. She thinks confidence in water is the thing that kills people. She is not entirely wrong; the boys from her school could both swim.

We have not resolved it. She has not come to the pool. I have stopped asking, because asking makes it a test she has to fail, and I have started just telling her when I go and when I am back, which she has never requested and which she has also never told me to stop doing.

What I actually learned, apart from the swimming, is that I had been treating her fear as an error in her that I could correct with evidence. Every time I brought a fact — the depth of the shallow end, the lifeguard, the number of strokes — I was arguing with the wrong thing. Her fear is not a belief about water. It is a memory of two funerals. You cannot out-argue a funeral.

So the disagreement stands, and I have gotten more comfortable with it standing. I go on Sundays. I text her when I am out. She reads it and does not reply, and then at dinner she asks how far I got.
