There is a stapled essay in the bottom drawer of my desk, eleven pages, on lined paper, with a comment in green ink at the end that I have read more times than I would like to admit. The comment says: This is competent. You are not a writer, and you should know that now rather than later.

I was fourteen. The essay was on Of Mice and Men and it was, I think, competent. That is not the part that stayed.

I did not throw it away. I put it in the drawer and did not look at it for about a year, and then I started looking at it fairly often, in a way that was not entirely healthy. When I wrote something I liked, I would take it out and read the green ink and feel a specific unpleasant thrill, half proof and half accusation.

I want to be fair to him. He was not being cruel, or did not think he was. He believed he was doing me a service by telling me early, and said as much when I asked him about it, once, standing at his desk after class with the pages in my hand and my voice not working properly. He said that a lot of teachers tell every student they can be anything, and that he thought this was dishonest, and that competent was not an insult. I have thought about that conversation for three years and I still cannot decide how much of it was principle and how much was a man having a bad Thursday.

What I know is what I did next, and it was not defiance. For about eight months I stopped writing anything that was not set work. I told myself I was being sensible. I was fourteen and an adult who read for a living had assessed me, and the reasonable response to that is to accept it. That period is the thing I would go back and change — not his comment, mine. The eight months of agreeing with him.

What ended it was small. Our school newsletter needed someone to write up a sports day and nobody volunteered, so I did it, and I made it funny on purpose, and a boy I did not know quoted a line of it back to me in the lunch queue two weeks later. But I noticed that I had spent forty minutes on four hundred words about the Year Eight relay and had enjoyed every one of them, and that this fact was not affected in any way by what was in the drawer.

I write now most days. Mostly things nobody sees. I have had two pieces in a regional youth magazine and one rejection that ran to a page and a half, which I have also kept, in the same drawer, because it took me seriously enough to be specific.

I keep the essay for a reason that has changed. At fifteen I kept it as evidence to be overturned. Now I keep it because it is the clearest example I own of how much weight I will hand to one confident adult if I am not paying attention. He was not lying and he was not right; he was one person, on one Thursday, reading eleven pages by a fourteen-year-old, and I let it decide something for the better part of a year.

I have not shown it to him. He still teaches there. Occasionally I think about walking in and putting the drawer's whole contents on his desk, and then I think that would be a story about him, and I would rather it stayed a story about me.
