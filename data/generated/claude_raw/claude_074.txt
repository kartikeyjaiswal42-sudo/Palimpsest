My grandmother has a vegetable garden that has been in the same spot for thirty years. When the drought came, I started watering it. This was supposed to be temporary, just helping while the water restrictions were in place. But it became a daily habit that taught me more about persistence and care than I expected.

The garden is in her backyard, maybe four hundred square feet. She grows tomatoes, squash, peppers, beans, and herbs. The plants had survived dry seasons before, but this drought was different. The city imposed watering restrictions. Watering between nine and eleven in the morning, twice a week. That's not enough for a garden that needs consistent moisture.

So I woke up early and hand-carried water from the house in buckets. Before my school started, I would fill buckets and pour them into the garden. It was inefficient and took maybe forty-five minutes every morning. But it worked. The plants survived.

What I realized over the course of that summer is that this daily action mattered more to my grandmother than I understood. She had lived through the Depression and through economic hardship. She had a tendency to worry about resources disappearing. She watched me water the garden and it seemed to calm something in her. The fact that the garden was alive mattered to her in a way that went beyond food production.

The drought lasted four months. For four months I woke up and carried water. On days when I slept late or was busy with school or just didn't feel like doing it, my grandmother would mention it gently. She never demanded it, but I could tell the routine mattered to her.

By the end of the drought, I had learned something about consistency. About showing up for something that doesn't really benefit you directly but benefits someone you care about. About the difference between doing something because you have to and doing something because it's the right thing to do. I was doing this by choice. I could have refused. The garden might have died back and eventually recovered. But I kept watering because my grandmother needed the garden to be alive.

I also learned about what plants actually need. I learned to feel the soil and know when it was dry. I learned which plants were more resilient. I learned that the garden was more fragile and more resilient at the same time than I expected. Everything was struggling but everything was determined to survive.

After the drought ended, I stopped watering the garden every day. But I still help my grandmother with it. I still wake up early sometimes and water before school. What was an emergency habit became a real relationship with the garden and with my grandmother's connection to it.

My grandmother is seventy-five now. She'll probably do the garden for many more years, but at some point, someone will need to take it over. I think it will be me. I think that summer of hand-carrying water woke something up in me about inherited responsibility. About the things that our families maintain and why we maintain them. The garden isn't just plants. It's continuity. It's proof that something can survive even in difficult times.
