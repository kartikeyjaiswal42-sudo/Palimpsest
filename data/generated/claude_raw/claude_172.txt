My uncle taught me to drive in a 1994 Ford F-150 with a manual transmission and a passenger door that only opened from the outside. We did it in the hay field behind his house in Cass County, where there was nothing to hit except one walnut tree and, in theory, the irrigation pump. He gave me exactly one rule that first afternoon: if you don't know what's about to happen, put in the clutch. Everything else, he said, we would figure out.

I was fourteen. I got good fast, or I thought I did. Within three weeks I could start on the hill by the culvert without rolling back, and I could downshift into second without the truck bucking, and I had started to enjoy the specific arrogance of a kid who can do something adults find difficult. By July I was driving the truck alongside the baler while my cousin Wes stacked bales in the bed, which meant holding a steady four miles an hour across uneven ground with a person standing up behind me.

The thing I got wrong happened on a Tuesday. Wes yelled something I could not hear over the tractor. I turned around to look at him. I did not put in the clutch, and I did not stop, and while I was turned around the truck drifted maybe six feet to the left, and the right rear tire dropped into a shallow washout. The truck lurched. Wes went down onto the bales and then off the side of the bed and landed on his back in the stubble.

He was fine. That is the part I have to say early, because it changes what kind of story this is. He got up, swore at me with real creativity, and had a bruise across his shoulder blades for two weeks. Nothing was broken. My uncle came over, looked at the washout, looked at me, and said, "You looked away," and then he had me get back in and finish the row, which at the time felt like cruelty and which I now understand was the whole lesson.

What I still think about is not the drift. Trucks drift. What I think about is why I turned around. Wes was yelling and I wanted to know what he was saying, and underneath that, I wanted him to see that I was the kind of driver who could hold a conversation while working. I had gotten good enough at the mechanics that I had started spending my attention on how I appeared instead of on what I was doing. The competence made me careless in a way that pure incompetence never had. When I was terrible at it, I stared at the field like it might attack me.

I have found that pattern in myself since, and I do not think I have solved it. It showed up in chemistry when I stopped writing out the intermediate steps because I could do them in my head, and then could not find my own error on the semester exam. It showed up in debate when I started improvising rebuttals because improvising looked better than reading from a flow sheet, and lost a round I should have won on the extended argument I forgot to extend. Both times the tell was the same: I was performing the task instead of doing it.

I do not have a clean method for catching it. What I have is Wes on his back in the stubble, and my uncle's flat sentence, and a physical memory of my own hands on a wheel I was not looking through. When I notice I am enjoying being observed, I try to put in the clutch. Sometimes I remember in time.
