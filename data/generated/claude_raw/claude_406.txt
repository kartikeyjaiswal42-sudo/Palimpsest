I have a desk that I don't use anymore. It's pale wood, ordinary, the kind of thing you buy from a store because it's functional and not expensive. My mother set it up in the corner of my bedroom before I started the homeschool program, and I spent eleven months at that desk. Almost a full year of high school happened there.

I was sick in a way that nobody could quite diagnose for the first few months. Fever that came and went. Joint pain. Exhaustion that was disproportionate to anything I was doing. Doctors ran tests. Tests came back normal or abnormal in ways that didn't quite add up to anything. Eventually it was diagnosed as some kind of viral aftermath, the sort of thing where the virus was gone but your body was still processing what it had done to you.

Homeschooling was supposed to be temporary. That's what everyone said. You'll feel better soon and then you'll catch up with the regular school. You'll be fine. But I didn't feel better soon. The months went by. I watched videos of classes instead of sitting in classrooms. I took tests alone at the desk. I did worksheets. I read books that my teachers had recommended or assigned or just thought I should read. The desk became the container for my education. It was where high school was happening.

What nobody tells you about chronic illness in high school is how isolating it is. Not just physically, but in the way time moves. While I was at the desk, my friends were forming the kind of memories that matter in retrospect. They were eating lunch together in new configurations. They were dealing with whatever drama was happening that year. They were bonded by the experience of actually being there. I was having a completely different experience.

The desk represented that year in a way that's hard to explain without sounding dramatic. Some days I would wake up and feel almost fine and I would be furious at having to be at the desk instead of at school. Some days I would wake up and feel terrible and the desk was the only place I could be. It was neutral territory between the person I had been before I got sick and the person I was becoming after it.

What I learned at the desk was different from what I would have learned at school. I learned how to be alone with ideas. I learned that education doesn't require an audience. I learned that I could structure my own learning, and sometimes my structure was better than someone else's would have been. I learned how to persist through monotony and doubt.

I got better eventually. Slowly at first, then more noticeably. I went back to regular school for my junior year. I was a year ahead in some classes because of the way the homeschool program had worked out, and behind in others. I was more confident in my own understanding of concepts, less confident in my ability to handle the social dimensions of school.

Now I don't sit at the desk anymore. I use it sometimes to do college applications or write essays, but it's not the center of my life anymore. It's just an object in my room. But I think about that year a lot. I think about who I was when I was sick. I think about how circumstances shaped me in ways I didn't choose. The desk is ordinary. What happened there was not.
