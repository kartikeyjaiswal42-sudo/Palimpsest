Tomás washed and I dried. That was it. That was the whole thing, every night for eleven months, and I did not think about it once while it was happening.

He came to us from Braga in the September I turned fifteen. My mother had signed us up for the exchange programme without much discussion, and I had a picture in my head of what a good host was, and the picture was: someone who talks. Fills the gaps. Explains things. Makes the guest comfortable by never letting there be a silence.

So for the first fortnight I was exhausting. I narrated our kitchen. I explained what a roundabout was. I asked him at least four times a day whether he was okay, which is a question that gets worse the more you ask it. He was polite about it. His English was better than he let on, and he used the gap deliberately, the way you would.

The dishes started because our dishwasher broke in October and nobody fixed it for seven weeks. Then it got fixed and we kept doing them by hand, and I could not tell you why, and neither could he.

It went like this. He washed, because he was faster. I dried and put away, because I knew where things lived. Twenty minutes, give or take. And here is the part that matters: we mostly did not talk. Not because it was awkward. Because our hands were busy and there was a rhythm to it and neither of us needed to prove anything.

Things came out sideways in those twenty minutes that never came out at dinner. That his father had not wanted him to come. That he found our house cold — not emotionally, literally, ten degrees colder than his — and had been sleeping in a coat since November without saying. That he was frightened of going back to Portugal a year behind. Nobody announces that over a meal with four people looking at them. You say it to a saucepan.

What I had believed, firmly enough to act on it for two weeks, was that hospitality is a performance of warmth. Talk, ask, include, repeat. I got that from my grandmother, who is genuinely like that and can carry a room. But my version was not warmth. It was a way of managing my own discomfort at not knowing what he was thinking, and it put all the work on him. Every question was a thing he had to answer in his second language while tired.

The dishes did the opposite. A shared task with a clear end, silence as a normal condition rather than a failure, and the two of us side by side rather than facing each other — which I would have laughed at before.

He went back in July. We got a new dishwasher last year and I still do a couple of pans by hand most nights, standing there for four minutes with nobody next to me, which my brother thinks is deranged.

I have started noticing where else this applies. The best conversation I have ever had with my father was while we were changing a tyre. I no longer think that caring about someone means producing evidence of it. Sometimes it means finding something dull to do together and then getting out of the way.
