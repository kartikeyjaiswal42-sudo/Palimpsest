My grandfather's Pentax K1000 doesn't have a light meter that works. It used to, probably, back when he bought it in 1978, but by the time it got to me the little needle inside the viewfinder just sat there, dead center, no matter how bright or dark the room got. I didn't know that when I started. I thought I was doing something wrong for the first two months.

Mr. Alvarez, who runs the photography elective at my school, told me flatly to stop using it. He'd been shooting for thirty years, had actual prints hanging in a gallery downtown, and when I brought the Pentax to class he picked it up, checked the meter, and said the glass was nice but the body was junk without a working meter, and that I should borrow one of the school's digital bodies instead, the ones with the little exposure bar right there in the screen. He wasn't being unkind. He just thought I was making things harder than they needed to be, and for someone who cared that much about the craft, I figured he probably knew.

I used the digital body for exactly one roll's worth of shooting, if you can call digital images a roll. Then I went back to the Pentax, and I didn't tell him for a while.

Here's what I couldn't explain to him at the time, not well anyway: guessing had become the point. Every morning before school I'd stand on the porch for about thirty seconds and just look at the sky, figuring out in my head what the light was doing — overcast, so open up a stop, or hard noon sun, so stop down and keep it fast. Sunny 16, that's the old rule, f/16 at a shutter speed matching your film speed on a bright day, and you adjust from there by feel. I did this every single morning for most of a school year. Thirty seconds, sometimes less. My mom used to ask what I was doing standing out there like I was waiting for a bus that wasn't coming.

I disagreed with Mr. Alvarez, and I still think he was more right than I was, technically. A meter is more accurate, full stop. My exposures were inconsistent for a long time — I have a shoebox of half-black and half-blown-out frames to prove it, some of them genuinely unusable, whole rolls I wasted on a wedding I wasn't even supposed to be shooting. But I wasn't trying to get accurate, not at first. I was trying to get to a place where I didn't need the machine to tell me what I already knew, and that only happens through repetition, through standing on a porch every single morning until your eye starts doing the math without you noticing it's doing math at all.

By spring I could look at a scene and call the exposure within half a stop, sometimes closer. I brought a strip of negatives to show him — not to prove him wrong exactly, more because I wanted him to see what the guessing had turned into over the months he didn't know about. He held the strip up to the window, went quiet for a second, and said, well, huh. Then he said I should still get a real light meter for anything that actually mattered, any job I got paid for.

He wasn't wrong about that either. I bought a handheld one eventually, a little Sekonic, secondhand, and I use it now for the two paid shoots I've done. But most mornings I still stand on the porch first, before I even think about the meter, just to see if I still remember how to read a sky. Some habits stop being about getting the right answer and start being about staying in practice for a kind of attention that nothing else in my life really asks of me.
