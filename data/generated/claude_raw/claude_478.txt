My father said it was a waste of space. He said a lot of things, actually—that balconies were for decoration, that growing food in pots meant the soil became exhausted and you'd get nothing worth eating, that if I wanted chillies I should buy them from the market like normal people. He said this as he stood over my small collection of pots, watching me pour water into the soil one morning before school.

I had been reading about agriculture. Not in textbooks for class, but actually reading, staying up too late with articles about urban farming and soil regeneration. My father saw this as peculiar. He's an accountant who trusts numbers and systems, and there was nothing systematic about my half-dozen pots arranged in no particular order on our balcony in Mumbai. The idea that I could take seeds and sun and water, and that something edible would come from this, seemed to him like fairy tale logic applied to real life.

But I started anyway. I planted three varieties of chilli seeds in recycled containers, and for weeks nothing happened except my father's sideways looks whenever he passed through the kitchen. Then the first seedlings emerged, thin green threads that looked impossibly fragile. I watered them carefully, moved them when they seemed to need more sun, talked to them in the morning before anyone else was awake. My father made a comment about how I was throwing away water that could have gone to actual plants in the building's garden.

This part still bothers me. This part is where I pushed back in a way that made the space between us wider. I told him he was stuck thinking about agriculture the way his generation always did—with big fields and old methods, never questioning whether there was another way. I said just because he didn't understand something didn't mean it was foolish. I remember the exactness of how this came out, designed to hurt a little.

The chillies grew. By August, the plants were flowering. By September, small green peppers were forming. My father didn't apologize, and I didn't expect him to. But he stopped with the comments. What he did instead was different—he started bringing up the plants in passing, asking me practical questions about them. "Did you change the soil yet?" "What's the pH supposed to be?" He researched regeneration too, I think, though he never said so directly.

By November, I harvested the first batch. They were small and intensely spicy, more so than anything from the market. I gave some to my mother, some to our downstairs neighbor who had always been kind to me. I kept one pepper and showed it to my father. He looked at it the way he looks at a balance sheet—as evidence that something worked according to principle.

We still haven't resolved it entirely, that disagreement about what's possible. He still doesn't think growing food on a balcony makes economic sense. I still think there are ways of living that matter beyond what the numbers show. But now he waters the plants sometimes when I'm at school, and last week he asked if I wanted more pots for next year. We haven't said any of this to each other directly. We're just both choosing to live with the question open.
