I quit piano on a Tuesday in October, told my teacher I wasn't coming back, and felt, for about three weeks, an enormous relief, like I'd put down something heavy I hadn't noticed I was carrying. Eleven years of lessons, competitions, juries, a wall of certificates my mom insisted on framing. Gone, just like that, over a five-minute conversation in a parking lot.

Here's what I got wrong, and I didn't figure it out for almost two years: I told myself I was quitting music. I wasn't. I was quitting the specific, narrow, competitive version of music I'd been handed since I was six, the one measured in points off a judge's sheet and rankings posted on a corkboard, and I mistook my exhaustion with that structure for exhaustion with the thing itself. I stopped touching a piano almost entirely for a year and told anyone who asked that I was "done with music," said it flatly, like a fact about myself, the way you'd say you're done with a sport after an injury.

What actually happened is I got a cheap laptop for my sixteenth birthday and, out of boredom more than anything, downloaded a free version of some music software a friend used for a class project. I wasn't trying to compose. I was mostly trying to make a weird beat to annoy my sister through the wall. But I stayed up until two in the morning that first night, layering a bass line under a chord progression I half-remembered from a Chopin piece I used to hate practicing, and I looked up and it was two a.m. and I genuinely didn't know where the time had gone. That hadn't happened with piano in years, not since I was small and lessons were still new.

It's the same underlying thing, I think, that used to captivate me before competition flattened it — the way a chord can almost resolve and then doesn't, the way silence between two notes is doing as much work as the notes themselves, the actual architecture of why a piece of music makes you feel something specific instead of something general. I used to feel that as a kid banging around on the keys before anyone was grading me on it. I'd just forgotten, or buried it under eleven years of being told exactly how to play things correctly.

Now I lose hours to it constantly, in a way that worries my parents a little, honestly, because it's not attached to any external reward the way competitions were. Nobody's handing me a plaque for a three-minute track I made at midnight. I don't care. I go down forum rabbit holes about mixing technique, I rewatch the same twenty-minute video on modal interchange four times until it clicks, I have a Discord server full of people twice my age who make music in their bedrooms and who answer my dumb questions at eleven p.m. without any condescension at all. There's a guy who runs a tiny studio near my house, Marcus, who lets me sit in sometimes on Saturdays and watches me work without correcting anything unless I ask — which is the opposite of every piano lesson I ever had, and it turns out that's exactly what I needed.

I don't regret quitting, even knowing what I know now. If I hadn't quit, I don't think I'd have found my way back to the actual thing underneath it, the part that had nothing to do with rankings. But I do think about the eleven years sometimes and wonder what I'd have made of them if someone had told me, early on, that being done with competition wasn't the same as being done with music. I might have believed that competitive piano version of me could disappear and something truer would still be standing underneath. I didn't believe it then. I do now, mostly because I've watched it happen to me at midnight, more than once, with a laptop and headphones and no judge anywhere in the room.
