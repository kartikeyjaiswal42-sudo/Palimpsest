For four years I sang in a church choir I did not believe in, and the thing I taught myself there was how to read music, which nobody ever sat down and explained to me.

I joined because my grandmother asked. She had sung in that choir since before my mother was born, and when her knees got bad she wanted somebody in the family holding the alto line. I said yes the way you say yes to a grandmother. I was thirteen and I had already quietly decided that I did not believe any of it, and I did not tell her that, and I still haven't.

Nobody in the choir taught anyone anything. That is not a complaint, it's just how it worked. The director handed out photocopied sheets on Thursday, played the alto line once on the piano, and then we sang. If you could not read the page you learned by ear, which most people did, and which I did for the first eight months. I would follow the woman beside me, half a beat behind, and hope.

The habit started because I was embarrassed. On the bus home from Thursday rehearsal, twenty minutes, I would put the sheet on my knees and try to work out which mark meant which sound. I had the melody in my head from rehearsal, so I had the answer key. All I had to do was match the shape on the paper to the shape in my memory. Up meant up. A filled-in note went past faster than a hollow one. Two lines and then a dot after a note meant it stretched.

Twenty minutes on a bus, once a week, for four years. That is the whole method. I never bought a book. I never looked anything up until much later, and when I finally did I found out I had gotten the names wrong for almost everything — I called the flat sign "the little b" for two years — but the system underneath was right, because I had built it out of things I could actually hear.

What learning it that way showed me is that I am much slower and much more stubborn than I thought I was. I did not have a breakthrough. There was no Thursday where it suddenly made sense. There was a Thursday about a year and a half in when the director put a new piece in front of us, something we had never heard, and I opened my mouth and the right note came out, and I remember being more suspicious than pleased. I thought it was luck. It happened again the next week.

I still sing there. I still don't believe it. My grandmother knows now — she asked me directly two winters ago and I did not lie to her, and she said that was fine and that I should keep coming, and I have. The disagreement is not settled and I do not think it will be.

But I can sight-read now, more or less, and I got it entirely off a bus seat with a photocopy on my knees, and I know exactly how long it took because I was there for every one of those twenty-minute pieces. It has made me distrust anyone who says a thing can be learned quickly. Mostly it has made me patient with the version of myself who is still bad at something, because that version is the one who does the actual work.
