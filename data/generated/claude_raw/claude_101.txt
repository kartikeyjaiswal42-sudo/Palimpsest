The object is a tire lever, cheap orange plastic, one of three that came in a five-dollar patch kit my neighbor Mr. Duval gave me two summers ago, and it's the only one of the three that hasn't snapped yet, which feels like it should mean something even though I know it's probably just luck.

I started fixing bicycles for the kids on my street almost by accident. A neighbor's son, maybe eight years old, was standing in his driveway crying over a flat tire he had no way to fix and no money to take anywhere, and I happened to be walking past with nothing better to do that afternoon, and I said I'd take a look, mostly because I didn't want to keep walking past a crying kid and pretend I hadn't noticed. I didn't actually know how to fix a flat. I looked it up on my phone standing right there in his driveway, patched the tube badly, and sent him off wobbling but riding.

Word got around the way things get around on a street with more kids than cars. Within a month I had a rotating list of neighborhood bikes lined up against our garage most Saturdays — chains that had jumped their gears, brakes that squealed, one memorable bike with training wheels so bent they'd started digging trenches in the driveway. Mr. Duval, who'd fixed his own bikes for probably fifty years before I ever showed up, started coming out to supervise, then to help, then, eventually, to just hand me tools before I asked for them because he'd learned to predict what I'd need next.

He gave me the patch kit that Christmas, wrapped badly in newspaper, no card, just left on our porch with my name written on it in marker. I don't think he meant it as a big gesture. I think he just had an extra kit and knew I'd use it. But it was the first time an adult outside my family had looked at something I did on my own initiative and decided it was worth investing five dollars and a little bit of faith into. That's a small thing to say out loud and a much bigger thing to actually feel, standing in a driveway holding a present from a man who'd watched me ruin at least four inner tubes before I learned to do it properly.

I still use that same lever, worn smooth now where my thumb sits, even though I could afford better tools myself at this point from the little bit kids' parents have started insisting on paying me. I've fixed maybe sixty bikes over two summers, mostly small stuff, occasionally something that took me a full weekend of watching videos to figure out. None of it made me any money worth mentioning, not really, and none of it was ever going to.

What it did was give me a strange, specific kind of gratitude I hadn't expected, aimed less at the kids who thank me with a lot of enthusiasm and more at Mr. Duval, who noticed a kid trying badly at something and decided the right response was tools, not correction. He never once told me I was doing it wrong, even when I clearly was. He just kept showing up with his own hands ready, letting me be the one who actually held the wrench.

I think about that orange lever more than its five-dollar plastic self probably deserves. It's outlasted two better ones I bought myself since. I keep meaning to replace it and I never do, mostly because I think some tools earn the right to keep being used past the point where it makes practical sense, just for what they were there for the first time.
