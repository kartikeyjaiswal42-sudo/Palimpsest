The summer I turned sixteen I got a job picking peaches at an orchard forty minutes outside town, mostly because it paid better than the movie theater and my cousin said they were always short-handed in July. I lasted the whole season, eleven weeks, six days a week starting at six in the morning before the heat became unbearable, and the person who taught me almost everything I actually learned that summer was a woman named Ofelia who I treated, for the first few weeks, with an embarrassing amount of thoughtlessness.

She'd worked that orchard for something like eighteen years. She picked twice as fast as anyone else on the crew, moved down a row like she wasn't thinking about it at all, and corrected my technique on my second day without being asked, showing me how to cup a peach and twist instead of pull so I wouldn't bruise the flesh or strip next year's bud along with it. I remember being irritated by this, which is an ugly thing to admit. She didn't talk much, mostly kept to her own row, and I filed her, without really examining why, as someone I didn't need to make an effort with, someone separate from the version of the summer I was having with the other teenagers on the crew who joked around and complained about the heat together at lunch.

About four weeks in, rushing to hit the count that got you a end-of-week bonus, I knocked a full flat of peaches off the back of the trailer. Twenty-some pounds of fruit, most of it bruised beyond selling, right as the foreman was walking the row doing his afternoon count. I stood there doing the math on how much that flat was worth against my hourly rate and understanding, with total clarity, that I was about to lose the bonus and possibly get written up on top of it.

Ofelia was two trees down. She didn't say anything to me. She walked over, looked at the flat for about two seconds, and started pulling perfect peaches out of her own bins to replace mine, fast, without a word, finishing before the foreman reached us and simply nodding at him when he glanced at my flat and moved on. It took me a second to understand what had actually happened, that she'd given up part of her own count, her own bonus, for a girl who up to that point had barely bothered to learn how to pronounce her name correctly.

I tried to thank her properly that afternoon and she waved it off, said something in Spanish to another picker that made them both laugh, and went back to her row like it hadn't cost her anything, though I knew by then exactly what a flat of peaches was worth to someone getting paid by the pound. I started paying attention to her after that in a way I hadn't bothered to before. I learned she had three grandchildren she was saving for, that she'd been the fastest picker on that crew for over a decade, that the thing I'd read as coldness in her was really just a woman who didn't waste words on people who hadn't earned them yet, and that I apparently hadn't, not for a month.

Here's the part I still think about. I never actually apologized for those first few weeks, for deciding she wasn't worth talking to before she'd done anything to me at all. I meant to, near the end of the summer, and kept not finding the right moment, and then the season ended the way seasons end, everyone scattering back to whatever the rest of their year looked like, and I never got her last name or a way to reach her. I went back the following June hoping to find her on the crew and was told she'd retired that spring after almost twenty years, and that was it, that was the whole ending, no goodbye, no thank-you that actually landed.

I think about that flat of peaches more than almost anything else from being sixteen. Not because of what it cost her, though it did cost her something real, but because of how easily I'd decided, before I knew a single true thing about her, exactly how much attention she deserved. She gave me more than she owed me and I gave her, in return, a summer of near-silence and one thank-you that came out too small and too late to matter.
