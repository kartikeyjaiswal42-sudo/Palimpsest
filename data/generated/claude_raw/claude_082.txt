Mr. Kapoor lived three doors down from us, and he'd been asking for help with his smartphone since probably the day smartphones were invented. My parents kept saying I should go help him—he was elderly, he was lonely, and his daughter lived overseas and wanted him to stay connected with his grandchildren through video calls. I put it off for months.

When I finally agreed, I thought it would take maybe an hour. I figured I'd show him how to open apps, send a text message, maybe video call, and I'd be done. It turned out to be far more complicated, and not in the way I expected.

The technical part wasn't the problem. Any seventeen-year-old can show someone how to navigate a screen. The problem was that Mr. Kapoor's entire relationship with technology was based on fear. He believed that if he pressed the wrong button, he would break something permanently. He was terrified of the internet—not in a reasonable, "I'll be careful about what information I share" way, but in a deep, existential way, like it was this dangerous wilderness he might wander into and never return from.

I tried to be patient. I really did. I showed him where the app store was, how to search for things, how to download an app safely. I explained that nothing he could do with his thumb would break the phone. I said it probably a hundred times in different ways.

But he couldn't actually believe it, and I got frustrated.

There's one moment I keep thinking about. He'd asked me to help him send a message to his granddaughter in London, and I'd shown him exactly how to do it. Then he looked at the keyboard and said he was afraid he'd spell something wrong. I made some comment like, "People spell things wrong in messages all the time, it's fine," and I could see him physically retreat from the phone. He literally didn't pick it up again.

I realize now that what I interpreted as him being unable to learn was actually him being afraid, and I'd responded to his fear with impatience instead of understanding. My comment wasn't mean, exactly, but it was dismissive. It was the kind of thing that confirmed for him that he couldn't do this, that this world of technology was too complicated for someone his age.

We spent three sessions working through things. By the end, he could send messages and make video calls and access his emails. Technically, I taught him what he wanted to know. But I'm pretty sure he still feels like he doesn't understand how it works, like he's just following a script of steps that I'd outlined and that if anything goes wrong, he'd be helpless. That's not actually learning. That's just being coached through something.

Last week, I saw a video call notification pop up from him when I was in his house. He'd successfully initiated one on his own without asking for help. It was a tiny thing, and he seemed embarrassed about it, like he'd probably done something wrong even though he'd obviously gotten it exactly right. I told him it was perfect and that he was doing great. I meant it, but I also wish I'd said things like that more often during our lessons.

I still think about what I should have done differently. I should have moved slower. I should have taken his fears seriously instead of treating them like something to brush past. I should have realized that for someone in his seventies, this represents a genuinely significant challenge—not because he's not smart enough, but because technology is designed by young people for young people, and it doesn't naturally make sense to someone from a completely different era.

I messed that up. I taught him the mechanics of how to use a phone, but I didn't teach him to actually feel capable with it. And I'm not sure that's something I can fix now.
