My cousin Bilal was six when he decided that the best possible thing in the world would be a game about a frog. Not a frog doing anything in particular. Just a frog. He told me this on a Sunday in his kitchen while eating a yoghurt, and because I had nothing better to do and no idea how hard it would be, I said I would make one.

The habit that came out of that lasted three years, and it was always the same shape: forty minutes on a Sunday afternoon, at the small table by his back door, with a laptop that overheated and a six-year-old giving notes.

I did not take a course. I did not have a teacher. What I had was a problem so specific that every question I asked the internet had to be phrased exactly right or it returned nothing useful. How do I make a thing move when I press a key. How do I stop the thing moving through a wall. How do I make it so the frog can jump but not jump again in the air. Each answer took a Sunday. Sometimes two. I would find code on a forum that almost worked, paste it in, watch the frog vibrate itself into the ceiling, and spend the next week thinking about why.

The thing learning it without a teacher showed me is that I had badly misunderstood what understanding feels like. In school, understanding feels like recognition — you see the worked example, it makes sense, you move on. Alone, understanding only ever arrived as the ability to predict what would break next. I could not tell you the formal definition of a collision box for most of the first year. But I could tell you, before I ran it, that the frog was going to sink through the lily pad if I moved him after checking his position instead of before. That was knowledge. It just didn't look like the kind I got graded on.

The other thing is that Bilal was a better teacher than any tutorial. He did not care about my code. He cared that the frog felt heavy when it landed. He would say "he's floaty" and I would have no idea what that meant for four weeks and then one Sunday I would change the downward acceleration from a constant to something that increased and he would say "yes, that." He gave me the only kind of specification that actually matters: a person who wants something and cannot tell you how to build it.

The game exists. It is called Ribbit and it is not good. There are eleven levels and a bug in the ninth where the frog can get stuck behind a rock if you approach it from the left. Bilal is nine now and has moved on to games with guns in them. But he still has it on the old laptop and he still, occasionally, gets stuck behind the rock on purpose, because it makes him laugh.

Forty minutes a week is nothing. It was so small I never once thought of it as work, which is probably why I kept doing it. Three years of nothing turned into the only thing I know how to do properly.
