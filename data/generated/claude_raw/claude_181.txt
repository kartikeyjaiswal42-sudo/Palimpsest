There are one hundred index cards in a rubber band on my desk, and the rubber band is the second one, because the first snapped in March from being stretched around the stack every single night for eight months. Card sixty-four is bent in the corner, softer than the rest, and it says, in my handwriting on one side and my mother's on the other: What is the supreme law of the land? The Constitution. She could not spell "Constitution" the first eleven times she wrote it. I know because I made her write it every time she got the question right, just so the word would go from her ear to her hand and stay there.

My mother started studying for her citizenship test the fall of my sophomore year, after eleven years of green card renewals and a nursing job that mostly kept her too tired at night to sit down with a workbook. I made the cards because the study guide the immigration office gives you is dense and formatted like a legal document, a hundred questions in tiny print with no room to write, and she needed something she could hold in her hand on the bus, at her lunch break, in the fifteen minutes before she fell asleep.

We quizzed most nights at the kitchen table after her shift, starting around nine, when she was already running on fumes. Some nights she got through the whole stack. Some nights she got through twelve cards and put her head down for a second that turned into twenty minutes, and I'd sit there with the cards and just let her sleep, because I understood, even at fifteen, that pushing her through the rest wouldn't teach her anything except how much she hated the process.

The hardest card for her, by far, was the one about the Constitution's amendments — how many there are, twenty-seven, and what the first one protects. She'd get the number right and blank on the content, or get the content right and forget the number, for weeks. I started making up small, dumb tricks to help her remember, rhymes mostly, nothing sophisticated, and one of them actually worked, and hearing her say the answer correctly, unprompted, on a random Tuesday in January, is one of maybe five moments in my life I can call up completely, in full detail, whenever I want to.

She passed her test in April. Six for six on the civics questions the officer asked her, and she called me from the parking lot of the federal building before she'd even gotten in the car, crying in a way I'd never heard her cry before, which is to say happily, which I don't think I'd actually witnessed from her until that phone call.

I kept the cards afterward instead of throwing them out, which is maybe strange, since they've done their job and she'll never need them again. But card sixty-four sits at the front of the stack on purpose, her handwriting getting steadier across eleven attempts at the same word, mine correcting nothing after attempt four because by then she didn't need correcting. It isn't really about the Constitution to me anymore. It's the most honest record I have of what eight months of trying, out loud, next to someone you love, actually looks like on paper.
