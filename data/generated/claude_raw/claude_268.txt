My sister Nadia thinks I should have stayed. This is a live argument in our house, still, fourteen months on, and I have not won it and neither has she.

I volunteered at Brackenridge Animal Rescue for eleven months and I was bad at it. Not bad in the way people mean when they are being modest. Bad. Nadia had been going for three years and got me in, and she is very good — she once spent forty minutes lying flat on a concrete floor with her hand out for a lurcher called Bess who would not come near anyone. I could not do any of that. Dogs got worse around me. I would go into a kennel to do enrichment and the animal would go rigid, and I would go rigid because it had gone rigid, and we would stand there feeding each other's fear until somebody senior came and did it properly in ninety seconds.

The belief I had, and it is a very ordinary belief, is that caring about something makes you good at it. I did care. So I assumed competence would arrive with hours logged, and that anything else was a failure of commitment.

I gave it eleven months. What I eventually had to accept is that the thing I was missing was not commitment, it was a particular stillness — the ability to be calm in a way that is not performed, because a frightened dog is not reading your face, it is reading your whole body, and mine was telling the truth.

So I stopped. And I went and did the other thing instead, which is the part Nadia objects to. Brackenridge had a grant application half-finished and a foster-placement spreadsheet nobody had updated since 2022, and I am good at that. I rebuilt the records. I wrote the funding bid that got them the second van.

Nadia's argument is this: I quit the hard part. She says that anyone can do admin, that I chose the version of helping where nothing frightening happens, and that I let myself off. She says — and this is the sentence that gets under my skin because I cannot fully answer it — that the dogs did not need a better spreadsheet, they needed someone to sit on the floor with them.

My argument is that a van is eleven extra collections a week, and that eleven months of me making animals more anxious was not a moral achievement just because it was uncomfortable for me. That there is a kind of vanity in insisting on doing the difficult, visible job badly when you could do the invisible one well.

I believe that. I also think she might be right that I found the exit slightly too gratefully, and that I have built a very tidy justification for something that was, in the moment, mostly relief.

We had the argument again at Christmas. She said, you never even tried with Bess. I said I did, twice, and she was more frightened when I left than when I arrived. She said, that is the job. I said, no, that is your job.

Nothing was settled. Nadia still goes on Saturdays. I still do their accounts on Sunday nights, and I have not been back to the kennels. What I challenged was the idea that caring converts into capability if you just want it enough. What I have not worked out is whether that conclusion is honest or convenient.
