My mother thinks I go to the retention pond behind Value Foods because I'm avoiding grief, and every time she says it we end up in the same half-argument, standing in the kitchen with the dishes not yet done, both of us too tired at six in the morning to actually finish the fight. She isn't wrong, exactly. My grandfather taught me to bird two winters before he died, standing in that same weedy stretch of cattails and shopping-cart wheels with a pair of Bushnells that now live around my neck every school morning by 5:50. What she gets wrong is the rest of it — that going out there is a way of staying stuck, that a seventeen-year-old should not be spending her mornings alone with a bird app and a notebook when she could be sleeping, or eating breakfast at the table like a person in a family. We have never once agreed on this. I don't think we ever will.

Here is what actually happens out there. The pond is not beautiful. It exists because a strip mall needed somewhere to put its runoff, and the water carries a faint gasoline sheen some mornings, and a shopping cart has rusted into the far bank for as long as I can remember. But a green heron nests in the willow scrub on the north side every April, and in October the pond fills up with ring-necked ducks stopping over on their way somewhere better, and once, in January, I watched a Cooper's hawk take a mourning dove out of the air four feet from my face and felt my whole body go still in a way it never does in a classroom. My grandfather used to say the best birding happens in ugly places nobody else bothers to look at twice, and I think about that sentence more than almost anything else he told me.

My mother knows all of this. I've told her about the heron, shown her photos of the ducks, tried to get her to come once, and she said maybe and never did. The argument isn't really about birds. It's about what it means that I'd rather be alone with a dying man's binoculars at dawn than sitting across from her, and neither of us has found a way to say that plainly, so instead we argue about sleep schedules and whether I'm avoiding something, and I always leave for the pond a little too fast, the way you leave a conversation you're afraid of finishing.

What I would bring to a campus is that same stubborn attention, turned outward instead of used to dodge my mother at the breakfast table. I notice what's easy to walk past — a heron in a drainage ditch, a rare warbler blown off course into a courtyard hedge, the kid who's been eating alone in the dining hall for three weeks. I keep a list. I go back. I have never once managed to explain to the person I love most why an ugly pond full of shopping carts matters to me, but I can promise that whatever patch of scrub or forgotten courtyard exists on your campus, I will find it, and I will notice things about it that everyone else stopped seeing months ago. My mother and I still haven't finished our argument. I don't think either of us wants to, really — it would mean admitting the other one might be a little bit right.
