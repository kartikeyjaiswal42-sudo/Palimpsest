I hadn't ever seen snow until I was sixteen years old.

My family had moved around a lot, staying in warm places, and I'd grown up in India, which doesn't have snow basically anywhere. So I'd seen photos of snow, read descriptions of it, imagined what it would be like. But I'd never actually experienced it in person.

When we moved to a city that has real winters, I was expecting to be disappointed. Snow, I figured, would be like most things that are hyped up—less impressive in reality than in the anticipation. But then winter came and the temperature dropped and we woke up one morning and everything was covered in this thick white blanket.

The first thing that hit me was the quiet. It's weird that I remember that most, but snow changes how sound travels. The whole world seemed muffled and soft. The second thing was that it was beautiful in a way that photos really can't capture—this particular quality of light bouncing off white surfaces, the way individual flakes catch the sun.

But what really changed my mind was the coldness of it.

I stood outside for maybe thirty seconds and my hands started to hurt. My face went numb. The cold wasn't romantic or picturesque from inside the cold—it was urgent and demanding and real. My body was having a genuine reaction to this weather in a way I hadn't anticipated. This wasn't a nice aesthetic experience. This was something that mattered physically.

I came inside convinced that snow was actually terrible and I was going to hate the whole winter.

But then something shifted. I started paying attention to how my body adapted. My hands got less sensitive to the cold. I learned to dress properly. The shock of the cold in the morning started to feel invigorating instead of painful. By week two of the snow season, I was actually enjoying being outside in it.

The thing that changed my mind was going sledding with some kids from school. It was absolute chaos—there were probably twenty of us on this hill and half of us couldn't sled worth anything. People kept falling off and sliding into other people and laughing hysterically. I fell, like, three times and every time I laughed harder than the last time.

At one point, I was sitting on the sled at the top of the hill and I remember thinking, A month ago I thought I was going to hate this place because of the weather, and now I'm sitting on a hill in the snow genuinely having one of the best times I've had since we moved here.

It was this realization that my initial judgment had been wrong. I'd decided that snow was going to be this romantic thing, and when it turned out to be actually cold and uncomfortable, I'd decided it was bad. But neither of those judgments was quite right. Snow was just snow—cold and wet and beautiful and inconvenient all at the same time. The experience of it depended entirely on whether I was willing to be present in the actual experience rather than comparing it to what I'd expected.

That seems like a small thing to learn from seeing snow for the first time, but it stuck with me. I think about it now when I encounter something new and I'm tempted to write it off as disappointing because it doesn't match my expectations. I think about that day sledding and I remember that reality is usually more complicated and more valuable than I expected.
