I started keeping score at my father's cricket matches when I was eight because he asked me to, and asking me to do things was how we spent time together. He played in a weekend league on Saturday mornings, and keeping score seemed like a job important enough for me to take seriously. He gave me a small notebook and showed me how to mark each run, each wicket, each boundary. It had a language and a system that made sense.

For years, that's what I did. I sat in the stands behind the players' families and I tracked the game in my notebook. I learned the rules so I could understand what I was tracking. I learned to anticipate moments. I knew when someone was building a real innings and when they were just hitting lucky boundaries. I could feel the momentum of the game the way other people read sports instinctively.

My father loved that I was doing this. He'd ask me at dinner about the match, about how close it had been, about what I'd noticed about certain players. I was the keeper of the record. I had information he wanted. It made me feel useful and important in his world.

But when I was fourteen, I realized something uncomfortable. I wasn't actually enjoying the cricket. I was enjoying the feeling of being useful to my father. The cricket itself was background. I showed up Saturday mornings not because I cared about the game but because the game was an excuse to be near him, to have a job to do that made me feel necessary.

There was a particular match that clarified this for me. I was fifteen, sitting in my usual spot with my notebook, and the weather was terrible. It was unseasonably cold, and I was miserable. I wasn't interested in the game. I wasn't interested in being there at all. And I realized, sitting there shivering and annoyed, that I had no idea what was actually happening on the field. I was keeping score on autopilot while my mind was elsewhere, wishing I could be at home.

That evening, I told my father I didn't want to keep score anymore. I said it was boring and I was too old for it. What I didn't say was that I felt trapped by my own habit, by something that had started as connection but had become an obligation.

He looked genuinely hurt. I think he had believed that I actually cared about cricket, that I was developing a real passion for the sport we shared. But the truth was I had been performing interest to keep him happy.

I didn't go to matches for about a month. He didn't ask me to. But then one Saturday, I woke up and realized I missed it. Not the scoring, but something about being there. Being part of something he cared about. And I went back to the pitch, but this time without the notebook.

I sat and actually watched. I learned cricket not because I needed to track it, but because I wanted to understand the game he loved. And it turned out, when I stopped doing it for him, I actually liked it. The strategy interested me. The beauty of a well-timed shot interested me. The social aspect of the league interested me.

That's when I realized that my father had been trying to teach me something the whole time, and I'd completely missed it. He didn't want me to keep score. He wanted me to see what he saw. He wanted me to learn not because I was obligated, but because the thing itself was worth knowing.

Now I go to his matches without keeping score, and we talk about the game differently. Real talk, about what we actually noticed. And I understand that what I taught myself wasn't cricket, but the difference between doing something for someone and doing something for yourself.
