The keyboard was sticky. I pressed the same note again, and this time my grandfather's name hung in the air with it. He had died two years before I was born, so I only knew him through the harmonium—a wooden instrument that sat in our living room like a piece of furniture nobody touched. My mother said it was too delicate, that it had cost more than a motorcycle, that it needed to be left alone. She meant the way rich people leave paintings alone, as if looking too hard might damage them.

One afternoon I sat down in front of it anyway. My fingers found the keyboard in the dust. The ivory was yellowed and some keys had turned to cream, which made a map of the harmonium's age more than any calendar could. I pulled the bellows toward me and the mechanism groaned, a sound like someone waking up after decades of sleep. I pressed a note and it sang back to me, strange and nasal but utterly real. There was a harmonium and there was a note. There was no space between thought and sound.

I started going back without telling anyone. I didn't know how to read music. There were no videos of my grandfather playing, no recordings, no instructions in the bench. I could only experiment—try combinations until something felt right, press keys by proximity and intuition. My fingers learned the keyboard's temperament, its quirks. Some keys stuck if you didn't press them hard enough. The middle octave had a sweetness to it that the higher notes lost. If you held the bellows open too long, the tone became thin.

Weeks into this, I played something that sounded almost like a melody. It wasn't my grandfather's style, because I had no reference for his style, but it was mine. That seemed to matter more than I expected. I wasn't learning his way of playing or approximating what I thought a classical instrument should sound like. I was finding my own way on something that had belonged to someone else, using tools that carried their history but making something new.

My mother came downstairs one day while I was playing. I thought she would tell me to stop, to protect the delicate thing from my untrained hands. Instead she sat on the edge of the sofa and didn't say anything. When I finished, she told me my grandfather had learned the same way, alone, by ear. He never had a teacher either. He taught himself first, and after many years, he helped his sister do the same.

I kept playing after that, not because anyone encouraged me, but because the instrument had stopped feeling like a monument. It felt like a conversation.
