The scanner is called an RF gun and it weighs about six hundred grams, which does not sound like much until hour seven. It is bright yellow, with a rubber bumper and a pistol grip worn smooth at the trigger, and every one at the Brantley Road distribution centre has a number stencilled on the handle. Mine was 47. It was not mine — you sign one out at the start of shift and back in at the end — but for eleven months I took 47 whenever it was on the rack, because its trigger had a slightly shorter travel than the others and by August I could feel the difference.

I worked Thursday and Friday nights, ten until six, from the week I turned sixteen. My mother's hours had been cut and we had a plan about it that we did not discuss much. The warehouse is a hundred and ninety thousand square feet of racking under lights that never change, and there is no window anywhere in the picking area, so time inside it is measured entirely by the gun. It tells you where to go. Aisle, bay, level, quantity. You go, you scan, you take, you scan again, and it gives you the next one. It also tells you how fast you are doing it. There is a number in the corner of the screen that is your rate against target, and it updates constantly, and I would like to say I ignored it but I did not ignore it once in eleven months.

What the gun taught me, more than anything, is what it feels like to be measured by something that cannot see you. On a bad night the number goes red and there is no argument to make. It does not know that bay 14 in aisle J has a light out and you have to read the labels by phone torch. It does not know that the tote you just filled with forty-eight litres of fabric softener is heavier than the one the person ahead of you got. It is not unfair, exactly. It is just indifferent, which I found was a different and stranger thing.

I got fast. By spring I was running about a hundred and ten percent of target most nights, and I understood by then that this was not a virtue, it was a set of small tricks: hold the gun in your left hand so your right is free, walk the aisle in the direction that makes the next pick shorter, learn which SKUs come in cases you can split with a thumb. Nobody teaches you this. There is a training video about lifting. The actual job is transmitted between people at four in the morning, in fragments, near the vending machines.

The people are the part the gun does not record. Dariusz, who had been a structural engineer in Gdańsk and did the maths on his rate in his head faster than the screen did. Marie, who was sixty-one and slower than everyone and who nobody ever once complained about, because she was the only person who noticed when someone was having a bad week. A boy from my year named Callum who I had barely spoken to in five years of school and who became, on the night shift, someone I would tell things to. School has a hundred ways to sort people. A warehouse has one, and it is the number, and because the number is so obviously stupid, everyone quietly agrees to judge each other by something else instead.

I handed 47 back in for the last time in September, before sixth form got serious. The rack was in the same place, the lights were the same, the girl signing it out after me looked about my age and did not look up.

I am going to study economics, and I know how that sentence sounds after this one. But I want to be the kind of person who, when a system produces a number, asks what the number cannot see. I held one of those numbers in my hand for eleven months. It was yellow, it weighed six hundred grams, and it never once knew that the light in J14 was out.
