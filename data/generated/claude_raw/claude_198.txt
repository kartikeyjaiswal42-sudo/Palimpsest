My grandfather told me I shouldn't take shop class. He said it was beneath me. I was smart—I should be doing academic things, thinking about colleges that cared about that kind of smart. Shop class was for people who couldn't do academics. That's what he believed, and he had opinions about what I should believe too.

But I had already signed up. The welding class had started and I was interested in a way I couldn't quite explain to him. Something about taking metal and heat and making something solid appealed to me. I had always been good with my hands but never interested in expressing that at school. Welding seemed different.

My grandfather didn't forbid me from taking it. He just made his disappointment clear. He would ask if I was still doing that welding thing and the way he said it made it sound like a phase I would outgrow. He suggested I join the robotics team instead, which he considered more respectable. I kept taking welding.

What happened is I started to actually care about it. My teacher, Mr. Hassan, had this quiet way of explaining things that made sense. He didn't talk down to us. He treated welding like a real skill, like something worth taking seriously. I learned that welding requires precision and understanding and an ability to visualize three-dimensional structures. It's not mindless. It's mathematics and physics and art combined.

I made a series of progressively harder projects. I made simple boxes. Then I made a three-dimensional sculpture. By the end of the year, I made a table that my teacher kept in the shop because he said it was well done. I was proud of it in a way I wasn't proud of most of my schoolwork.

The argument with my grandfather was that he couldn't see why I would want to do this. He still doesn't, really. He's asked if I'm planning to be a welder professionally, like there's no point in knowing how to do something unless it's your career. I'm not planning that. I'm planning to study engineering, which appealed to me partially because of welding, because understanding how things are built matters for designing them.

But the fundamental disagreement is still there. He thinks trades are less valuable than intellectual work. I think that distinction doesn't make sense. Intellectual work that produces nothing is less valuable than skilled work that produces something real. Building a table matters. Understanding how to build things matters. These are intellectual activities.

We haven't resolved this disagreement. He still makes comments when I mention the welding class. He still suggests I focus on things he sees as more important. I still keep taking the class and keep being interested in it regardless of what he thinks. It's a stalemate of sorts.

What the class taught me is that people can be smart and wrong about what counts as intelligence. My grandfather is a brilliant person in many ways. But he's got this particular blind spot about manual skills. He doesn't see that thinking with your hands is still thinking. That creating something physical is still creating. That the binary between intellectual and manual work is false.

I'm not going to convince him of this. We're probably not going to have a conversation where he admits he was wrong. But I keep welding anyway. I keep coming to class and learning and making things. Not to prove him wrong exactly, but because I know he's wrong. And that knowledge is enough to keep going, even though we disagree about something I care about and someone I love thinks I shouldn't.
