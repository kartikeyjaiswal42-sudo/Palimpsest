I decided, in the fall of eighth grade, that I was going to stop speaking Tagalog at home. Nobody told me to. My parents never once asked me to, and my grandmother, who lived with us and spoke almost no English at all, certainly never asked me to. I decided it on my own, in the particular quiet way that thirteen-year-olds decide things that will matter more than they understand, sitting in the back of a classroom where a boy had mimicked my mother's accent at pickup the week before, not cruelly exactly, just carelessly, the way kids repeat things without weighing them.

I told myself it was about efficiency. English was faster for me by then, more natural, and switching between languages mid-sentence felt like static I didn't want to deal with anymore. That was the story I gave myself, anyway. The truer story is that I had started to associate the sound of my first language with being marked as different, and I wanted, badly, to stop being marked.

So I answered my mother in English when she spoke to me in Tagalog. I answered my grandmother the same way, even though her English vocabulary topped out at maybe two hundred words, even though I watched her face do something small and confused every time, a kind of recalibrating, before she'd either repeat herself in broken English or just let the thread drop. I told myself she understood more than she let on. I told myself this went on for almost a year, and it worked, in the sense that my accent flattened out and nobody at school commented on my house sounding foreign anymore.

What it also did was slowly wall my grandmother out of my daily life. Our conversations shrank to logistics — food, homework, whether I'd locked the door — because anything with nuance required a language I had decided I was too good, or too embarrassed, to keep using. She had stories about growing up during a typhoon that flooded her town for two weeks, about my grandfather courting her with letters he couldn't really write and had a neighbor draft for him. I know these stories now only in fragments, secondhand, because I spent a year making sure I wouldn't have to sit through them in the language they actually lived in.

I started speaking Tagalog again the following summer, clumsily, after I noticed how much my grandmother's face changed when I managed a full sentence, like a door had opened that she'd stopped expecting to open. It came back faster than I thought it would, muscle memory more than knowledge, but a year is a long time to withhold yourself from someone who has less time left than you do. I think about that gap more than almost anything else from those years, the version of myself who thought fitting in was worth that particular cost and didn't fully register what he was paying.

What I would bring to a campus is not some neat resolution to that story, because I don't think there is one. It's the specific attentiveness of someone who has watched a language nearly slip away and knows exactly how ordinary and how quiet that kind of loss can be — no dramatic scene, just a year of small refusals adding up. I notice, now, when someone's grandparent is sitting at the edge of a gathering not quite following the English around them, and I go sit next to them and ask what language they'd rather use. I bring the particular discomfort of having gotten something wrong that can't be fully undone, only kept from happening again, and the habit that discomfort built in me of not letting anyone go quiet at the edges of a room if I can help it.
