I believed, before the power went out for a week, that we needed electricity to have a normal life. Not just liked it, not just were accustomed to it—needed it. It was the foundation of everything. School, work, communication, refrigeration, heat, light, entertainment, everything. Without it, we'd be helpless.

My family proved me wrong in a way I didn't expect.

It started with a transformer fire on Sunday afternoon. We heard the explosion from our house, that distinctive electrical crack followed by silence. The power died. They said it would be restored within hours. By Tuesday, the company was saying they didn't have parts, that it might take longer. By Wednesday, they were saying a week, maybe more.

I was furious. We had a physics test scheduled for Friday. My younger sister had debate finals. My mother was supposed to work from home. Everything was going to be ruined. The first two days, our family moved around the house like we were in crisis mode. We kept trying lights and checking outlets, as if the power might return if we just checked enough times. We used flashlights. We complained about the cold. We argued about what to do.

On the third day, something shifted. My father taught my sister to make bread from scratch. Not toast, not the instant stuff—real bread, using ingredients we had and techniques from his mother's old cookbook. They worked on it together for hours, and I watched, expecting this to be a temporary novelty. But then they asked me to help, and suddenly I was sitting at the table doing something that had nothing to do with electricity.

My mother taught me to play chess. She'd tried to teach me before, years ago, and I'd dismissed it as boring. But with no television, no phone charging, no laptop, with nowhere else I really needed to be, I sat down and actually learned. The game has depth I'd never appreciated before.

By day five, something strange had happened. The power outage had stopped feeling like a disaster and started feeling like something else. We ate dinner together every night because the alternative was sitting in the dark. We played board games. My father told stories about his childhood. We took walks in the neighborhood, which we almost never did. We talked. Not surface talk, not the hurried kind we do when we're all distracted by screens. Real conversations.

That's when it hit me: I'd been wrong about what we needed. Not that electricity is bad—I wasn't naive enough to think that. But I'd believed we needed it to have a normal, good life. And then we went a week without it and had more quality time together than we'd had in months.

When the power came back on Friday morning, it was almost disappointing. The lights flickered on and we all blinked. My mother immediately went to charge her devices. My sister found the charging cable for her laptop. I felt the pull back toward the normal world, the connected world. We went back to our separate things.

But something in me had shifted. I understood that there was a difference between needing electricity and letting it organize our entire existence. That convenience isn't the same as necessity. That we had this whole other capacity for contentment that didn't require being constantly connected.

I still use technology the way I always did. I'm not pretending the power outage made me into someone who rejects modern life. But I question things differently now. When I find myself reaching for my phone out of habit rather than need, I remember those seven days. I remember that conversation with my father, the chess game I was learning, the bread my sister and I made together. I remember that before power outages were inconveniences, they were just how people lived. And people lived fine.

I'm not grateful for the transformer fire. I'm not grateful for the week without power. But I'm grateful for what it proved to me about what I actually needed versus what I thought I needed. That's a belief I don't think I'll ever question back.
