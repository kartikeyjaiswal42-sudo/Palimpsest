I have a green plastic watch, the kind with a rotating bezel and a strap that's cracked now from years of wear, and it's the object that carried me through the year I was the only one of my friends without a phone.

My parents held out until I was fifteen, longer than anyone else's parents in my group, and the disagreement wasn't really with them, it was with a friend named Priya, who thought I was being ridiculous about the whole thing and told me so, more than once, with real frustration in her voice rather than the joking tone she usually used with me. She thought I should have pushed harder, made a bigger case, maybe even gone around my parents to a grandparent or something, the way she'd seen other kids do. She respected my parents' right to decide things for our family, but she genuinely disagreed with how I was handling my own end of it, which she thought was too passive, too accepting.

I disagreed with her back. I told her I didn't want to fight a battle I didn't fully believe in myself, that some part of me actually agreed with my parents' reasoning even while it inconvenienced me constantly, and that pushing hard against something I half-believed felt more dishonest than just living with the restriction and finding workarounds. She thought that was me making excuses for not wanting to have an uncomfortable conversation at home. We argued about this a handful of times that year, never resolving it, both of us convinced the other one was missing something obvious.

The watch was my workaround. I couldn't text anyone to say I was running late, couldn't check a group chat to know where everyone had ended up meeting, so I got precise instead, obsessively precise, about time and place. If we said 4:15 at the fountain, I was at the fountain at 4:15, watch checked and rechecked on the walk over, because being unreachable meant being unmissable in every other way I could manage. I became, without meaning to, the most reliably on-time person in my friend group, the one people started building plans around because they knew exactly where I'd be and when.

Priya still brings up the phone thing sometimes, half as a joke now, saying she still thinks I should have fought harder, and I still tell her I'm not sure I would have won that fight even if I'd tried, and we've genuinely never landed on the same side of it. But I got the phone eventually, at sixteen, and by then the watch wasn't really about the workaround anymore. I kept wearing it anyway. It's sitting on my wrist right now, actually, cracked strap and all, a small daily reminder of a year I spent disagreeing with someone I respected about how much a person should fight the limits set on them, and never quite settling the question either of us was actually right.
