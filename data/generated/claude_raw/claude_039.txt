Every morning for three years, I have woken up at 6:10, twenty minutes before I actually need to, to help my younger sister stretch her hands.

Mia was diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis when she was nine, which meant that some mornings, especially in winter, she woke up with her fingers curled and stiff, barely able to close them around a toothbrush, let alone hold a pencil for first period. The doctors gave us a set of exercises — gentle range-of-motion stretches for her fingers, wrists, and knees, meant to loosen the joints before the day's swelling and use made them worse. My parents were usually already gone by 6:10, my dad to the hospital where he works nights, my mom to open the pharmacy she manages. So the stretching became mine, not because anyone assigned it to me exactly, but because one morning I found Mia crying quietly at the kitchen table, unable to open a granola bar wrapper, and I sat down and did it for her, and then I stayed and did the stretches with her, and then I just kept showing up at 6:10 the next day, and the one after that.

It is not a complicated routine. I sit across from her, and we go through each finger, each knuckle, gently, while she tells me about whatever she's annoyed at that week — a friend, a teacher, a show she's behind on. It takes maybe fifteen minutes. Some mornings her hands are fine and we do it anyway, more out of habit than medical necessity, because by then it had become less about her joints and more about the fifteen minutes themselves, the only time in the day when it was just the two of us with nowhere to be yet.

I did not choose this the way I chose to join the debate team or sign up for AP Chemistry. It chose me, the way most of caregiving does, through absence — someone had to be there at 6:10, and I was the one who was. For a long time I resented that a little, quietly, the way you resent things you'd never say out loud because saying them makes you feel like a bad person. I wanted to sleep until 6:30 like everyone else I knew. I wanted mornings that were just mine.

What changed my mind about it wasn't a single moment so much as watching, slowly, what the fifteen minutes actually did. Mia started sleeping better, I think because she knew the mornings weren't going to ambush her anymore. She started talking to me the way she doesn't talk to our parents, telling me things at 6:15 in the morning that she'd never say at dinner with everyone listening. And I noticed something about myself I hadn't expected: I got good at reading small changes in another person's body and mood before they said anything about it. I could tell from how she held a fork whether it was a stiff day. I could tell from her voice, half-asleep, whether she'd slept through the pain or fought it most of the night.

That skill — noticing the small stuff before it becomes the big stuff — is not something I learned in a classroom, and it's not something that shows up on a transcript. But it is, I think, exactly what I would bring to a campus that wouldn't otherwise be there. Not grand gestures. Not the kind of leadership that gets announced. The other kind, the unglamorous kind: showing up at the same time every day for someone who needs it, noticing when a roommate has gone quiet in a way that means something, sitting with a friend through a bad week without needing them to explain why it's bad. I've had three years of practice at exactly that, fifteen minutes at a time, and it is the single most useful thing I know how to do.

I still do the stretches most mornings, even now that Mia manages a lot of it herself and mostly doesn't need me to. She's twelve now, more independent, and some mornings she tells me to go back to sleep, she's got it. I get up anyway. I think what started as an obligation turned, somewhere in those three years, into the thing I am actually best at — not the arthritis part, the showing-up part — and it's the habit, more than any single achievement I could list, that I think would make your campus a slightly different, slightly more attended-to place than it would be without me in it.
