I could play the guitar for four years before I could read a single note of music, and for most of those four years I thought this made me better than the people who could.

I had a theory, and the theory had a hero. My uncle plays in a covers band and cannot read music, and he can hear a song twice and play it back, and when I was eleven I decided that this was the real thing and everything else was clerical. Notation was for people who could not hear. It was a crutch, a set of instructions for the deaf. I said this out loud, more than once, in front of people who could read music, and none of them argued with me, which I took as agreement and now suspect was pity.

So I learned by ear. By fourteen I could work out most things off a recording in twenty minutes, and at family parties I was the one with the guitar. My uncle told me I had a good ear, which was the only qualification I wanted.

Then at fifteen I auditioned for the county youth ensemble, because a girl I liked was in it, which is the honest reason. They put a piece of paper in front of me. I had never in my life been handed a piece of paper and asked to produce sound from it, and I sat there for maybe eleven seconds, and then I said I could play it if they played it to me first.

I did not get in. That is not the part I still think about.

The part I still think about happened three weeks later, at a workshop I went to anyway, where I ended up sitting next to a girl called Nadia who played the cello and read fluently. And I watched her sight-read something she had never seen, and I said — meaning it as a compliment, I genuinely thought I was being generous — that it was amazing how she could do it so mechanically.

She put the bow down. She asked what I meant by mechanically. And I explained my theory. The crutch, the deaf, the whole thing. I was fifteen and I explained the nature of music to a girl who had been playing since she was five.

She did not get angry. She asked me to sing her the second violin part of something I knew well, and I could not, because I had never once thought about it, because I had spent four years listening to melodies and chords and treating everything underneath as texture. Then she said something I have not been able to put down since. She said that reading did not replace her ear, it gave her ear more things to be interested in — that she could hear the middle of a piece because she had spent years looking at the middle of a piece on paper.

I had got it exactly backwards. I had thought notation was a substitute for listening. It is a technology for listening harder. It lets you hold four things at once and compare them, and go back to bar thirty-two, and notice that the thing that moved you in bar thirty-two was a viola doing something small. I had been proud of a limitation for four years. Worse, I had been proud of it publicly, and I had built a small identity on top of it, which is why it took an actual humiliation to shift.

I started learning to read that autumn, at fifteen, which is old for it, and it was awful. Reading music at fifteen when you can already play is like being an adult learning to swim: the failure is not just difficult, it is embarrassing, because everyone in the room associates the skill with children. I still read more slowly than people who started at seven and I probably always will.

What I got wrong was not really about music. I have thought about this a lot and I keep arriving at the same place. I had taken a real strength — the ear, which is real, my uncle is not wrong about that — and used it to justify not doing something difficult. And then I had gone further and turned the avoidance into a principle, and defended the principle to other people, which is the move that makes it hard to reverse. Nobody has to argue you out of laziness. They have to argue you out of a belief.

I now watch for it. When I find myself explaining why some skill is overrated, I have a small alarm that goes off, and the question it asks is: is this true, or am I just bad at it and would rather not be seen learning?

Nadia and I are still friends. She is applying to conservatoire. Occasionally she sends me a photograph of a score with something circled and no explanation, which is her idea of a joke, and I can now, slowly, work out what she means.
