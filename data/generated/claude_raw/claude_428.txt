My mother thinks that what I did last year was kind. We have been going round this for eight months and neither of us has moved.

I was a scribe. There is a boy in the year below me, Tomas, who is blind, and for two terms I sat in exams and in some lessons and wrote down what he said. Nobody trained me. There was supposed to be a training session and it was cancelled twice and then term started. So what I know about scribing I taught myself, in the room, while doing it, which is not a good way to learn anything and is how most people learn most things.

The first thing I learned was that I did not know what a scribe was for. I had assumed I was a pen. He would say words and the words would appear on the page and my job was to be transparent. That lasted about forty minutes. Because he would say a sentence and then stop and say "no, wait," and I would have written half of it, and now there was a decision to make about what to cross out that he could not see me making. Or he would spell a word and the spelling would be wrong and I would have to decide, in a fraction of a second, whether correcting it was help or cheating. There is no manual for that. Or if there is, it was in the training session that got cancelled.

I built my own rules. That is the honest version of "taught myself." I decided that I would read back everything, always, even when it was slow, even when he was impatient, because the alternative was that my version of his answer would go in and he would never know. I decided I would not fix spelling unless he asked, and that I would ask once at the start of every session whether he wanted me to. I decided that when he paused I would say "you're at, and therefore the pressure" and let him hear where he was, rather than waiting silently, because silence made him rush.

None of those rules were obvious. I got them wrong first. In the November mock I "helped" — he was going nowhere on a question and I read the question back with a slightly different emphasis, and the emphasis was a hint, and he took it. He got a better mark than he had earned. I have never told anyone that except my mother, and here.

Which is where the disagreement is.

My mother says I am being hard on myself. She says the whole point of an access arrangement is that the boy should not be disadvantaged, that a sighted student would have reread the question with their own emphasis anyway, that I am inventing a crime. She says this in the voice she uses when she has decided the conversation is finished. And she says the larger thing, which is that it was generous of me to do it at all, that most seventeen-year-olds would not have given up their frees, and that I should let myself have that.

I do not think it was generous. I think that is the part she will not hear. It was interesting. I wanted to be in the room. There is something in the mechanics of it — the exact latency between his sentence and my hand, the fact that a comma is a decision somebody has to make and normally the writer makes it and here it was me — that I found more absorbing than any lesson I sat in that year. That is not kindness. That is curiosity wearing a volunteer's badge, and being praised for it makes me feel like a fraud in a way I cannot explain to her without it sounding like fishing.

And the emphasis thing was real. She calls it a nothing. It was not a nothing to me because I felt the moment I did it — I felt myself decide to be liked rather than be accurate, which is exactly the failure mode a scribe has to not have.

We are not going to resolve it. I have stopped raising it. She thinks I have come round. What I have actually concluded is narrower: that the only reliable thing I learned without a teacher was where my own hand wants to go when nobody is checking, and that knowing that is not the same as being able to stop it. Tomas has a different scribe this year. She was trained. I hope she was told about the pause.
