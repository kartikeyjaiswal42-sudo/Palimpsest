The habit was that I wrote down what people said, exactly, in a small notebook, and it got me into more trouble than anything else I have done.

I started doing it in ninth grade for a boring reason. I joined the school paper and my first assignment was a piece about the new cafeteria contractor, and I interviewed the vice principal and then went home and wrote up what he had told me, and when it was published he said he had not said that. I did not have proof. He was probably not lying either — I think he genuinely remembered it differently — but the piece got pulled and I got a talking-to and my byline was replaced with the word Staff.

So I bought a notebook. The kind with the elastic band. And from then on, whenever anybody said anything to me for a story, I wrote it as they said it, in the moment, with the date at the top of the page. Not summary. The words.

For two years this was invisible. It looked like a tic. People teased me for it — my friend Ravi used to say things very slowly in a fake official voice so I could keep up. I filled four notebooks. Most of what is in them is nothing: what time the bus leaves for the debate meet, somebody's guess about the exam schedule, three pages about a broken water cooler.

Then in eleventh grade I wrote a piece about the school's counselling office. It was not an exposé. It was supposed to be a service piece about how to book an appointment. But in reporting it I found out that eleven students had been told there was a waiting list, and that there was no waiting list, and that the actual situation was that the counsellor's hours had been reduced to one afternoon a week in September without anyone announcing it. I put that in the piece, four sentences, near the end.

The article ran on a Thursday. On Friday I was in the principal's office with my faculty advisor and a man from the district whose role was never explained to me, and I was told that I had misrepresented the school, and that the counsellor had never said her hours were cut, and that I had invented the number eleven.

I had page seventy-three of notebook three. Date, time, her office, her words, including the sentence about the reduction and including her saying that she had asked twice about it. And I had the eleven students, each with a page.

It did not go the way it does in a film. Nobody apologised. What happened was that the room got quiet and the man from the district asked to see the notebook and I said he could look at the page and not the notebook, which I am still a little proud of, and after about ten minutes they told me I could go and that the matter was closed. The article stayed up. In January the counsellor's hours went back to three afternoons and no announcement was made about that either.

I got a reputation out of it that I am not sure I wanted. Teachers are careful around me now. One of them said, half joking, that talking to me is like being deposed.

But I have kept doing it, and I have thought a lot about why the habit worked, and I do not think it is because it protected me. It is that writing down exactly what someone says forces you to hear exactly what they said, which is different from what you expected them to say, and the gap between those two is where every real story I have ever found has been sitting.

I am on notebook seven. Most of it is still nothing.
