My older sister ran the lighting board for our school's fall play for three years before she graduated, and I inherited the job from her the way you inherit most things in our family, without much of a ceremony, just her handing me the binder of cue sheets and saying good luck, you'll need it, on her way out the door to college.

She still comes to opening night every year, even now, home from school for exactly one weekend that always happens to line up with our production, and she still sits in the booth with me for the first act like she owns some part of it, which, in a way, she does. This year we disagreed, and it wasn't really about lighting at all, though lighting was the argument we actually had.

I wanted to run the final scene of our show — a quiet two-person dialogue at the end — with a single warm spotlight and everything else dark, hard focus, no ambiance. She thought it needed a wash of blue behind them, something softer, more atmospheric, the way she used to run comparable scenes when it was her board. I told her the show wasn't hers anymore. I didn't mean it as cruelly as it came out, but it came out that way, and I watched something in her face close a little when I said it.

She backed off outwardly. Said fine, it's your call, your name's on the program this year, not mine. But she said it in the specific flat voice she uses when she's decided not to fight about something she still thinks she's right about, and I know that voice better than almost anyone alive, because I grew up underneath it, always one grade behind her, always walking into rooms where she'd already set the expectations.

We ran it my way both nights. The spotlight, the hard dark around it, exactly as I'd designed it. And I still don't know if it was right. Some nights, sitting in the booth after the show ends, I run that final cue back in my head and picture her blue wash instead and I genuinely cannot tell you which version serves the scene better. I only know I wanted, badly, for it to be mine, uncomplicated by her opinion, even a good one.

She left Sunday afternoon, back to school, and we hugged at the door the way we always do, and neither of us brought the lighting up again. That's not unusual for us — we don't really relitigate things, we just let them settle into the pile of small unresolved disagreements that seem to accumulate between people who love each other and used to share every single thing, right down to a booth eight feet wide, and now mostly share holidays and a handful of weekends a year instead.

I think about it more than the size of the disagreement should probably warrant. Not because I regret the spotlight choice, exactly. Because I keep noticing that I wanted to win an argument with my sister more than I wanted the scene to be as good as it could be, and I don't fully know what to do with that discovery about myself, sitting up in a booth she taught me to run, using a binder she built, doing a job that will always, in some sense, still partly be hers no matter whose name is on the program this year.
