We got the rescue dog in March, and I was genuinely convinced I could fix him. His name was Max. He'd been abused, which meant he was reactive, anxious, and basically feral in social situations. The adoption paperwork came with pages of instructions. Don't let him off-leash. Don't expect socialization. Don't bring him around other dogs. He might never fully adjust.

My parents warned me. They said not to set my expectations too high. But I was seventeen and convinced I had something other trainers didn't—time, patience, genuine care. I'd watched YouTube videos. I'd read books about dog training. I thought the issue was that Max had just never experienced genuine love and consistency.

So I trained him. Or I tried to. Every day after school, I'd work with him. Basic commands, positive reinforcement, trying to teach him that human interaction was safe. He'd make progress for a few days and then regress. He'd be calm and then suddenly aggressive with no apparent trigger. He'd seem like he was learning and then he'd act like he'd never encountered the concept of a leash before.

By May, I was frustrated in a way I'd never experienced before. I was angry at him for not responding to my training. I was angry at the world for breaking him so thoroughly that I couldn't fix him. I was angry at myself for believing I could fix something that everyone else had given up on.

Then one day I was in the backyard and Max just lay down next to me. Not in a trained way, not because I'd commanded him to do anything. He just... chose to sit next to me. He didn't do anything. He wasn't suddenly trained or fixed or better. He was still anxious and reactive and the same difficult dog. But for that moment, he was choosing to be near me.

That's when I realized I'd been approaching this wrong the entire time. I'd been trying to fix him, as if he was broken in a way that had a solution. As if enough training and patience would make him normal. I'd been measuring success by whether he could behave like a well-trained dog, and I'd been seeing his inability to reach that standard as a failure.

But sitting there with him doing absolutely nothing, I understood something different. Max wasn't a project. He wasn't something to fix. He was a dog who'd been through trauma and would probably always carry that. He would probably never be the kind of dog you could take to the park or let off leash at the beach. He would always be anxious. He would always have triggers.

And somehow that was okay.

It sounds simple when I say it now, but it changed how I thought about a lot of things. Not just Max. I was trying to fix myself the same way, trying to overcome things that maybe just needed to be accepted. I was measuring myself against a standard of being "normal" and seeing every difference as something that needed correction.

Max still doesn't do most of the commands I taught him. He still has bad days where he's reactive and scared. But he's here. He's my dog. And I'm here, understanding that he doesn't have to be fixed for me to love him.

I feel most myself in the backyard with him, actually, which I didn't expect. I expected to feel like myself doing something productive or impressive. Instead I feel like myself just being present with something broken and understanding that broken doesn't mean lesser.
