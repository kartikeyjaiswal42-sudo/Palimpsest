My grandfather has a brass measuring cup, about the size of a fist, with a lip on one side and a dent near the base where it was dropped a long time before I was born. It sat on a shelf in his room in Coimbatore and it sits on a shelf in our flat in Leicester now, and for most of my life it was just an object I had to move when I dusted.

He grew up in a village called Perumbakkam that is not there anymore. It was cleared in the seventies for a reservoir project, and the people were relocated to a town nine kilometres away with better roads and a school and no history. He was fourteen. He has told me about it perhaps two hundred times, and until I was fifteen I heard almost none of it, because he tells stories in a loop and I had decided, somewhere around age eleven, that the loop meant nothing new was coming.

When I was sixteen I decided to fix this. I was going to record him. I had a plan and a phone and a list of questions, and I was going to build an oral history — that is genuinely the phrase I used, to myself, in my own head — a proper document of a place that no longer existed, so it would not be lost.

It did not work, and the way it did not work is what I want to write about.

The first session lasted forty minutes and I got almost nothing. I asked him what the village looked like. He described the temple. I asked how many houses. He said, many. I asked what his father did and he told me a story about a bullock that had already been told at three weddings. I pushed him with the questions I had prepared, the good ones, the ones about how it felt to leave, and he answered them in about eleven words each and then asked if I wanted tea.

I tried again a month later and it was worse, because by then he knew what I was doing and he performed. He gave me the version he gives to guests. He got dates wrong that I knew he knew. Halfway through he said, in Tamil, "you are writing a report," and it was not a compliment.

I gave up for nearly a year. I told myself he was old and it was too late, which was a comfortable thing to believe and also unfair to a man who does the crossword faster than I do.

What I did next was accidental. Last August, cooking, my mother needed a measure for rice and could not find the cup we use, and my grandfather went and got the brass one off the shelf and said, without anyone asking, that this was the padi his mother had used and that in Perumbakkam a bride's family sent one to the new house, and that theirs had been left behind in the move and this one was bought in the town afterwards to replace it, and that it was the wrong size, slightly, and his mother complained about it for eleven years.

That was more than I got in eighty minutes of interviews. He talked for an hour. I did not record it. I have thought about that a lot and I have decided I am not sorry.

What I was wrong about was the form. I had assumed that a life comes out when you ask for it, in order, at a table. It doesn't. It comes out sideways, attached to objects, when someone needs a cup for rice. My questions were a container of the wrong shape, and every time I held it up he could see it and adjusted himself to fit.

So now I do not interview him. I cook with him. I ask about the thing in his hand. I have filled a notebook in fourteen months, badly, out of order, undated, and it contains more of Perumbakkam than my recordings ever would have.

The cup is still the wrong size. It holds about three-quarters of what it should.
