My grandmother thinks I'm wasting my time learning sign language, and she's told me so more than once, in the specific patient tone she uses when she believes she's protecting me from something I haven't lived long enough to see coming.

There's a boy in my chemistry class, Tomas, who is deaf, and who came to our school this year with an interpreter who isn't always available for every class period, every lunch, every hallway conversation that makes up most of what school actually is. I started learning ASL in September mostly because I was tired of watching him sit alone during the gaps where the interpreter wasn't scheduled, tired of a friendliness between us that had to route entirely through a laptop screen and typed sentences that lose almost everything a real conversation carries.

It was hard in a way I hadn't expected, not intellectually — I'm decent at picking up patterns — but physically, in my hands, which apparently have never been asked to carry meaning before and resisted the assignment for weeks. I practiced constantly, videos at night, a app on my phone during lunch, badly signed sentences at Tomas that he'd patiently correct without ever making me feel small for getting it wrong.

My grandmother, who raised me for large stretches of my childhood while my parents worked, found out about all this around November, and her reaction surprised me. She didn't think it was sweet. She thought it was a distraction from what she calls "the real work" — my grades, my college applications, the handful of things she believes actually determine whether a life goes well or badly. She grew up believing effort should be spent where it counts on paper, and to her, hours spent learning a language for one classmate, a language I might never use again after this year, counts as effort spent somewhere it shouldn't be.

We've argued about this more than once, always circling back to the same unresolved place. I tell her it matters because Tomas matters, because friendship shouldn't require a translator every single time, because some things are worth doing even if they don't show up as a line on a transcript. She tells me she loves that I think that way and she also thinks I'm being naive about how the world actually rewards people, that colleges won't ask who I signed hello to in a hallway, that I'll regret hours I could have spent on the SAT prep she keeps buying me instead.

Neither of us has moved the other, not really, not after months of this same conversation resurfacing at different dinners. I don't think she's wrong that the world runs on transcripts and test scores more than it runs on kindness people don't put on a resume. I don't think I'm wrong that spending my afternoons that way has changed something in me I can feel but can't fully explain to her — a patience, maybe, or a willingness to sit in not-understanding long enough to eventually understand.

Tomas signed something to me a few weeks ago, slow enough that I actually caught the whole sentence without asking him to repeat it, something small about a joke from class, and I laughed before I'd even consciously translated it in my head, my hands had gotten there faster than my thinking had. I went home and told my grandmother about it, hoping the story might finally land differently. She smiled, and said that's wonderful, mija, and then asked, in the same breath, how the practice test had gone that week.

I don't think we'll ever fully agree on this. I've mostly stopped trying to make her agree. I just keep signing anyway, and she keeps buying practice books, and somehow we still sit at the same table every night without either of us conceding an inch.
