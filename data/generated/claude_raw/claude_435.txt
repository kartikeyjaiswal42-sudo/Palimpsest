The plan was a strip-built cedar canoe, sixteen feet, the kind of project my uncle had done once in the nineties and still talked about like it was the defining achievement of his life. I decided in April I would build one before the summer ended, working off a set of plans I bought online and a stack of cedar strips I ripped myself on a table saw I barely knew how to use safely.

By July I had given up on it, mentally, even though the hull still sat half-formed on sawhorses in the garage. The strips kept splitting where I stapled them to the forms. The bead-and-cove edges, which are supposed to lock each strip against its neighbor, never quite fit right, leaving gaps I had to fill with wood filler that never matched the color. I'd planned to be on the water by the Fourth of July. By the Fourth of July I had a lopsided wooden shell with more clamps in it than actual finished hull, and I told my dad I was done, that I'd wasted a summer and two hundred dollars of cedar on something I clearly didn't have the skill to finish.

What changed my mind wasn't a pep talk. It was going back into the garage two days later to clear it out, meaning to strip the forms and throw the wood in the scrap pile, and actually looking at the hull for the first time without the pressure of a deadline attached to it. Up close, most of the gaps weren't as bad as I'd told myself they were. A few strips near the bow were genuinely wrong, split clean through, but the majority of the boat was fine — better than fine, in places, where the grain lined up in a way I hadn't even noticed while rushing.

I pulled the bad strips, re-cut new ones, and stopped trying to hit the Fourth of July or any date at all. It took until late August. The canoe I eventually paddled out onto Miller's Pond was not the flawless one I'd pictured in April. It has a visible seam near the stern where two strips didn't perfectly match, and a slightly asymmetric curve at the bow that I could see from twenty feet away and that nobody else has ever noticed.

I nearly let a deadline convince me that imperfect work wasn't worth finishing. What actually saved the canoe was giving up the version of the project that existed only in my head and going back to look, honestly, at the one taking shape in front of me. I still think about that gap between the plan and the actual wood every time I start something that isn't going the way I pictured it.
