The belief I challenged that summer was that understanding comes before doing, and the thing that dismantled it was a habit so small I did not know I had it until my grandfather pointed it out.

The motorcycle was a Bajaj Chetak, technically a scooter, which my grandfather bought new in 1989 and stopped riding in 2016 when his hip went. It sat under a sheet behind the house. I asked for it the summer I was sixteen because I wanted something to do that was not a screen, and he said yes on the condition that I did not sell it, which had not occurred to me and which I found slightly insulting at the time.

I approached it the way school had taught me to approach anything. I read first. I read a service manual PDF cover to cover before I touched a bolt — one hundred and forty pages, two evenings, and I took notes. I could have told you the ignition timing spec and the correct plug gap and the torque figure for the flywheel nut. I understood, in the way you understand a chapter.

Then I opened it up and I did not know what I was looking at. Nothing in the photographs was oriented the way the object was oriented. The manual said remove the flywheel and there was no way to remove the flywheel because it requires a puller, which the manual assumes you have, and which nobody in my house had ever heard of.

The habit started around the second week. When I got stuck, I would put my hands on the part and just move it. Turn it, wiggle it, follow the thing it was attached to with my fingers to where it went. Ten minutes at a time, no thinking, no phone. I did it because I was frustrated and it was something to do with my hands instead of giving up.

Almost every problem I solved that summer, I solved during those ten minutes.

Not because touching is magic. Because my hands would find the thing the manual had not mentioned — that this cable runs behind that bracket, that the cover will not come off until you loosen a bolt on the other side, that the reason the throttle stuck was a rubber grommet that had gone hard, which no page anywhere describes because it is not a fault, it is just what happens to rubber in Delhi after twenty-nine years.

My grandfather came out one afternoon in July and watched me for a while and said, you have started listening to it. I said I was just fiddling. He said that is what listening looks like.

So the belief. I had genuinely thought that competence was downstream of comprehension — that you read, you understand, and then you can do. What I actually found is that with a physical object the arrow goes the other way most of the time. You do, badly, with your hands, and the understanding assembles itself afterwards out of what your hands found out. The manual made sense to me in September in a way it had not in June, and nothing about the manual had changed.

The outcome, in the small sense, is that the Chetak runs. It runs poorly. It smokes and the horn does not work and it will not start below about eighteen degrees.

The larger outcome is that I have stopped waiting to understand things before beginning them, which has made me worse at some subjects and much better at others. In physics I now do the problem wrong first on purpose, quickly, before reading the section. My teacher thinks this is a strange method. It came off the back of a house in July with a grommet in my hand.
