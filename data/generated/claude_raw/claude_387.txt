My grandfather had hip surgery in May, which meant I ran the orchard alone that summer, forty-one peach trees on six acres he's kept alive since before my mother was born, mostly because a physical therapist told him flatly that climbing a ladder before August was not an option he was allowed to negotiate.

He tried to run it from a lawn chair anyway, which is its own story, but the argument I keep coming back to was about thinning. Peach trees set far more fruit than they can actually ripen well, so sometime in early June you're supposed to go through and pull off a good portion of the small green peaches, leaving space for the ones that remain to grow properly. My grandfather does this by feel, spacing by eye, a method he's used for probably fifty years and trusts completely. I wanted to thin by a formula I'd found from the extension office online, one peach every six to eight inches along each branch, measured with a ruler I brought out to the orchard in my back pocket like it was a piece of scientific equipment.

He told me not to use the ruler. He said, from his lawn chair, in the specific tone he uses when he thinks I'm about to learn something the expensive way, that a formula doesn't know which branch is weak and which is strong, doesn't know which side of the tree gets afternoon shade, doesn't know anything about that particular tree that fifty years of standing under it teaches you. I respected him more than almost anyone, and I did it my way anyway, because I liked the idea of a method I could defend with a number instead of a feeling, and because some small part of me wanted to prove that his knowledge, real as it was, could be replaced by something more systematic.

I thinned about half the orchard with the ruler before he made me stop, not because he'd won the argument but because thinning season was running out and we needed the rest of the trees done regardless of method. The half I did his way, eyeballed, spaced by feel and years of instinct I don't have, came in beautifully in August — full peaches, good size, the branches holding without snapping under the weight. The half I did with the ruler came in noticeably worse. I'd thinned some weak branches too lightly because the math said the spacing was already correct, and they buckled under fruit they couldn't actually support, several branches splitting clean off two trees by the third week of July.

I never fully admitted to him that the formula had failed. I told him some of the ruler trees "struggled a little," which he accepted without pushing, though I think he knew exactly what I meant and was choosing not to make me say it plainly, which somehow felt worse than if he had.

I still think about those broken branches more than I think about the good harvest from the trees I thinned his way. Not because I lost the argument — I did, clearly, the orchard settled it for us — but because I understand now that I wasn't actually disagreeing with him about thinning technique. I was disagreeing with the idea that some kinds of knowledge can't be written down as a number, that being right requires the burden of proof I was used to from a classroom. He was right, and he was right in a way that no ruler could have captured, and it took two split branches and a summer of picking up his slack to make me actually believe it instead of just admitting it out loud.
