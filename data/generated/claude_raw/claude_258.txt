Three years ago, I started collecting rainfall data. I have a rain gauge that I installed in our backyard, and every morning I write down the measurement from the previous day. It was supposed to be a project, but it became something else entirely, which is where the argument with my parents comes in.

The project started in ninth grade for science class. I was supposed to do a weather experiment, and I thought rainfall data over time would be interesting to track. I set up a simple rain gauge—a plastic tube marked with measurements—and started recording. At first, it was just data. Rain fell, I measured it, I wrote it down. The science teacher gave me an A on the project and that should have been the end of it.

But I kept measuring.

I made charts. I tracked monthly patterns, annual patterns, driest days and wettest days. I started comparing my data to the official weather station data and noticed small discrepancies, which led me down a research rabbit hole about how measurement methods affect accuracy. I built a better gauge. I started collecting data from three different locations in our neighborhood to track how rainfall varies geographically. It became this obsessive thing that nobody else in my family understood.

My dad eventually asked me what I was doing with all of this. I tried to explain the patterns I was seeing, the connections between rainfall and temperature and humidity, the way weather systems moved through our area. He listened, and then he said, "But what is the point? Are you trying to become a meteorologist? Are you planning to use this for something?"

And I didn't have a good answer because the point was becoming harder to articulate. The point was that I found it satisfying to know things precisely, to track patterns over time, to understand the minute variations in something as simple as water falling from the sky. The point was also that I was processing my parents' divorce through the lens of meteorological data, which I didn't tell him because that sounded insane.

My mom was more concerned. She said that I spent too much time on this and not enough time on things that would "matter" later, whatever that means. She suggested I find a hobby that was more social, that involved other people, that would look good on college applications. She suggested cheerleading and model UN. She didn't understand that my rain gauge was how I was making sense of my world when everything else had become chaotic and unpredictable.

The argument escalated when my parents wanted to send me to summer camp. Three weeks away from my rain gauge, from my daily measurements, from the one thing that felt stable. I refused. I had a meltdown about it. I said they didn't understand me, that they never respected my interests, that they just wanted me to be someone I wasn't. All of which was probably true, but also, I was being irrational about an inanimate piece of plastic and some numbers.

We compromised. I didn't go to camp. I also agreed to spend less time on the data collection. But the rift was there, the understanding that my parents thought I was wasting my time and I thought they didn't see what I was trying to do.

Here's the thing I don't think I'll ever explain to them: I wasn't just collecting data about weather. I was collecting evidence that the world was measurable and knowable. I was proving to myself that even when everything felt random and chaotic, some things followed patterns. Some things could be tracked and understood.

Three years of data, and now I have a clear picture of the rainfall patterns in my area. I can predict, with reasonable accuracy, whether a given month will be wet or dry. It's not nothing. But I'm also aware that I've spent three years on something that only I care about, that won't matter to anyone else, that doesn't lead anywhere.

Maybe that's okay. Maybe there's value in caring about something just because it matters to you, even if it doesn't matter to anyone else. My parents still don't really get it. But I'm still measuring, and the rain is still falling, and the data is still there.
