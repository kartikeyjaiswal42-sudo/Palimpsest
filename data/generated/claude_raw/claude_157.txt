The route was my brother Danny's before it was mine. Forty-one houses along Birch and Somerset, delivered before 6:30 every morning, seven days a week, for the Ridgefield Courier, a paper that most of my customers still pay for by check mailed to the office because half of them don't trust anything you can't hold. Danny did it for four years and handed it to me the summer before he left for college, walking me the route once, pointing out which porches wanted the paper tucked behind the screen door and which ones wanted it thrown, underhand, onto the second step exactly.

House twenty-six is Mrs. Ostrowski's. She's eighty-something, lives alone, and Danny told me on that first walk-through that she reads the whole paper every morning with her coffee before anyone else in the family is awake, obituaries first, then the crossword, then everything else. He said take care of her specifically. I said I would.

For almost two years I did. Then, in October, my parents told my sister and me they were separating, on a Tuesday night, and I will not pretend the next week was normal for me in any way. I stopped sleeping right. I stopped doing a lot of things right. And for four mornings in a row, I did not deliver Mrs. Ostrowski's paper. I delivered everyone else's — I was on autopilot enough to finish the route, I just kept skipping house twenty-six without registering that I was doing it, walking right past like my body knew the way better than my head did that week.

I found out on the fifth day, when she called the paper's office, not to complain, just to ask if the delivery had stopped for some reason, and the office called my mom, and my mom told me in a tone I still feel bad about. I went to Mrs. Ostrowski's door that afternoon to apologize in person. I had a whole explanation ready about my parents, figuring I'd need it to excuse myself.

She didn't let me get through it. She just said she'd figured something was going on, that a route boy doesn't miss four days for no reason, and she wasn't upset, she'd just been worried about me specifically, not the paper. Then she handed me a tin of shortbread and a five-dollar bill folded in half, and said it was for hot chocolate on cold mornings, and to come knock if I ever needed to sit down for a minute on my route, any morning, rain or not.

I did not expect that. I'd built up, in my head, some version of this conversation where I got yelled at or reported or at minimum made to feel like the screwup I was pretty sure I'd been. Instead an eighty-something-year-old woman I'd failed for four straight mornings was worried about me and gave me shortbread.

I think about those four missed mornings more than I think about almost anything else from that fall, and not in a way that's fully resolved, honestly — I still feel the specific shame of walking past a door I knew mattered. But Mrs. Ostrowski's kindness did something to how I carry that memory. It didn't erase the mistake. It just turned the mistake into something I owed a debt for, rather than something I was only ashamed of, and a debt is a thing you can actually act on. I have not missed a morning since, at her house or anyone else's, and some mornings I still knock, just to sit for a minute, the way she told me I could.
