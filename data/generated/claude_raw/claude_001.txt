My grandmother kept a single wooden spoon on a nail by the stove, worn so smooth on one edge that it had gone the color of caramel. She didn't call it anything, because she didn't call most things by name in English, not to me — the only English she had was "hot," "sit," "wait," and a fast "no, no, no" when I reached for something before it was ready. I was twelve when she came to live with us, and that spoon became, without either of us planning it, the only language we actually shared.

For the first few weeks I stood in the kitchen and failed constantly. I chopped onions too thick. I let garlic burn while I checked my phone under the counter. She would take the spoon out of my hand, not unkindly, just fast, like she couldn't stand to watch a mistake finish happening, fix what I had done, and hand it back. I wanted her to explain, in words, what I was doing wrong, and she couldn't, and one afternoon I got so tired of not understanding that I put the spoon down on the counter and walked out of the kitchen.

She didn't come after me. When I came back an hour later, ready to apologize for something I couldn't even name, she was rolling out dough at the counter, and she just handed me the spoon again, like nothing had happened. That was the moment I actually started paying attention — not to her mouth, but to her hands. The way she tilted the pan a half second before the oil began to smoke. The way she pressed two fingers into rising dough and waited for it to spring back a particular way. None of that was translatable. I had to learn it the way you learn to catch a ball, by getting hit in the face a few times first.

By spring I could tell, just from the smell coming off the pan, whether the mustard seeds were about to pop or whether I had already lost them to burning. I still didn't know most of the words for what we were doing together. But I knew the weight of the spoon in my hand, and I knew what her silence meant when I had gotten something right — she would take a spoonful, taste it without looking at me, and set the spoon back down on the counter instead of taking it out of my hand. That was the whole compliment. It took me months to learn to hear it.

What I actually failed at that year wasn't cooking. It was my assumption that understanding required language, that if we couldn't talk to each other we couldn't really know each other. I was wrong about that in a way that still embarrasses me a little. She taught me an entire craft, and something about patience I don't think I could have learned any other way, using maybe forty words of English and one wooden spoon, and I came out the other side able to make her dal exactly the way she made it, and able to sit in a room with someone and not feel the need to fill every silence with talking.

She died the winter before I started writing this application, and the spoon is in a drawer in our kitchen now. I still use it sometimes, not because I need to, but because my hand remembers what to do with it before I do, and that, it turns out, was the whole point.
