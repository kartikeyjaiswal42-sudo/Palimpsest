The canvas bag is olive green and says HERALD-EXAMINER on the side in letters that have gone the color of weak tea. My brother Danny carried it for three years before I did. There is a stain near the bottom seam shaped like Delaware that neither of us has ever identified. When he handed it over the summer before eighth grade, he gave me the rules of the route as if they were laws of physics: the Petrosyans get theirs on the porch, 214 gets it in the mailbox, and you never bother the man at 331. Danny said the man at 331 was mean. He said it flatly, the way you'd say a street was one-way.

So for two years I threw the paper from the sidewalk at 331, into the general vicinity of the yard, and then I pedaled hard. I believed the man was mean because Danny believed it, and Danny believed it because a kid named Aaron told him, and Aaron got it from whoever came before him. The route had been handed down for a decade, and the belief came with it, folded into the bag like the stain.

The winter I turned fifteen it snowed badly enough that the paper landed in a drift and vanished. I got off my bike to dig for it. While I was on my knees in his yard, the man came out. He was maybe eighty, in a bathrobe over a sweater, and he was holding a snow shovel, which for one second I read as a weapon. He handed it to me. He said the drift went deeper than it looked and I should scoop, not paw. Then he went inside and came back with the paper he had already found, and a cup of instant coffee that I did not want and drank anyway.

His name was Mr. Kolar. He had lived there fifty-one years. He was not mean. He was deaf in his left ear and slow to the door, and at some point a kid on a bike had knocked, gotten no answer, decided he was ignoring them, and passed that story down the line like the bag.

What bothers me still is how little it took to keep believing it. I never tested the claim. I had passed his house six hundred times and never once knocked twice. The belief was cheap to hold and would have been cheap to check, and I held it because checking would have meant contradicting my brother, who was seventeen and knew things.

I finished the route in June and gave the bag to a kid named Marcus down the block. I told him the Petrosyans want it on the porch and 214 wants the mailbox. Then I told him Mr. Kolar is deaf in one ear, so knock twice, and hand it to him if you can. I don't know if Marcus does it. But the thing I passed down was true, and I had gone and found out.
