We hosted an exchange student for a year and I was bad at it. That is the honest version. Her name was Lise, she was from a town outside Antwerp, and she arrived in August of my sophomore year, and for roughly five months I failed at the one thing my family had actually asked of me, which was to be her friend.

I had said yes to the whole idea enthusiastically. I told people about it for weeks beforehand. I imagined a sister, essentially — someone to sit on the end of my bed at night and tell me things. What I got was a very quiet seventeen-year-old who went to her room after dinner and closed the door, and who answered most questions with a single word, and who I decided, somewhere around week three, did not like me.

So I stopped trying, which is the part I am not proud of. Not dramatically. I did not do anything you could point at. I just started leaving earlier in the mornings so we would not walk to the bus together. I stopped inviting her when my friends did things, and I had a reason ready every time, and the reason was always that she probably wouldn't want to come. I let my mother handle her. I remember my mother asking me in November whether Lise seemed lonely, and I said she seemed fine, and I believed it enough that saying it did not feel like a lie.

The thing that changed my mind happened in January and it was not a conversation. It was a photograph.

Lise had a small printer, one of those instant ones, and she kept the pictures in a shoebox under her desk. I was looking for a phone charger and I found the box and I opened it, which I should not have done. Most of the photos were of our house. The kitchen. Our dog on the stairs. My father asleep in the chair with the newspaper on his chest. The back garden in three different seasons. And there were maybe fifteen of me — me at the counter, me in the driveway, me laughing at something with my back half-turned, all of them taken from a distance, none of them ones I had known were being taken.

She had been documenting us like someone who was going to lose all of it, which of course she was. And she had been watching me the whole time I had been deciding she didn't like me.

I sat on the floor of her room with the box and I understood, in a way I had not let myself understand before, that quiet is not the same as uninterested, and that a person alone in a country where she has to translate every joke before she can laugh at it might go to her room after dinner because she is tired, not because she is cold. I had read her silence as a verdict on me. It was never about me at all.

What I did next was small and slow, because there was no big gesture available and it would have been for my benefit anyway. I started walking to the bus with her again. I asked her about her actual life, the one in Belgium, the friends and the boy and the mother she was fighting with over the phone every second Sunday — and she talked, a lot, once anyone asked. I stopped translating her flat expression into a rejection. In March we drove four hours to see snow because she said she had never seen the kind that stays.

She went home in June. I did not fix five months in four; that is not how it works, and I do not want to pretend it is. What I got instead was a permanent suspicion of my own read on people. I now assume, as a default, that when I have decided someone doesn't like me, I have probably assembled that out of very thin material and then acted in a way that made it true.

We still write. She sent me a photo last month of her kitchen in Antwerp, taken from the doorway, no people in it.
