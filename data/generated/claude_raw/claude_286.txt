I was holding the camera up to the bright afternoon light and I still couldn't tell if I had the exposure right. There was no light meter. The dial on top of the camera told me the shutter speed and the aperture but nothing about whether enough light was reaching the film. I had to guess. I had been guessing for weeks.

My father gave me the camera after I showed interest in photography. It was an old Pentax from the eighties, film, no automatic settings. He said it would teach me to actually think about light instead of just letting the camera figure it out for me. I thought he was being difficult. He was being brilliant, but I didn't realize it yet.

The first roll of film I shot was mostly failure. Pictures too dark or too bright or somewhere in between and I had no way of knowing until I developed them. I took notes on every exposure. The time of day, the lighting conditions, the settings I had chosen. Then I would get the prints back and I would see which of my guesses had been right. It was the slowest possible feedback loop.

What I did was look at the sun and try to estimate how much light was bouncing off my subject. I learned about the Sunny Sixteen rule—f/16 on a sunny day with ISO 100 film. I started to understand that exposure is about the relationship between light and sensitivity and the time the film is exposed. It's mathematics and it's also art. It's physics and it's also intuition.

By the second roll, I was getting better. By the fourth roll, I was predicting correctly most of the time. And then something shifted. Instead of thinking about exposure as a problem to solve, I started seeing light as the thing itself. I started noticing how light changed throughout the day. How it bent around objects. How it created shape and shadow and dimension. The camera became a tool for capturing what I was actually seeing.

The Pentax is sitting on my desk right now. It's scratched and worn. I've used it for maybe five years now and I've gone through dozens of rolls of film. I have a digital camera too. Most of the time I use that. It's faster and it lets me see the results immediately. But I still pick up the Pentax sometimes. Not for results—the digital camera gets better pictures. But for the discipline of it. For the reminder of having to think before I shoot.

Photography changed for me because of that camera without a light meter. I learned to see light instead of just recording it. I learned that limitations can be a gift. I learned that the slowest feedback loop sometimes teaches you the most.

Now when I use my digital camera, I think about what I learned on film. I'm more intentional. I pay attention to the actual light instead of trusting the camera to handle it. The absence of a light meter taught me to develop my own sense of light. In a way, it gave me something that automatic cameras never could.
