I'd been at Lincoln High for exactly six weeks when I realized that this new school smelled the same as the one before it. Industrial cleaner, cafeteria grease, and the specific adolescent musk of seventeen hundred teenagers crowded into one building. The familiarity was depressing.

I'd moved between schools the way other kids moved between classes. My mother had always been looking for something better—better neighborhoods, better schools, better job opportunities. Every two or three years, we'd pack up and start over. By the time I was seventeen, I'd attended six different schools. I'd learned how to be the new student. I could navigate it efficiently. But I'd also learned to not bother.

My previous school had been my third or fourth—I'd stopped counting exactly. I had the skills. First week, find the quiet spaces. Don't push too hard on friendships because you'll be leaving. Learn where things are. Keep your head down. Get good grades because that's something consistent you can control. By week six at Lincoln, I'd already figured out that the cafeteria had a decent quiet corner in the back where I could eat alone, that the library had chairs by the windows where I could hide between classes, that Mrs. Hammond in the English department was one of the few teachers who actually noticed when I wasn't there and seemed to care.

She asked me to stay after class one day, about a month in. I assumed I was in trouble. Instead, she asked me if I wanted to help her with the school's literary magazine. I said no. Not in a rude way, just automatically. Why would I commit to something here? I'd be gone before the magazine came out. I'd learned that lesson at the school before this one.

Mrs. Hammond didn't accept that. She told me, very bluntly, that I was the most talented writer in her senior class and that it would be a shame if I didn't do anything with that. She said the magazine would be published in March. She asked if I could commit to staying until March. Just March. She wasn't asking for forever.

I told her I couldn't make any promises about where I'd be living. She said that wasn't what she was asking. She was asking if I could commit to March as if I'd be here for March. That's different, she said. That's just committing to now, not to the future.

Something about that landed differently. Maybe because Mrs. Hammond was the first teacher at Lincoln who seemed to see me instead of seeing the new kid. Maybe because she was asking me to invest in something small instead of something permanent. Maybe because, for the first time, I realized that I'd been using the fact of my impermanence as a shield. As long as I didn't commit, I couldn't lose anything. But I was also not gaining anything.

I helped with the literary magazine. I submitted three of my own pieces and also took on the role of senior editor. I went to meetings. I had conversations with other students about writing and about their work. I started to know people not just as classmates but as writers with specific things they were trying to say.

In February, my mother mentioned that a new job opportunity had come up and we might need to move after graduation. She was giving me time to prepare, to understand that this school, like all the others, was temporary. She looked guilty about it, the way she always did when she broke this news.

But something was different this time. I felt disappointed, which was new. I wanted to stay until the magazine came out. I wanted to see it finished. I wanted to see how the contributions came together, how the final product would look with all of our work in it.

That's when I understood that I'd been afraid, not practical. I'd been protecting myself from loss by refusing to commit. But refusing to commit meant refusing to live anywhere, really. I'd been moving my whole high school career and not actually being anywhere at all.
