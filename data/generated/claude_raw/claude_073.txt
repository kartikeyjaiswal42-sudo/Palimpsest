My mother thinks the garden was a waste of a summer that could have gone into an internship, and she still says so, and I still disagree with her, and neither of us has managed to actually resolve it even now, a year later.

It started because of the drought. The county issued watering restrictions in June, two days a week only, early morning or late evening, and my mother, who has never once in my life shown interest in the backyard beyond complaining about the weeds in it, suddenly decided the whole thing should just be let go, ripped out and replaced with gravel, since keeping it alive under the restrictions seemed to her like effort spent on something that was going to die anyway. I disagreed, loudly, mostly because my grandmother had planted half of what was out there before she passed, and something about watching it brown and curl under a hundred-and-five-degree sky felt like losing her twice.

So I took it over. I set an alarm for five-thirty every morning, before the heat, and hand-watered instead of running the sprinklers, moving a hose section by section so nothing got wasted on gravel or sidewalk, mulching everything I could to hold moisture in a few extra hours. I lost the tomatoes almost immediately, no saving those, and I lost the roses along the fence line by mid-July no matter what I tried. But the fig tree survived. The rosemary, absurdly, thrived, like the drought had nothing to do with it at all. The lemon tree lost most of its leaves and then, somehow, in August, pushed out new growth anyway, small and pale green against all the brown around it.

My mother watched all of this from the kitchen window most mornings, coffee in hand, and told me, more than once, that I was pouring effort into something that would look exactly the same either way come fall — dead branches whether I watered them or not, she said, was still just dead branches. I told her that wasn't true, that the fig tree was proof it wasn't true, and she said one surviving tree wasn't the same as a garden, and I said it was closer to a garden than gravel would ever be, and we've had some version of that same argument probably a dozen times since, none of them resolved, both of us equally sure we're right.

I don't think she's a villain in this, for what it's worth. I think she watched her own mother work in that garden for twenty years and doesn't want to watch it die slowly under my hands too, and ripping it out fast, on her terms, was maybe its own way of trying to protect herself from that. I've never quite said that back to her, that I think I understand where her position comes from even while I still think she's wrong about what to do with it.

The garden's still there. Smaller than it used to be, patchier, but alive — the fig tree especially, heavy with fruit again this year, more than it's produced in a decade, according to my mother herself, who mentioned it almost begrudgingly one evening in August while eating one straight off the branch. She hasn't said I was right. I don't think she's going to. But she ate the fig, and didn't mention gravel again that whole week, and for now, that's about as close to an ending as this particular disagreement is going to get.
