Room 118 has a busted thermostat. The dial goes past 80 before it clicks over to heat, so in October it's either freezing or a sauna, no in between, and for the first six weeks of chess club — my chess club, the one I begged the front office to let me start — it didn't matter, because nobody was in there to complain about the temperature except me and Mr. Boyle.

Mr. Boyle cleans that wing of the building. I knew him the way you know a custodian, which is to say I said hi and he said hi back and that was basically it, for two years. Then I started this club nobody wanted, Tuesdays and Thursdays at lunch, and it turned out Mr. Boyle unlocked room 118 every single one of those mornings before I got there. Not because anyone told him to. He wasn't even supposed to be on that hallway before ten.

I found this out by accident, on a Thursday when I showed up ten minutes early to fix something with the folding table, and the door was already open, lights on, thermostat cranked so the room wouldn't be an icebox for whatever eleven people I was still, at that point, imagining might one day appear. He was in the hallway with his cart. I asked if he'd unlocked it. He said something like, yeah, figured you'd want it warm, and kept moving.

He kept doing that. Every single meeting, all fall, whether one kid showed up or zero. I didn't ask him to. I don't think he ever mentioned it to anyone else, and honestly I might never have said anything about it either, except one day in December I caught him setting up two folding chairs across from each other at the little table by the window — a table nobody used, off to the side — and I asked why, and he told me, kind of sideways, not really looking at me, that his younger brother used to beat him at chess when they were kids and he hadn't played since his brother died, years back, and something about the room made him think of it.

I didn't know what to say to that. I still don't, really. I just said thanks, and he shrugged like it was nothing, and went back to his cart.

Here's the thing about gratitude nobody tells you: it doesn't always feel like a big warm feeling in your chest. Sometimes it just quietly rearranges what you think you're doing. I'd started that club because I liked chess and figured other people might too, eventually, if I was patient enough. After I found out about Mr. Boyle, I started showing up fifteen minutes early myself, and I started leaving the door propped even when it was just me sitting there, because it turns out an open door is a whole different message than a locked one, and I'd never once thought about that until somebody did it for me for six straight weeks without saying a word about it.

We have fourteen members now. Mr. Boyle still unlocks the room before I get there most days, out of habit I think, even though I have my own key. I never taught him to play. He's never asked, and I've never pushed it. But sometimes I set up that little table by the window anyway, two chairs, nobody sitting in them, and I just leave it like that until I lock up for the day.

None of this shows up on a transcript. There's no line for it. But if you asked me what actually taught me to keep something going when it looks like it's failing, it wasn't a coach or a book, it was a man with a cart who decided, quietly and for no credit at all, that a cold empty room deserved to be warm anyway.
