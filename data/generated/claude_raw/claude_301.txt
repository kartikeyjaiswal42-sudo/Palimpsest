My calculator is a TI-84 Plus with a cracked corner and three faded stickers on the back, a chess pawn, a small green alien, and a sticker for a math camp I never actually attended myself. It was handed to me freshman year by a senior named Owen who was graduating and cleaning out his backpack, and he gave it to me specifically, not to anyone else on the team, which felt at the time like being knighted.

I've belonged to my school's math team for three years now, a group of maybe fourteen of us who meet in Room 214 every Tuesday and Thursday after school and most Saturday mornings during competition season, working through old olympiad problems on a whiteboard that never fully erases clean. It's a strange kind of community, built almost entirely around silence, actually, twelve or fourteen people bent over the same problem for twenty minutes without anyone talking, and then someone finally says "wait, what if" and the whole room turns toward the whiteboard at once.

I trained for the state olympiad qualifier for eight months of my junior year, using that calculator through every single practice set, its buttons worn smooth in the spots I press most. I wasn't naturally the fastest solver on the team — that was Deja, who could see a combinatorics trick in four seconds that took me twenty minutes to find on a good day — but I was consistent, and consistency was supposed to be enough to qualify for the next round. It wasn't. I missed the cutoff by three points, choking on a number theory problem I'd solved correctly in practice a dozen times before, freezing on the actual test in a way I still can't fully explain, my hand just refusing to write down an approach I knew was right.

I sat in the parking lot afterward for twenty minutes not able to go back inside where everyone was comparing scores, holding that calculator like it had personally let me down, which is an unfair thing to think about a piece of plastic and silicon that had never done anything except exactly what I told it to.

Here's what I've come to understand about my actual place on that team, though, which took the failure to see clearly. I'm not the one who's going to qualify for nationals. I've made peace with that in a way that took longer than I'd like to admit. What I am is the person who keeps the shared problem log going, who remembers which approach we tried on a given problem two months ago and can pull it back up when someone gets stuck in the same place again, who notices when a freshman hasn't said a word in three meetings and asks them, directly, what they're working on. Deja solves the problems nobody else can. I keep the room functioning as a place where solving them is possible at all, which is a smaller kind of contribution and, I've decided, not a lesser one.

Owen's calculator sits in my backpack pocket now, and this spring I'm planning to hand it to a sophomore named Iris the same way it was handed to me, cracked corner, faded stickers, and all, minus whatever new scuff marks I've added to it these three years. I never did qualify for nationals. But I've sat in that room three years running, missing points on the same problems everyone misses points on, and somehow ended up as one of the people the newer kids look for when they're stuck, which is not the thing I set out to become there, but it's the thing that stuck.
