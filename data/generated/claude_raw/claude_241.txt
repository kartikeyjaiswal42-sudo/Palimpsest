I still have issue fourteen. Not a photo of it, not some PDF somebody archived — the actual paper, newsprint gone soft at the folds, a coffee ring on the front page that isn't mine because I never got to finish my coffee that morning. It lives in the bottom drawer of my desk, under a calculator I broke sophomore year and still haven't thrown out for no good reason.

Here's what happened. Junior year I found out the district had quietly cut our school's counselor position from full-time to two days a week, right around the same month they approved forty thousand dollars for new turf on the football field. Nobody announced the counselor cut. I only knew because a friend went to make an appointment and got told the office was closed until Thursday. I wrote it up for the paper using numbers from a budget document a front-office secretary let slip after I'd asked one too many annoying questions in one too many visits. We ran it on a Tuesday.

Admin was not thrilled. That undersells it, honestly — the principal called our advisor, Mr. Danziger, into his office and wanted to know exactly who'd handed a seventeen-year-old district financial records. I was sure, walking past that office and seeing the door shut, that he'd give my name up. Why wouldn't he? He had a job. I had a byline and a stapler.

He didn't. I found out weeks later, from another student who'd overheard part of it through a wall that is apparently not as soundproof as the district thinks, that Mr. Danziger told the principal flatly that a paper doesn't function if sources aren't protected, and that if someone needed to be disciplined it should be him, not a kid doing exactly what journalism is supposed to do. He never told me this himself. He just kept running the paper the same way he always had, like nothing had happened, like he hadn't put his own job on a table for a story about counseling hours.

I found out the whole thing had almost gotten him formally reprimanded when I overheard my own mom on the phone with his wife — small town, everyone's moms know each other — and by then it was old news to everybody but me. I never even got to thank him properly. I tried once, after class, and he just said, "That's the job," and started erasing the whiteboard like the conversation was already finished.

That newspaper sat in the recycling bin outside the front office for exactly one afternoon before I fished it back out, cover a little damp, and I don't totally know why I kept it instead of just archiving the file like everyone else does now. I think I wanted something I could actually hold that proved a person had decided, on a random Tuesday, that protecting a seventeen-year-old was worth more to him than staying comfortable.
