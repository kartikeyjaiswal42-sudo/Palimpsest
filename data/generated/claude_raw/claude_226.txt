The kitchen is already humid from her being in there for two hours. She stands at the counter, wearing the same apron she's worn for decades, the fabric thin in places and stained with curry turmeric that won't come out. She doesn't use a recipe. She never has. She cooks by feel, by memory, by something that lives in her hands.

I'm standing beside her taking notes. I have a notebook and a pen, and I'm trying to quantify something that was never quantified in the first place. She laughs at me, not unkindly. She knows I think there's a problem here, that something this important shouldn't live only in her head.

"How much garlic?" I ask.

She looks at what's already in the pot. "That much," she says, gesturing vaguely. I write down "approximately three cloves, minced, to taste" and I know I'm already being too precise. She's adding something else now, something she's pulled from the back of the cabinet that I've never noticed before. It's not labeled. The bottle is old, greenish glass, and I have no idea what's inside.

"What is that?" I ask.

"Your grandmother used it," she says. "From her mother's time. She grew it."

I write: "Family spice, origin unknown, from great-grandmother's garden, amount unknown."

This is going to be impossible to document. I can feel that now. I thought if I could just follow along carefully enough, take detailed enough notes, I could preserve this recipe. I could write it down in a way that made sense, that could be followed, that could be passed on. I thought the recipe was something separate from the person making it, that it was a discrete set of instructions I could extract.

But watching her work, I'm realizing that the recipe isn't actually the recipe. The recipe is her knowledge of how her mother made it, and her mother's knowledge of how her mother made it, and all the small adjustments she's made over her lifetime. The recipe is her hands knowing when the spices are right, her nose knowing when the ghee is the right temperature, her instinct knowing how much time and attention this particular pot of food needs today.

She tastes it with a small wooden spoon. Her face doesn't change. She adds a pinch of salt. She lets it simmer and checks on it in ways I can't predict. She knows something about food that I'm not sure can be written down.

"Come," she says, and she hands me a spoon. I taste what she's made and I understand that this is better than anything I could make from notes. It's the thing itself, completed.

I close my notebook. I pull up a chair and sit at the counter. She starts the next dish and I start taking notes again, but differently this time. Not trying to capture the recipe, but trying to see what she's seeing, to learn by proximity and repetition rather than by instruction. And she smiles, really smiles, in the way she always does when I stop fighting against how she works.

The food will be delicious. I'll eat it and it will taste like home and like her, and that knowledge will sit in my hands and my memory the way it sits in hers. That's all I need to know. That's everything, actually.
