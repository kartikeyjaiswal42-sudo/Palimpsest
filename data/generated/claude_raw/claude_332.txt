The roof of our block is eleven flights up and you get to it through a fire door that is supposed to be alarmed and is not. There are two hives up there, a National and a smaller nuc box, on breeze blocks I carried up myself over three trips, and between April and September that is where I am most Saturday mornings before anyone else in the building is awake. It is where I feel most like myself. It is also where I did the worst thing I have ever done, which was not deliberate and which killed about thirty thousand animals.

I should explain the roof first. It is asphalt and it slopes slightly towards a drain, there is a satellite dish nobody has used since before I was born, and if you stand at the north end you can see the gasholder and, on a clear day, the hills past Dinnington. But the reason it matters is not the view. It is that when you open a hive, everything else stops. You cannot be distracted while your hands are inside a colony. You cannot check your phone. You cannot think about the chemistry mock, because there are between forty and sixty thousand insects in there and your entire attention is being asked for at once, and there is a specific quality to that attention that I have never found anywhere else. The sound changes when you have done something they dislike. It goes from a hum to something harder, and you feel it in your sternum before you consciously hear it. Standing in that sound, moving very slowly, being wholly in one place — that is the closest I get to being a person rather than a list of deadlines.

Now the thing I got wrong.

Varroa is a mite. It is about the size of a pinhead, it attaches to the bee and feeds on it, and it carries viruses; if you do not manage it, a colony that looks strong in August is dead by February. Every book says the same thing. Monitor, treat, monitor again. In my first full year I did a sugar roll count in August, got a low number, and decided I was fine.

The mistake was not skipping the treatment out of laziness. It is worse than that. I wanted the count to be low. I had told a boy at school that I kept bees, and I had told my aunt, and there was a version of myself I was enjoying being — the kid on the roof, the one who does something unusual — and a mite problem did not fit in it. So I did one sample, from one frame, at one moment, which any beekeeper will tell you is not a count, it is an anecdote. And I wrote a low number in my book and felt relieved and did not test again.

I opened them in March. There is a particular smell to a colony that has died out and I do not want to describe it. The cluster was the size of a fist. There were bees head-down in cells where they had crawled in looking for the last of the stores. There were deformed wings, which is the virus, which is the mite, which is the thing I had decided not to look for.

I cried on a roof at seven in the morning, which is a ridiculous image and was not funny at the time.

I keep bees differently now. I count from three frames. I count when I do not want to. I have a laminated card in the box with the dates on it because I have learned that I cannot trust the version of me that is holding a smoker and feeling good about himself. And I have stopped telling people about it at school, mostly, which I think is related.

What the roof gives me now is not the identity. It is the check. Eleven flights up, hands in a hive, being told the truth by a colony that has no interest in whether I am impressive. I am most myself there because it is the one place where wanting something to be true does not make the slightest difference to whether it is.
