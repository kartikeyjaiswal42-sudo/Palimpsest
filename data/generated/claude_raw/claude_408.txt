I missed most of Year 10 with an autoimmune thing that took eight months to get a name and another four to get medication that worked. For the middle of that year I did school at the kitchen table when I could sit up, which was some days and not others, and the belief I ended up challenging is my mother's, and we have still not settled it.

Her position is simple and she has never once wavered from it. She believes the year was stolen. That is the word she uses. Stolen. She believes I lost twelve months of my life and that everything since has been catching up, and she says it with a kind of grief that is entirely for me and that I find very hard to be in the room with.

I understand where it comes from. She sat in the car outside three different hospitals. She fought the school twice about the work packs. She watched me at fifteen unable to climb our stairs. If I were her I would probably call it theft too.

But I do not think it is true, and the reason I do not is specific.

In February of that year I could not concentrate for more than about twenty minutes, so I stopped trying to do the biology packs properly and started reading the same textbook chapter over and over at whatever speed my head allowed. Twenty minutes, sleep, twenty minutes. What I found was that on the fourth or fifth pass the chapter had stopped being a list of facts and had turned into a shape. I could see why the sections were in that order. I had never once experienced that in a classroom, where the pace is set by a timetable and understanding is something you are supposed to arrive at on schedule or quietly not arrive at.

That year is where I learned that I am slow and that slow is not the same as unable. Every good piece of work I have done since — the extended essay, the thing I built for the physics competition — came out of a method I only have because I was ill. I do not think you can call something stolen when it is the source of the only intellectual habit I actually trust.

I said this to my mother in the summer, badly. It came out sounding like I was glad I had been sick, which is not what I meant and which hurt her, and she said that I was doing the thing people do where you decide afterwards that the worst thing was secretly good so you do not have to be angry about it. And I do not have an answer to that. It might be exactly what I am doing. I cannot get outside my own head far enough to check.

So it sits there. She still says stolen. I still flinch. We have agreed, without agreeing, to have the conversation about once a year and get no further.

What changed for me is not that I won. It is that I stopped believing that a hard experience has one correct meaning, and that the person who suffered it gets to decide. My mother suffered that year too, differently, and her reading of it is not a mistake I can correct. It is just the view from where she was standing, which was outside a hospital, in a car.
