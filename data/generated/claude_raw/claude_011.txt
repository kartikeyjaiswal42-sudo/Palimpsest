On my desk, next to a stack of unread textbooks, sits a screw. It's titanium, an inch and a half long, threaded like something you'd find in a hardware store, and it used to be inside my right shoulder. My surgeon gave it to me in a small specimen cup after he took it out, half as a joke, half because he could tell I wanted proof that something had actually happened to me.

I swam competitively for nine years before that screw went in. Butterfly and free, mostly, four hours a day between two pools, the kind of schedule that eats your whole adolescence and you let it because you love it. I tore my labrum during a 200 fly at a meet I'd been pointing toward for a year, felt it go on the third turn, and finished the race anyway because finishing was the only thing I knew how to do with my body in the water. I found out later that finishing made the tear worse. Two surgeries and eleven months of rehab later, my doctor told me, gently, that the shoulder I had now was not built for competitive butterfly anymore. He said it the way you'd tell someone bad news about a person, not a joint.

I kept the screw because I didn't know what else to do with the fact that swimming was over. For a while it sat in a drawer and I didn't look at it. Grief, it turns out, is not a metaphor when it's about your own body; it is very literal, and it takes a long time to stop being furious at a piece of titanium for outlasting your career.

What I'd bring to your campus is not a fast time, because I don't have one of those anymore. It's what happened after the screw: I started helping coach the eight-and-unders at my old club because I couldn't stand to leave the pool deck entirely, and I discovered I was good at the part of swimming nobody puts on a résumé — sitting with a nine-year-old who just false-started and feels like her whole life is over, and telling her, truthfully, that it isn't. I learned to read a stroke for flaws instead of just times. I learned how to be useful to a team from outside the water, which is a skill almost nobody wants until they suddenly need it.

I think every campus has people whose plans got cut short by something out of their control — an injury, a diagnosis, a family situation, a body that stopped cooperating — and most of them are quietly figuring out, the way I did, how to stay attached to the thing they loved without the identity they'd built around doing it perfectly. I want to be the person who notices that happening to someone else and knows what to say, because I've been on the other side of it and nobody said much of anything to me. I'd bring that noticing. I'd bring a strange souvenir in a cup and the story attached to it, and the patience that only comes from losing something and having to build a different relationship to it, on purpose, slowly, on land.
