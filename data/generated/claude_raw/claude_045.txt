I told Mrs. Okafor I was quitting piano on a Thursday in October, eleven years and two months after my first lesson with her, standing in her front hallway with my shoes still on because I didn't plan on staying long enough to need to take them off. I'd just come off a regional competition where I'd frozen halfway through the second movement of a Chopin piece I'd played probably four hundred times in practice, gone completely blank in front of six judges and a room of parents, and walked offstage feeling like eleven years of Tuesday afternoons had just proven themselves worthless in about ninety seconds.

I expected her to argue with me. She'd invested more time in me than any teacher I'd ever had, had rearranged her own schedule around competition seasons, had driven me to two out-of-state events herself when my parents couldn't take the days off work. I had a whole defense prepared in my head for the conversation I thought was coming.

She didn't argue. She just nodded, said okay, asked if I wanted to come in anyway for one more lesson since I was already there, since it was already paid for that month. I said fine, mostly out of guilt about the money.

That last lesson wasn't a lesson. She didn't have me touch the Chopin, didn't bring up technique or competitions or anything I'd been working toward for the past year. She went to her shelf and pulled out a piece of sheet music I'd never seen, a Satie piece, one of the Gymnopédies, and put it on the stand and said she wasn't assigning it to me, that I didn't have to learn it, that she just wanted me to hear it once before I stopped coming, because she thought I'd like it and she'd never had a reason to show it to me before. Everything we'd worked on for eleven years had been in service of something — a recital, a jury, a competition. This had no purpose attached to it at all. She played it through once herself, slow and unhurried in a way I'd genuinely never heard her play anything, and then she just closed the book and said that was it, that was the lesson, and thank you for eleven years, and she meant it, I could tell she meant it.

I went home still fairly sure I was done. But I kept thinking about the fact that she hadn't tried to keep me, hadn't made one argument for why I should stay, and instead had used her very last chance with me to give me something with absolutely no strings on it, just because she thought it was beautiful and thought I might too. That felt like a strange kind of gift, the kind where someone gives up their own interest in the outcome entirely in order to give you something real. I sat at our piano at home a few days later, found a recording of the Satie piece, and taught myself the first page just from listening and picking it out, badly, no sheet music of my own yet.

I never went back to lessons with her, not formally. But I started playing again within about three weeks, on my own terms, pieces I picked because I wanted to hear what they sounded like under my own hands, no recital deadline attached to any of it. I'm more grateful to Mrs. Okafor for that last lesson than for any of the technique she built into my hands over eleven years, because she gave me the one thing that made it possible to come back to the instrument at all: proof that it could exist without anyone judging what I did with it. I don't think she knew, when she pulled that Satie off the shelf, that she was handing me a reason to keep going instead of a goodbye. I don't think I knew it either, not for a while.
