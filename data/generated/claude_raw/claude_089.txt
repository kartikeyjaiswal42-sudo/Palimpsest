I've been cut from varsity soccer three years in a row now, freshman through junior year, and somewhere in the middle of that streak I started doing something I never told my coaches about, because it felt private in a way that trying out never did: I started watching full ninety-minute matches with a notebook, pausing constantly, mapping out where players moved when they didn't have the ball.

It began almost by accident. After the sophomore-year cut, I couldn't sleep, and I put on a random match to distract myself, and instead of watching the ball the way I always had, I found myself watching the fullback on the far side of the field who never touched it for the first fifteen minutes. He was constantly adjusting his position by a few feet, shifting to cover space that didn't exist yet, reading the game two passes ahead. I paused the video. I rewound it. I watched those fifteen minutes probably six times before I understood what he was actually doing, and by the time I looked up it was past two in the morning and I had somehow filled four pages of a notebook with arrows.

This became a routine without my really deciding it should. Most nights, at least three a week, I'd watch forty-five minutes of match footage and lose an hour and a half to it, tracking one player at a time across an entire half, noting the tiny anticipatory movements that never show up on a highlight reel because nothing dramatic happens in them — a step to the left before a pass even gets played, a shoulder check over one side instead of the other. I couldn't have told you, freshman year, that soccer had this much invisible architecture underneath the visible game. I know it now the way you know something you've stared at for hundreds of hours.

What captivates me about it, if I'm honest, is that it's the one part of this sport where being smaller and slower than the players who made varsity doesn't matter at all. Spacing and anticipation aren't athletic gifts. They're a kind of literacy, something you build by paying obsessive attention over time, and I have paid that attention more consistently, I think, than most players who've actually made the roster I keep missing.

I don't have a coach for this part. I go looking for it myself — old broadcasts, post-match tactical breakdowns from analysts online, occasionally a forum where people argue for hours about a single defensive shape. Nobody assigned me this. I built the routine out of three consecutive disappointments and a lot of empty evenings, and it's become the thing I actually think about when I'm supposed to be doing homework, the notebook now on its fourth volume, still full of arrows nobody but me has ever asked to see.
