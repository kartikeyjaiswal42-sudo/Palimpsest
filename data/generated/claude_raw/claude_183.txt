My mother keeps a laminated index card with a hundred questions on it, and by February the lamination had gone soft and gray at the corners from how many times she folded it into her coat pocket. Question 57: Who is the Commander in Chief of the military? Question 58: Who signs bills into law? She had the answers memorized the way you memorize a phone number, not the way you understand a sentence, and this was the first thing we fought about.

I wanted her to understand what she was memorizing. She wanted to pass.

Every night after her shift at the pharmacy, I quizzed her at the kitchen table, and every night I tried to sneak in an extra question, not off the card, a real one. Why do we have three branches of government? What does "checks and balances" actually mean, not the dictionary version, but why anyone thought it mattered enough to build a country around it? She would answer with whatever fragment of the flashcard she could recall, and when it didn't fit my question, she would go quiet, then annoyed, and eventually she would say, in Vietnamese, so there was no mistaking how serious she was, that I was making this harder than it needed to be. She did not need to understand the Federalist Papers. She needed to answer six out of ten questions correctly in front of an officer who had the power to say no.

I kept pushing anyway. I told her that if she only memorized answers she wasn't really becoming a citizen, she was just passing a quiz, and I remember the exact look on her face when I said it, because it is the closest I have ever come to hurting her on purpose. She said, and I'm translating loosely, that citizenship had already cost her a country, a language, a version of herself that used to be funny in front of large groups, and she was not going to let her seventeen-year-old daughter tell her it wasn't real enough.

We never resolved this. I would like to write that we found a compromise, that I learned to meet her where she was and she let me in a little further, and maybe some of that is even true. She did let me write short practice scenarios in the margins of the flashcards instead of definitions, and she used a few of them without ever admitting she liked them. But the actual disagreement, whether understanding matters more than passing, whether my version of loving her, wanting her to feel the citizenship instead of just holding it, was really about her or about me, stayed unsettled. It is still unsettled. She passed the test in March. She missed one question, the one about the three branches, which she got wrong in front of the officer in almost the exact way she used to get it wrong in front of me, and she cried in the car afterward in a way that had nothing to do with pride and everything to do with relief that it was finally over.

I used to think an experience like this is supposed to end with a tidy insight, the kind you put in an essay exactly like this one. I don't have that. What I have is the memory of sitting across from my mother at a table with a soft gray index card between us, both of us right in ways the other one couldn't quite accept, and the understanding, which I am still working out, that you can love someone completely and still think, quietly, for years, that they are doing an important thing the wrong way. I don't know what to do with that yet. I'm not sure it's the kind of thing you're supposed to do anything with.
