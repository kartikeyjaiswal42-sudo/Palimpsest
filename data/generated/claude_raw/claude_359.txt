Every Sunday for the last year and a half, I have made my grandmother the same cup of tea in the same chipped blue cup and told her the same story about the summer she taught my father to fish on Lake Wachusett. I do this even though she does not remember the lake, or my father as a boy, or, on her harder days, that I am her granddaughter and not some pleasant stranger who keeps showing up with tea. My mother thinks I should stop.

My mother is not a cold person. She visits too, more than I do in raw hours, handling the parts of my grandmother's care that are not sentimental at all — the medication schedule, the arguments with the insurance company, the physical therapist who comes twice a week. I respect her judgment about almost everything, and on this one thing we have never agreed, not once, not in eighteen months of the same conversation happening in slightly different words in the car on the way home from the memory care facility.

Her position is that the ritual is for me, not for my grandmother, and that dressing it up as something my grandmother needs is a way of protecting myself from admitting she's already mostly gone. She says watching me tell the fishing story to a woman who looks at me blankly through most of it is its own kind of grief, delayed and drawn out, and that at some point continuing to do it stops being love and starts being a refusal to let go. She is a hospice social worker's daughter herself, my mother, raised by a woman who believed in clean endings, and she has never trusted rituals that outlive their audience.

I kept doing it anyway. Not out of stubbornness exactly, or not only that. The first few months, it really was for me — I'll admit that much, because my mother is usually right about people, including me. I needed a shape to Sunday afternoons that didn't feel like watching someone disappear in real time. The chipped cup, the same route from the parking lot, the same three sentences about my father falling off the dock in his church clothes — it gave me something to do with my hands and my voice while everything else in that room was uncertain.

Then, about four months ago, on a Sunday I almost skipped because I had a physics test the next morning, my grandmother said, halfway through the story, before I got to the part about the dock, "he ruined his good shoes." She wasn't looking at me. She said it to the window, flat, like a fact surfacing out of water. I stopped talking. I asked her what she meant, and the moment closed over again, and she went back to looking at me the way she looks at the nurses, politely, from somewhere else. But she said it. Four words that belonged to a story only she and I and my father had ever told, and she pulled them up from wherever she'd put them, on the one Sunday in eighteen months when I'd told the story in almost exactly the same words for the two hundredth time.

I called my mother from the parking lot, shaking a little, and told her what happened. She was quiet for a second and then she said that was wonderful, genuinely, and also that one sentence in a hundred visits didn't change what she thought the ritual was doing to me. She might be right about that too. I don't think either of us is going to change our mind, not really — she still thinks I go for myself, I still think there's a version of my grandmother listening under the one who forgets, and neither of us can prove it to the other.

I still make the tea in the chipped cup. I still tell the story, in the same order, the dock and the shoes and the fish that got away, every single Sunday, half for her and, my mother would say, half for the seventeen-year-old who isn't ready to stop.
