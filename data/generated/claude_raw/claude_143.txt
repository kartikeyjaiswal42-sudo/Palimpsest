My grandmother taught me to sew the summer I was fourteen, hunched together over her old Singer in the back room she called the sewing porch even though it had been enclosed and heated for twenty years. She showed me how to read a pattern, how to press a seam flat with the iron before you trust it, how to rip out stitches without tearing the fabric when you inevitably mess up. I didn't think much of it as a skill worth having until two years later, when the community theatre down the street put out a call for volunteers to help with costumes for their production of a play set in the 1920s, and I showed up mostly because my friend Naomi wanted a scene partner for auditions and I had nothing better to do that Saturday.

I ended up staying in the costume room instead of auditioning, and I never really left. I spent that fall building drop-waist dresses out of fabric bought on clearance, hand-beading a flapper costume because the machine kept eating the delicate mesh, staying until eleven some nights before a dress rehearsal because a hem had come undone and the actress needed it fixed before she went on. It became, without my really deciding it should, the thing I did most Tuesdays and Thursdays and most of my Saturdays, for almost two years now.

My grandmother is the one I keep arguing with about it, and it's an argument we've never actually finished, because neither of us wants to be the one who ends it wrong. She taught me to sew as something practical — a skill for mending, for making do, the kind of usefulness she learned from her own mother during years when you didn't throw out a dress because a button fell off. She is proud of the costumes, genuinely proud, comes to every show and cries a little at the curtain call regardless of what the play actually is. But she thinks I spend too much time on it. She says it in a specific tone, not angry, more worried, the tone she uses when she thinks I'm giving something too much of myself for too little return. She wants me to spend more of my Saturdays on things that "count," her word, meaning things a college application can measure.

I've tried to explain to her that this is the thing that counts, to me, more than almost anything else I do. That the two hours I spent last month getting the beading on a 1920s dress to catch the stage light exactly right taught me more about patience and precision than any class has. That I like being useful in a room where being useful looks like something you can hold in your hands. She listens. She nods. And then, a week later, she'll ask, gently, whether I've thought about cutting back before senior year gets busier, and I'll say I'm managing fine, and neither of us pushes further, because we both know we're not going to agree and we'd rather leave it soft than make it sharp.

What gets me is that she's the reason I can do this at all. Every stitch I put into a costume traces back to her patience with my clumsy fourteen-year-old hands on that Singer. I am doing, with the thing she gave me, something she isn't sure she wanted me to do with it. I don't know how to hold both of those at once except to keep showing up on Tuesdays, keep calling her after every opening night, and keep letting the disagreement sit there between us, unresolved, the way you leave a seam unpressed when you're not sure yet which way it should fold.
