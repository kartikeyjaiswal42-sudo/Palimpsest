The old woman who'd lived three houses down from us taught me how to keep bees on a rooftop, which is a sentence I never expected to say about my life.

Her name was Mrs. Sharma, and she had been keeping bees on the roof of her building for thirty years. I'd noticed them there the summer I was fifteen, noticed the white boxes and the way she moved around them with such certainty. I asked her about them while she was watering plants on her front porch, and she invited me up to show me.

I was going to look for maybe five minutes. I stayed for three hours.

She explained everything with such care—how the bees organize themselves, how the queen lays eggs, how you can tell if a colony is healthy by the pattern of comb they build. She showed me how to move through the hive without startling them, how to read what the bees were doing and what they needed. She said that people think beekeeping is about managing bees, but it's really about listening to what they're trying to do and helping them do it.

After that, I came up to her roof every week. She had seven hives, and each one had its own personality. One hive was aggressive and protective. Another was calm and curious. She could look at a hive for thirty seconds and tell you what was happening inside it. I asked her how she learned, and she said experience and reading and talking to other beekeepers and just paying attention.

I was going to stop the next summer when she got sick. She was hospitalized for a week, and I went to visit her, and when I asked about the bees, she said her daughter would have to handle them now. I understood—beekeeping requires energy and attention, and she was going to need to focus on recovering.

But when she came home, the bees were still there, and she was still struggling. Her daughter was helping, but the daughter had a job and two kids and wasn't passionate about the bees the way Mrs. Sharma was. The hives weren't getting the attention they needed.

I asked if I could help.

It became a daily thing. Every morning before school and every afternoon when I got home, I'd go up to her roof and check on the hives. I learned to recognize signs of disease, to identify when we needed to harvest honey or move brood, to read the signals that Mrs. Sharma had been reading for thirty years. She'd sit on a chair on the roof—she couldn't do the physical work anymore—and I'd do what she was telling me to do. She was the mind. I was the hands.

At some point during this process, something changed in me about the whole thing. It stopped being something I was doing for Mrs. Sharma and became something I genuinely cared about. I became invested in keeping those bees alive. I looked up research on honey bee decline. I got angry at the pesticides people used in their gardens. I started seeing the bees as important in a way that went beyond just the beekeeping hobby.

By the time Mrs. Sharma passed away the following year, I had started keeping two of my own hives on the flat part of our roof. Her daughter found me a mentor in a local beekeeping group. I'm still doing it now, three years later. I have five hives at this point. I harvest honey and I give jars to friends and neighbors. I've learned everything I can find to learn about bee health and colony management.

What I mean when I think about changing my mind about something is that I started out just trying to help an old woman who was sick. I didn't go into it thinking I was going to develop a genuine passion for beekeeping. I didn't imagine that I'd end up devoting this much of my time and energy to something I didn't even know existed a few years before. But that's exactly what happened. Something that started as duty turned into genuine care, and now it's part of how I understand myself in the world.
