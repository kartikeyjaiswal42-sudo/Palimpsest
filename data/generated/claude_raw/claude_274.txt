I started building furniture from pallets when I was about fourteen because I watched a YouTube video and thought it looked easy. It absolutely was not easy, but I kept doing it anyway, and at some point it stopped being a thing I was trying to learn and became just something I did.

Every Saturday morning, I'd get up and go to the industrial area near our house where they stack pallets behind buildings. I'd pick out the ones that looked solid, haul them back home, and spend the day disassembling them. Sand the boards. Figure out what I could actually make. Sometimes it was shelves. Sometimes it was a small table. Sometimes I'd start a project that made no sense and I'd scrap it halfway through.

I never did it thinking it was going to become my passion or lead to anything. I just did it. I liked the smell of the sawdust. I liked how predictable it was—pick up pallets, take them apart, see what could become of them. I liked that it didn't require any money to start with, just time and effort.

Over the course of two years, I probably built fifteen pieces of furniture. Most of them are still in our house. My mum uses the shelf I made in the kitchen. My little brother sleeps on a bed frame I built when I was fifteen. None of it is perfect. Most of it has rough edges or slightly misaligned joints. If I looked at it with a critical eye, I'd see all the mistakes.

But that's actually the thing I learned from doing this over and over, the piece that mattered more than learning to use a saw properly or understanding how wood grain works or any of the technical stuff. I learned that something doesn't have to be perfect to be valuable. Something doesn't have to be professionally made to be useful. You can take waste material and transform it into something that serves a real purpose in your life.

That might sound small. It probably is small. But it changed how I think about a lot of things. My parents would stress about keeping the house looking nice and perfect, and I remember thinking, the functional furniture that I built is more valuable to us right now than any of the expensive stuff that we're supposed to keep pristine and untouched. Use what you have. Make it work. That's enough.

Now, when I'm working on anything—schoolwork, projects, conversations, relationships—I think about those pallets. I think about how the imperfect thing you actually create is better than the perfect thing you never make because you're too afraid to start. I think about how you learn by doing the small, repeated thing over and over, not by theorizing about it or planning it to death.

My guidance counselor asked me last year what my hobbies were, and I mentioned the pallet furniture. She asked if I'd ever thought about carpentry or woodworking as a career. I hadn't, actually. It wasn't a path thing for me. It was just something I did, a way I thought about my own capacity to create things from scraps. But I think that attitude—the willingness to start with whatever you have and build something useful from it—that's something I want to hold onto, no matter what I end up doing.
