My grandmother makes a dish called ghormeh sabzi about nine times a year, and she has never written it down, and she will not, and this is the only thing she and I have ever genuinely fought about.

I started trying to record it when I was fourteen. I stood at her elbow with a notebook. She would put a handful of dried fenugreek into her palm and I would say how much, and she would say this much, and hold out her palm, and I would write "1 palm (Maman's palm, smaller than mine)" and she would laugh at me. The kidney beans soak overnight, except when they don't. The limoo omani get pierced with a fork, four holes or five, and when I asked whether it mattered she said it depends on the lime.

I kept at it for two years. I have eleven versions in a green notebook. None of them work. I have made it seventeen times and it has been good four times and I could not tell you what was different.

The fight, when it came, was in her kitchen in March, over the herbs. I wanted her to weigh them. I had bought a scale. I said, if you don't write it down it dies with you, and I said it in that flat cruel way you can only use on somebody who will forgive you, and she put the scale in the drawer under the tea towels and said, "Then you will learn it the way I learned it."

She learned it standing next to her own mother in Shiraz for eleven years. She was not taught. She was present. She says the recipe is not information, that the amounts change with the herbs and the herbs change with the year, and that a written recipe would teach me to follow a number instead of the pot. I say that is a beautiful idea that will not survive her, and that half her sisters are gone and I cannot call them.

We have had this argument maybe six times. It has not moved. She is eighty-one.

What changed is not the argument. What changed is that I stopped bringing the notebook.

I go on Saturdays now. I do not write anything. I chop, badly at first, and she corrects my knife by moving my hand, and I have learned that the herbs go in when the onions are past golden and starting to catch, and that you know the sabzi is done frying when the kitchen stops smelling green and starts smelling dark, which is not a sentence I could have written down two years ago because I did not have the sense it describes. I know now that her palm holds about forty grams. I measured it once, when she was out of the room, which I am not proud of and which I did anyway.

I still think I am right. I think she is wrong about the notebook, and I think it is a kind of vanity, and I have not said that part out loud and I am not going to. She still thinks I am a person who wants a shortcut. Neither of us has conceded anything.

But I have made it three times now without her in the room and it has been right all three, and the last time my mother went quiet at the table, which is the only review that counts in our house. So something transferred. Not through the notebook. Through two hundred Saturdays and a hand on my hand.

The green notebook is still in my desk with its eleven wrong versions. I have not thrown it out. I do not expect to win, and I still go every week, because she is eighty-one and the arithmetic is not complicated.
