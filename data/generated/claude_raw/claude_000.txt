My grandmother's English is about two hundred words and eighty of them are food. My Cantonese is worse. So for most of my childhood we communicated through a system of pointing, eyebrows, and my mother, and I assumed that was just the arrangement and that there was nothing underneath it.

I started cooking with her because of a school thing. Year Ten, a project on family heritage, and I needed something to photograph. I was going to make her turnip cake, take pictures, write four hundred words about tradition, and hand it in. That was the plan. It was not, I want to be honest, an act of love. It was homework.

The first afternoon was awful. She would say a word and I would not know it. I would hold something up and she would shake her head and take it off me. At one point I put the ginger in and she made a noise I had never heard her make, took the pan off the heat, and started again from the beginning. Forty minutes gone. I nearly walked out. I remember standing at the sink being seventeen kinds of annoyed and thinking that this was going to be the worst-documented school project in the borough.

Then something happened that I have thought about ever since.

She wanted me to grate the turnip — lo bak, it's a daikon, "turnip" is just what everyone calls it — and she wanted it a specific way. She could not tell me. She had no word for it and neither did I. So she took my wrist. Not the grater, my wrist. And she moved my arm through the stroke she wanted, four or five times, holding my hand around the vegetable, adjusting the angle, and then she let go and watched.

I did it wrong. She took my wrist again.

We did that for maybe ten minutes. And somewhere in there I stopped being annoyed, because I realised I was being taught. Properly taught, in detail, with corrections, by a woman I had spent sixteen years thinking of as someone I could not talk to.

That's the thing I got wrong. I had been treating language as the whole channel. If we had no shared words we had no shared anything, and the relationship was therefore a limited one — polite, warm, shallow. And it turns out that a huge amount of what one person can hand another does not go through words at all. It goes through the wrist. Through the angle of a knife, the sound the oil makes when it's ready, the number of seconds you leave something before you move it. She has forty years of that and none of it is in a language, so it had been sitting there my whole life, transmissible, and I had never asked because I assumed the door was shut.

We cook most Sundays now. Not always Chinese food, oddly — she has opinions about my roast potatoes. I have picked up maybe sixty words, mostly ingredients and one phrase that I think means "too much" and might mean something ruder. She has learned to say "leave it" and she says it constantly.

I did hand in the project. It got a B. The photographs were bad because my hands were covered in grated turnip.

What I would say about my background, if I have to say something, is not that I'm Chinese, or half, or a bit, or whatever the form wants. It's that I grew up next to a person who was full of things I could not access, and I found the door by accident while trying to get a piece of coursework done, and it turned out the door was in the kitchen and it had been unlocked the entire time. I think about how close I came to never opening it. There was no reason I had to do that project on her. I could have done it on my dad's side, who all speak English, and it would have been easier and I would have got a better grade.

She's eighty-one. We have Sundays.
