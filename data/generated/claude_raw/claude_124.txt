Three mornings a week for the last three years I have left the house at ten past six and walked twenty minutes to a flooded gravel pit on the edge of town that the council calls a nature reserve and everybody else calls the pits. I am back by half seven. I do not tell many people about it, partly because it sounds like an affectation and partly because for two of those three years I was doing it for a reason that did not work out.

The reason was a bittern.

There had been one at the pits in the winter of 2021, before I started. Somebody photographed it in the reeds at the north end and it went on the county bird group's site, and there were about forty records over six weeks and then it left. A bittern is a heron that has decided to be invisible: streaked brown, motionless, standing in reeds pretending to be reeds. Most people who watch birds in this country go years without seeing one. Some never do.

I decided I was going to see one at my pits. That was the project. That was, in a real sense, why the habit started at all — I had a target and the target justified the alarm at half five.

I did not see one. Not in the first winter, not in the second, not in the third. I stood at the north end in fog and in rain and in a cold that made my hands stop working, and I looked at reeds. I got very good at looking at reeds. There was a morning in January two years ago when I convinced myself for a full ninety seconds that a dead branch at the edge of the reedbed was a bittern, and the feeling when it resolved back into a branch is one I can still summon exactly.

So: I failed. Three years, roughly four hundred and fifty mornings, one specific goal, not achieved.

What I did next is that I kept going, and I would like to be honest about why, because the honest answer is not perseverance. I kept going because by then the habit had detached itself from the reason. I had stopped needing the bittern about halfway through the second winter and had not noticed. The alarm went off and I went, in the way that you go to a place where your life happens.

And the thing that had grown underneath the failed project turned out to be worth much more than the project. I have three years of notes on that pit now — sixty-odd pages, undated at first because I was careless and then rigorously dated because I taught myself better. I know that the gadwall arrive in the second week of October within about four days every year. I know which of the three willows on the west bank the kingfisher uses and that it changed from the middle one to the far one after a branch came down in 2023. I know that the water level drops about forty centimetres between May and August and that when it does, a mud shelf appears at the south end and waders stop there — I have logged green sandpiper eleven times on that shelf and never anywhere else on the site.

Last spring I sent the notes to the county recorder, mostly because a man I met on the path told me I should. He wrote back and asked if I would take on the pits as a formal recording site, which means I am now the person whose numbers go into the county report. I am seventeen and I am nobody, and I got that because I turned up four hundred and fifty times to fail at seeing one bird.

I still look at the north end every time. I would still like to see a bittern.

But what I actually learned is that I am bad at judging in advance which part of a thing is the valuable part. I chose the bittern because it was rare and hard and would have made a good story. The good story was the gadwall arriving in the second week of October, every year, unremarkably, in front of a boy who happened to be standing there.
