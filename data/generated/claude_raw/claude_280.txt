The land is four acres outside the village my father grew up in, and it has not been farmed since my grandfather died. There is a well, a line of mango trees, and a boundary wall fallen down on the eastern side. Nobody in my family lives within three hundred kilometres of it. Somebody offered to buy it two years ago and that is when the arguing started.

I was on my father's side, which was the side that said sell. His case was practical and I could repeat it in my sleep. We pay tax on it. We pay a man in the village to look at it. Twice a year my father takes three days off work to deal with a dispute about a boundary or a document, and comes back tired and short-tempered. The money would clear most of what is left on our flat. Nobody is going to farm it.

My mother's side was not really an argument, which is what made it so annoying. She would say that it was my father's father's land. She would say you cannot buy it back. When pressed she went quiet, and my father took the silence as a concession, and three weeks later we would be having the same conversation.

I was loudly practical about this for eighteen months. I am not proud of how much I enjoyed being the one who agreed with the sensible parent.

What changed my mind was a Tuesday in April when I went there with my father because somebody had to sign something. I had not been since I was about nine. While he was inside with the clerk I walked out to the trees.

There is a stone at the base of the third mango tree, flat on top. My father had mentioned it perhaps twice in my life. He used to sit on it. That is all it is — a stone a boy sat on in the nineteen-eighties while his father worked, still exactly where it was, and it will be there whether we sell or not.

I sat on it for about ten minutes and did not have a revelation. What I had was duller and harder to argue with: the physical understanding that this place had existed continuously the whole time I had been discussing it at our dinner table. It was there while I was making the tax point.

I had been treating the land as a line on a balance sheet because that was the only form in which it appeared to me — a number, a burden, a set of documents. My mother had never been able to explain her position in that language, and I had taken her inability to translate as an absence of reasons. She could not put four acres into a sentence, and I had decided that anything that could not be put into a sentence did not count.

We have not sold it. I am not certain that is right; my father's arguments are all still true, and last year the trip cost him five days. But when it comes up now I am the one who says let us wait another year, and he looks at me as though I have changed teams, which I have.

What I take from it, and what I think is genuinely mine rather than inherited, is a suspicion of my own fluency. I am good at making a case. I can lay out three reasons in order at speed. That skill made me confident that I understood a thing I had not stood on in eight years.
