My mother thinks that summer was the worst thing that happened to me, and I have never been able to convince her otherwise. We have had this argument, in different forms, maybe forty times. It always ends the same way, with her saying "you were fifteen" as though that settles it, and me saying "I know" as though that settles it too.

The shelf was in the Fairmount branch, second floor, the section between the window and the water fountain. Call numbers 813 through 813.54, American fiction, which meant it ran from a water-stained Willa Cather to four copies of the same John Updike nobody had checked out since 2009. Seventy-one books. I counted them the first week of June because I wanted to know what I was agreeing to.

I did not choose that shelf for any reason worth defending. My friend Priya had gone to Ahmedabad for the summer, the pool cost eleven dollars, and my father had just moved into the apartment on Delancey with the balcony that faced a parking structure. The library was air conditioned and free and nobody there asked me how I was doing. I sat in the same orange chair every morning at ten. I read the way you eat when you are not hungry but the food is in front of you.

Here is where my mother and I disagree. She says I hid. She says a fifteen-year-old who reads seventy-one novels in eleven weeks is not curious, he is barricaded, and that if she had understood at the time what she was watching she would have driven me to the pool herself and paid the eleven dollars. She says this with real grief in her voice, which is the part I cannot argue with, because I think she is grieving something she did not do.

And I say: but I liked it.

That is my whole case, and I know how thin it sounds. I liked it. Not every book. I abandoned three, which felt like a betrayal of the project until I decided that abandoning was allowed if I could say why. I liked the strange accident of order — reading a 1922 novel about a farm on Monday and a 1978 novel about a divorce on Tuesday, and finding, without looking for it, that both of them had a scene where somebody stands in a doorway and does not come in. Nobody assigned me that. No teacher had built a unit around doorways. I found it because I was going alphabetically through a shelf like a person walking a fence line, and the pattern surfaced on its own.

That is the thing I have tried to explain to her and failed. When you read everything in a category, including the bad ones, including the four Updikes, you stop reading for the good parts and start reading for the shape of the whole. You notice what a decade was afraid of. You notice which sentences were fashionable. I did not have the vocabulary for any of this at fifteen. I only had the feeling that the books were talking to each other over my head, and that if I stayed in the chair long enough I would catch the thread of it.

I still go back to that shelf. It has been reordered twice; the Cather is gone. I am applying to study literature, and I have practiced saying that out loud to my mother in a way that does not sound like an accusation, because it isn't one. She was right that I was sad. She has always been right about that. What she has never accepted is that the sadness and the reading were not the same thing wearing one coat. One of them ended in September. The other one is why I am writing this.

We are still not agreed. Last month she said, "You would have been a person who read anyway." Maybe. I cannot run the summer again without the divorce in it. But I know what I was doing in that chair, and it was not hiding. It was the first work I ever chose.
