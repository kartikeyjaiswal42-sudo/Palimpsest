Every Sunday since I was eleven I have walked the fence line at my grandmother's with a bucket of staples and a hammer. It takes about forty minutes. Most weeks I find nothing. Maybe four times a year a post has rotted at the base and leans, and I mark it with orange tape for my uncle, and that is the whole job. My mother calls it my chore. I have never thought of it as one, because nobody assigned it to me. I started going out with my grandfather when I was small, and then he died, and I kept going.

The land is sixty-one acres and we do not farm it. My uncle leases forty of it to a neighbor for soybeans and the rest is scrub and a creek. Last February a developer offered a number I am not going to write down, because it embarrassed everyone at the table. My mother wanted to sell. My uncle did not. The argument went on four months and it was the worst thing my family has ever done to itself.

I was sure my uncle was right. It is our land, my grandfather's father cleared part of it, and you do not sell that. I made that argument at the kitchen table in March with real heat, and my mother listened and then asked me one question. She asked how many times I had been out there in the last year. I said every Sunday. She said, "And your uncle?"

I did not answer, because I knew. He comes for the lease check and at Christmas. He had not walked the fence in six years.

What I understood then, and what took me the rest of the spring to accept, is that I had been treating the land as an inheritance and my mother had been treating it as a bill. She is the one who pays the taxes on it. She has paid them since 2016 out of a paycheck from a job she does not like, and she had never once mentioned that to me, and my uncle had never once mentioned it either, because it was easier for both of us to be the people who loved the land while she was the person who kept it.

I still think we should keep it. But I stopped arguing the way I had been. I asked her to show me the tax bill. Then I asked what the lease brings in and why the two numbers do not meet. Then, in July, I asked the neighbor who leases the forty whether he wanted the scrub acres for pasture, and he did. It is not enough to fix anything, but it was the first time in that entire argument that anyone brought a number instead of a feeling.

We have not sold. We may still. My mother has not forgiven my uncle and I do not know that she should. What changed in me is smaller than the outcome: I found out that the thing I was proudest of — forty minutes a week with a hammer — had let me feel like a steward without ever costing me anything. Loving something is not the same as carrying it. I had confused the two for six years, every Sunday, in orange tape.
