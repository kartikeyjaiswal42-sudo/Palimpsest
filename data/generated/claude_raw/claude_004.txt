My grandmother taught me to cook without ever explaining anything, because she could not, and because I do not think she would have anyway. She came from Fuzhou at sixty-one and her English stayed at about thirty words, most of them nouns. My Mandarin is worse than her English and my Fuzhounese does not exist. So the arrangement we arrived at was this: on Saturday mornings I stood on her left side at the counter, and I did whatever she did, roughly a beat behind.

The habit was the beat behind. That was the whole method. She would take a piece of ginger and hit it with the flat of the cleaver, and I would take a piece of ginger and hit it with the flat of the cleaver. If I did it wrong she took the cleaver out of my hand and did it again slowly, twice, and gave it back. If I did it right she said nothing whatsoever. For about two years I thought silence meant she had not noticed.

I learned the food this way — red-braised pork, fish soup with a great deal of white pepper, the sticky rice she wrapped in leaves for the summer festival. But the thing I actually learned was smaller. Somewhere in the third year I noticed that she tasted everything about eleven times. Not at the end. Throughout. She would dip a chopstick, touch it to her tongue, adjust, and dip again a minute later, and I had been standing next to this for a hundred and fifty Saturdays without registering it as a technique. I had been waiting for a recipe. She was not using one.

Once I saw it I could not stop seeing it. It is why her soup is better than my mother's, who cooks from a book and tastes once at the end and then it is what it is. And it is, I think, why my grandmother is the only person in our family who has never seemed frightened of arriving somewhere new. She got off a plane at sixty-one into a country whose language she had no intention of learning properly, and she did what she does with soup: put something in, taste, adjust, taste again.

I applied it to my own work almost by accident. I used to write essays the way my mother cooks — plan, write, hand in, receive the verdict. Now I read paragraphs aloud while I am still in the middle of them, which my brother finds unbearable, and I have stopped treating a chemistry procedure as a set of instructions to be executed rather than checked.

My grandmother is eighty-one and has arthritis in both hands, so mostly now she sits at the kitchen table and I stand at the counter, and the beat has reversed. She watches. She still takes the spoon out of my hand sometimes, which at seventeen is annoying in a way it was not at eleven, and she still says nothing at all when I get it right.

Last month I made the fish soup for eleven people at my cousin's engagement and three relatives asked for the recipe. There is no recipe. There is a chopstick and a tongue and the willingness to keep changing your mind about what the pot needs. They thought I was being difficult. My grandmother, at the end of the table, went on eating and did not look up.
