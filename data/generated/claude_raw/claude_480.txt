For most of last year I wanted the cows gone. I want to be honest about that first, because the story only works if you know I was on the wrong side of it and pleased with myself for being there.

We have two. Sona is older and has a torn ear from a dog when she was a calf. Lali kicks. Between them, on a good week, eleven litres a day, which the collection centre at the crossing takes at forty-two rupees. I know the number because I was the one who started writing it down.

That was the whole trouble. Ninth standard, I had learned to make a table in a notebook, and I made one. Fodder, the green stuff and the dry, the vet twice a year, the man who comes for artificial insemination, the medicine after Lali's fever, the four hours a day my mother spends that nobody costs at anything. Against it, milk. The bottom line came out to something like eighty rupees a day, and I remember thinking eighty rupees, that is one plate of chowmein at the bus stand.

I argued it at dinner for about seven months. Sell them, put the money in the bank, sleep past five in the morning like a normal person. My father mostly said nothing, which I took as winning. My mother said things I dismissed, about dung for the field and for the fuel cakes, about how you cannot buy milk for a house where a child has to have milk, about Sona being there before I was. Sentiment, I thought. I had a table.

In February a man from Bakewar came with a tempo and an offer for both. Papa walked him around the shed and then walked him to the road, and came back and sat on the charpai and said the number out loud, and then he said what it was for. Coaching in Kanpur. Physics, chemistry, maths. Two years. For me. He did not say it like a big speech. He said it the way you say the price of diesel.

I had done a whole table and left myself out of it. That is the part I keep going back to. Every rupee I had counted as a loss was a rupee holding up a house that was about to spend everything it had on one person, and that person had been sitting there for seven months explaining efficiency. I said no. Out loud, straight away, before I had a reason ready. It surprised everybody including me.

We kept them. The argument did not end that night, it just changed sides, and my mother is the one who now says maybe we should sell Lali, who is genuinely a menace. I do the five o'clock feed four days a week now. I am not good at it and Lali still kicks and I have not become a person who loves cows. Sona's ear still has the tear in it. I check it without meaning to.

What changed is smaller and harder to say. I had thought being right was a matter of getting the arithmetic correct. Now I think the difficult part is knowing what belongs in the table. Four hours of my mother's morning was in mine as a cost, but four hours of hers was never in it as a gift, and I was not in it at all. Somebody who is very sure of his numbers should probably check who wrote the categories.

I still have the notebook. The last page has the eighty rupees on it, underlined twice, which I am leaving there.
