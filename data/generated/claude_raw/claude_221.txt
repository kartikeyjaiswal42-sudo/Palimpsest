The calculator I tutor with is a TI-84 my cousin left behind when she went to college, and the battery cover has been held on with a strip of blue painter's tape for so long that the tape has gone gray and I've stopped noticing it's there. Half the buttons have lost their printed labels from being pressed so many times — the 2 and the equals sign are basically smooth plastic now, no numbers left on them at all, so you have to know where they are by memory. I've used this thing almost every school day for two years, in a spare classroom off the cafeteria where I tutor algebra during lunch to kids who are failing it.

I started tutoring because a friend was about to fail ninth grade math and asked me to help her study for a retake, not because I had some plan about contributing to anything. I sat with her at a picnic table for maybe forty minutes with this calculator between us, walking through factoring, and she passed the retake by six points. Word got around the way it does in a school that size. By the next semester I had a standing lunch slot and a list of names on a sign-up sheet taped to the classroom door, and the calculator went with me every single day because most of the kids I work with don't own one, or their families can't afford the eighty dollars a graphing calculator costs, which nobody tells you is basically required equipment for algebra in most classrooms here.

There's a girl, Jasmine, who I tutored for most of last year. She'd failed the same unit test twice and had decided, out loud, in front of me, that she was "just not a math person," which is a sentence I've heard from almost every kid I've worked with, always delivered like a fact about themselves rather than a gap in what they'd been taught. She was suspicious of me the first few sessions, answered in single words, clearly there because a teacher had told her to be. What actually opened things up wasn't anything I explained. It was that I'd hand her the calculator to check her own work, and she'd hand it back, and I'd use it, and we kept passing it back and forth across the table for weeks before she said much of anything real to me. There's something about sharing one physical object between two people, taking turns with it, that does work a conversation alone doesn't.

By March she was correcting my arithmetic before I finished the problem on the board. She still has the same handwriting she started with, cramped and slanting hard to the right, but she stopped erasing every wrong attempt like it was evidence against her, started leaving the crossed-out work visible, which I think is its own kind of progress nobody grades.

What I'd bring to a campus isn't really about being good at math, though I am decent at it. It's this specific willingness to sit across a table from someone who has decided they're bad at a subject and just keep showing up, keep handing over the calculator, keep not being impatient with the sixth wrong attempt at the same kind of problem. That's not a skill anyone taught me in a classroom. I think I learned it from watching how many kids just needed one person to not give up on them before they gave up on themselves, and from a battery cover held together with tape that I've never bothered to actually fix, because somehow it still works exactly the way it needs to.
