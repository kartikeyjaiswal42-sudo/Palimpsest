When I was seven, I went under at Lake Serrano and came up somewhere I didn't recognize, thirty feet from where I'd gone in, my feet not finding bottom no matter how hard I kicked for it. A lifeguard had me by the arm within maybe ten seconds, and by any reasonable measure nothing bad actually happened to me that day. But something closed in my head anyway, some door that shut and stayed shut for nine years. I didn't go past my waist in a pool again until I was sixteen. Bathtubs were fine. Anything deeper than standing, I found a reason to sit out.

I want to be honest that I didn't decide to learn to swim because I had some noble realization about facing fear. I decided because the summer camp where I'd worked the two previous summers, doing arts and crafts and lunch duty, told me that this year every staff member, regardless of role, had to pass a basic swim test — fifty meters, treading water for two minutes — because of a new liability policy after some incident at a different camp in the district. No test, no job. And I needed that job, both for the money and because I genuinely liked the kids and didn't want to lose the position to someone else over something as stupid, in my mind, as not being able to swim.

So in March I signed up for beginner lessons at the YMCA, the ones technically meant for adults but that in practice were about forty percent actual adults and sixty percent kids around eight or nine who just hadn't gotten around to lessons yet. I was sixteen and six foot one and folding myself into a lane next to a second grader named Petey who could already do a passable freestyle. I hated almost every minute of the first three weeks. I hated the smell of the pool, I hated how my body wouldn't do what the instructor said even when I understood the instruction perfectly in my head, I hated feeling like the water was something happening to me rather than something I was doing.

The instructor was a woman named Rosa who'd apparently taught swimming at that Y for over twenty years, and she was patient in a way that I initially mistook for not really seeing how bad I was. Every session for weeks she tried to get me to do a back float — just lie back, let your ears go under, arms out, trust that your lungs will keep you up. I could not do it. Every single time, within two or three seconds, my body would jackknife upright, my legs would drop, I'd be standing chest-deep and breathing hard like I'd actually been drowning instead of just floating on my back in four feet of water with an instructor's hand an inch from my shoulder blade.

The turn, if I can call it that, happened on a Thursday in early May, week seven or eight. I don't remember it being a particularly different day. Rosa had me try the float again, same as always, and I did the same thing, jackknifed up within two seconds, except this time she didn't say try again right away. She just asked me what I thought was going to happen if I actually floated. Not rhetorically — she waited for an answer. And I said, out loud, before I'd really thought about it, that I thought I'd disappear. Which sounds dramatic written down, but that's genuinely the shape of the fear I'd been carrying since I was seven: some belief, never examined, that the water at Serrano had almost taken me somewhere and would finish the job if I ever gave it the chance again.

Rosa didn't make a big thing of it. She just said, quietly, that floating was the opposite of that, that floating was the water holding me up, not pulling me down, and that the panic I kept having was fighting something that wasn't actually trying to hurt me. I went back, leaned my head back into her hand, and made myself stay there past the two-second mark. Three seconds. Five. She took her hand away without telling me and I didn't notice for a full ten seconds, floating on my own, ears underwater, sky directly above me, nobody holding anything.

I didn't become a strong swimmer that day. It took the rest of the spring before I passed the actual test, and I'm still not fast, still not someone you'd want on a relay team. But something did change permanently in that one float. The fear I'd built for nine years wasn't really about water — it was about the idea that losing control meant losing everything, that the second I stopped fighting something I'd be finished. Learning that my body would hold itself up if I just let it, in a swimming pool with a chlorine smell and a second grader doing laps two lanes over, is honestly the closest thing I have to a lesson that actually moved from the pool into the rest of my life.
