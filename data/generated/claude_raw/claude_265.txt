The leash hangs on a hook by my desk, red nylon gone pink from the wash, the clip slightly bent from a car door. It belonged to a dog named Duke, and I am, by any honest measure, one of the worst volunteers the Millbrook Animal Shelter has ever had, which is exactly why I have it.

I signed up sophomore year for service hours and turned out to be bad at nearly all of it. I let a beagle mix slip his collar in the parking lot and had to chase him past four cars while a staff member watched, unimpressed. I mixed up two cats' medication schedules. I was scared of the bigger dogs in a way I tried to hide and clearly didn't hide well, since Priya, who ran the volunteer desk, started quietly assigning me to laundry instead of walks, which I also folded wrong.

By November I'd decided to quit. I sat in the little office off the kennel hallway, waiting to tell Priya I wasn't coming back, rehearsing something about how I wasn't cut out for it, which was true. While I waited, a volunteer walked past with Duke, a fourteen-year-old shepherd mix missing a back leg, slow and gray around the muzzle, the kind of dog nobody photographs well. Nobody wanted to walk him. He took twenty minutes to cover what a healthy dog covered in five, and most people had other dogs to get to.

Priya was on the phone, so I ended up outside holding his leash by default, the only volunteer standing around with nothing better to do. We went maybe forty feet before he stopped, sat down on the pavement, and leaned his whole weight against my leg, breathing hard, not going anywhere for a while. I didn't know what to do except sit down next to him on the curb, so I did, and we stayed there for the better part of ten minutes while I told him, out loud, like an idiot, that I was planning to quit that day.

I didn't quit. I asked Priya, when she got off the phone, if I could keep walking Duke specifically, and she said, a little surprised, that nobody had ever requested him before. I walked him three times a week for the next eight months, twenty slow minutes at a time, until he was adopted by a retired couple who'd specifically asked for an older dog nobody else wanted.

I still can't hold a leash steady around a dog that pulls. I'm still, by most measures, mediocre at the job. But Duke's leash sits on my hook now, and some days it's the only proof I need that being bad at something in general isn't the same as being wrong for it entirely.
