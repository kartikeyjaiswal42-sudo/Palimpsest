The pool at Hamilton High has never been the same since I left, or at least that's what I tell myself when I pass it on my way to school now. The water still looks blue and calm through the glass doors, but I don't see myself there anymore. I used to spend more time in that pool than I did in my own house. From 6 a.m. to 7 a.m. before school, then again after classes until dinner, my life orbited around lane four, the one closest to the left. The coach knew exactly when I would arrive and which goggles I would wear—the black ones with the clear lenses, never the tinted ones because I liked to see the bottom of the pool.

Then junior year, three months before regionals, I tore my rotator cuff. It happened on a Tuesday during a set of butterfly drills, a movement I had done thousands of times. One moment my arm moved through the water the way it always had, and the next moment something inside my shoulder just gave way. The pain was sharp but strangely small, like someone had put a single wrong note in a song I knew perfectly. I kept swimming for another lap before I told Coach.

Everyone said I would come back. The doctor said maybe six months of physical therapy, maybe a year. My parents whispered about surgery in the kitchen, their voices reaching my room as a low hum of possibility. But what I heard underneath all of that was the unspoken thing: maybe not. Maybe the thing that had defined me, that had made me who I was supposed to become, would not be something I did anymore.

For three months after the injury, I didn't go near the pool. I couldn't watch from the deck because it would hurt too much, not in my shoulder but somewhere deeper. My teammates would text to ask how I was doing, and I would respond with thumbs-up emojis that felt like lies. I started sleeping in. I picked up books I had meant to read. I learned to bake bread, actually bake it, not just help my mom measure flour. One Tuesday morning, when I would normally have been in the pool, I went back to that same place and sat on the side for the first time since the injury.

That sounds like a small thing, I know. But sitting there, watching the water and the swimmers moving through it, I realized something unexpected. I wasn't watching them and thinking about what I was missing. I was just watching them, the way a person might watch anything beautiful they weren't part of. And that felt like freedom, which made me angry and then guilty for feeling angry.

The physical therapist asked me eventually if I wanted to try swimming again, just for fitness, just for the feeling of it. And I said yes, and it was strange, learning to move in water like someone who had never been trained, like someone who was just discovering what their body could do. It's been six months now. I still swim, but I'm not training toward anything. The pool doesn't own me anymore. I'm there because I want to be, and somehow that matters more than all the medals I never got to earn.
