The scoreboard at our field is operated by a metal box the size of a briefcase, and the box lives in the equipment shed, and the switch for the home score has been broken since before I joined the team. You have to hold it down. If you let go, the number resets to zero. So somebody has to stand there with a thumb on a switch for the entire second half if we are ahead, which, over three years, was not a problem we had often.

I was that somebody for most of my time on the team, because I was the reserve keeper and the reserve keeper does not play. I want to be honest about this: I was not good. I was tall and I was reliable about showing up, and those two things kept me on the roster for three seasons in which I played, in total, about ninety minutes. My coach never lied to me about it. In our first meeting he said I would probably not play much and asked if I still wanted in, and I said yes without thinking about it, which is the only genuinely brave thing I have ever done, and I did not know it was brave at the time.

Our last season we lost thirteen games. Not close ones, mostly. There is a particular quality to a team that has stopped expecting to win — people stop talking on the pitch, and the silence is worse than the score. By October the parents had thinned out to about eight, and one of them always brought a thermos, and the thermos became a sort of running joke, the last remaining piece of infrastructure.

The last game was away, against a school that had beaten us 6–0 in September, and they were resting players. We went ahead in the eleventh minute. Deven, who had scored twice all year, hit something from outside the box that went in off the underside of the bar, and I remember the sound of it more than the sight, this flat metallic knock, and then the eight parents making the noise of eighty.

We had brought the box. We always brought the box, to away games, because their board was digital and ours was a superstition by then. So I stood behind the bench with my thumb on the switch, holding a 1 in place, for the rest of the match.

It is not a dramatic thing to do. My thumb ached at about the sixty-minute mark and after that it went numb and I switched hands, badly, losing the number for half a second in the changeover, and Deven turned around and shouted at me and then laughed. They equalised in the eighty-first. I let go of the switch. Nobody made me; it just seemed pointless to keep a 1 alive next to a 1.

We drew. Thirteen losses, four draws, no wins. That is the season. I have thought about it more than I have thought about anything I actually won.

What I keep is the shape of the thing. Nobody would have known if I had put the box down. It resets to zero, and everybody watching knows the real score anyway, and the board is a courtesy, not a record. But there is a version of a team where somebody holds the number and a version where nobody does, and they are not the same team, and I cannot fully explain the difference to people who have only played on good ones.

The box is still in the shed. Somebody should fix the switch. I have thought about doing it — it is a five-dollar part, I looked it up in Year Twelve — and I have decided not to. That is probably sentimental and possibly selfish, since whoever is holding it next year would rather I fixed it. But I want there to be one job left on that team that costs something and does not appear anywhere afterwards, and that a fifteen-year-old who is not good at football can do, standing behind a bench in the cold with a thumb going numb, keeping a one where everyone can see it.
