I learned to fix bicycles because my own had broken and my family couldn't afford the repair shop. I watched YouTube videos and taught myself how to align the chain, patch tubes, true wheels. Once I fixed it, I felt capable in a way I hadn't before. So when kids in the neighborhood started asking if I could help with theirs, I said yes. I was fourteen and I thought I was doing something good.

What I got wrong is that I thought understanding the mechanics was enough. One Saturday I spent three hours working on a bike belonging to a nine-year-old named Arun. The brakes had been grinding, and I replaced the pads and adjusted the cables. I was proud of the work. Arun rode it away, and for two days nothing happened. Then his mother came to our house. She was calm about it, which somehow made it worse than if she'd been angry. She said the bike had started pulling to one side, and when Arun swerved to compensate, he'd hit a car parked on the street. He wasn't badly hurt, but he could have been.

I realized that I'd aligned the wheels wrong. When I rushed through that final step, checking them against a wall instead of doing it properly with a true, I'd created an asymmetry that only showed up under real riding conditions. I had assumed that because I understood the theory, the execution didn't matter as much. I had been overconfident in the way that young people often are about things they've only just learned.

It shook me. For a while I didn't want to touch another bike. But Arun's mother asked me to try again, to fix it properly this time, and I did. I also changed how I approached the work. I stopped rushing. I started keeping a notebook where I wrote down what I'd done to each bike, so if something went wrong I could trace my steps. I asked kids to bring their bikes back a week later to check if anything had shifted. I looked up the actual specifications for different wheel sizes instead of guessing.

What changed isn't that I became perfect at it. There's still a lot I don't know about bicycles. But I learned that the thing I was offering mattered more than I'd understood. These were kids who needed their bikes to get to school or to friends' houses. They were trusting me with something that affected their safety. That knowledge made me slower, more careful, less certain. It made me better.

This still bothers me sometimes, how easily I could have seriously hurt someone. But it also taught me something important about the difference between knowing something and doing something. Watching a video and understanding the principle is one level. Actually building skill takes repetition and attention and humility. It takes accepting that you're going to make mistakes, and that other people's welfare depends on you catching them before they do.

I still fix bikes for neighborhood kids. I've fixed maybe forty of them now, and I've gotten much better. I still mess up sometimes—a cable routing that seemed right but wasn't, a bolt not tightened quite firmly enough. But I catch those things now because I'm looking for them. I'm slower. I'm more aware. I think that's what real accomplishment actually looks like, not the confident moment at fourteen, but the sustained attention that comes after you've learned what confidence can cost.
