I started writing the first line for them. That's it. That's the whole thing.

Let me back up. Tuesdays and Thursdays, room 114, the math lab, which is a converted storage room with one window that doesn't open and a poster of a cat hanging from a branch. I've been there since sophomore year. Mostly Algebra I kids, mostly sophomores and juniors who took it, failed it, and are taking it again, which is a specific kind of tired that I didn't understand until I'd been doing this a while.

The first semester I was terrible at it. Not at math. At the other part. A kid named Ruben would sit down with a worksheet on factoring and just look at it, and I would explain factoring, clearly, correctly, using the same words Mrs. Achebe used, and he would nod, and then he'd go home and do nothing. Every time. I thought he wasn't listening.

He was listening fine. The problem was that when I left the table he had a blank page and no idea what a person is supposed to physically do first. Not conceptually. Physically. Where does the pencil go.

So one Thursday, out of frustration honestly, I took his pencil and wrote x² + 7x + 12 and then underneath it I wrote a set of empty parentheses. ( )( ). Nothing else. And I said, okay, go. And he did.

That became the thing. Every kid, every session, I write the first line and then I stop and I don't help until they ask. Just the first line. The problem restated, or the equation copied over with a box drawn around what you're solving for, or two empty parentheses, or on word problems, just the words "let x =" and then a blank.

It sounds small because it is small. It took me about four seconds per problem. But something happens when the page isn't blank anymore. Ruben passed with a C+. He is now in Algebra II and he texts me sometimes to complain about it, which I consider a win.

I've watched this work on maybe forty kids now. Not all of them. There was a girl last year, Priya, who genuinely did not know her multiplication tables and no amount of first lines was going to fix that, and we spent six weeks on times tables and both of us hated it and she still got a D. So it's not magic. I want to be straight about that.

But the pattern holds more than it doesn't. Almost nobody in that room was failing because they couldn't do math. They were failing because starting is expensive and they'd been paying that cost alone, forty problems a night, and had quit paying it. My job turned out not to be explaining. It was going first.

Here's the part that changed how I think about myself. I always assumed I was good at math and that's why I could help. That's not it. I'm good at math because for eight years my dad sat at the kitchen table and did the first line of my homework with me and I never once noticed he was doing it. He'd say "okay, what are they even asking" and write it down. I thought that was just talking. I found this out last spring, when I described the math lab to him and he laughed and said where do you think you got that.

I don't know what I'm going to study. I know I'm going to keep sitting down next to people and writing the first line.
