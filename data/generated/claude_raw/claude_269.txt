I am not a natural with animals, which is an inconvenient thing to discover only after signing up for eight months of Saturday mornings at Fairview Animal Shelter. My leash technique is bad. I get tangled in it. Cats sense something in me that makes them go rigid and refuse to be held, and the first time I tried to clean a kennel I left the gate unlatched and a beagle named Pepper trotted calmly past three volunteers and out into the parking lot before anyone noticed. I cried in the supply closet that day, certain I would be quietly uninvited from ever coming back.

I wasn't. The woman who runs the shelter's volunteer program, Marisol, found me among the mop buckets, handed me a paper towel, and said, "You're on Saturday intake with me from now on." I assumed this was a demotion, a way of keeping the girl who loses dogs away from the dogs. It took me weeks to understand it was the opposite. Intake is where the newest, most frightened animals come in, and Marisol wanted someone there every single week, not because I was good at it, but because I kept showing up.

So I did. Every Saturday for the rest of that year, I sat on the concrete floor of the intake room with whatever animal had just arrived, usually terrified, sometimes matted or limping or too thin, and I did nothing more skillful than stay still and let them get used to a person being nearby. I was still bad at the parts that required competence. I still couldn't read a dog's body language the way the other volunteers could, still got told, gently, to step back from a growling shepherd I'd misjudged as merely shy. But I was there every week, on the same patch of floor, and somewhere in that repetition I stopped thinking of it as something I was failing at and started thinking of it as something I was simply doing.

What surprises me now, looking back, is how much Marisol's decision to keep me shaped what I understand about being useful. She never once told me I was good at the work. She corrected me constantly, without softening it, in a way that made it clear she took me seriously enough to bother. What she gave me instead of praise was persistence in me — she kept scheduling me, kept trusting the floor to someone who had let a beagle escape, kept treating my presence as worth something even when my skill wasn't. I didn't understand until much later that this was a form of generosity, maybe a rarer one than encouragement: the decision to keep counting on someone who hasn't proven they deserve it yet.

I think about this most on the mornings when I don't want to go, when I have a test Monday and my hands are still not steady with a leash. I go anyway, because someone once decided that showing up mattered more than doing it well, and treated me accordingly for long enough that I started to believe it myself. I am, I should say, still not particularly good at this. Just last month a cat bit me hard enough to draw blood and I still don't trust myself around anything larger than a beagle. But I have sat on that concrete floor almost every Saturday for two years now, and the animals that come through intake terrified usually leave the room a little less so, and I've come to understand that Marisol's stubborn faith in my ordinary, repeated presence is the closest thing to a gift I've been given that I didn't ask for and didn't expect to need.
