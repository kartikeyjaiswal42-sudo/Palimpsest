I taught myself to make and sell jewellery, and what I actually taught myself, it turned out, was pricing, and that took me nearly two years and one bad afternoon to learn.

It started with wire. My aunt gave me a spool of copper wire and a pair of pliers for my fourteenth birthday because I had liked a bracelet of hers, and for about four months I made things nobody would have paid for. Loops that were not round. Ends that scratched. I got better the way anyone gets better at a physical craft, which is by doing it while watching your hands, four hundred times, until the wrist knows something the head does not.

By the summer I was fifteen I had an online shop with eleven listings and a name I am not going to write down here because it is embarrassing. The photographs were taken on my bedroom floor on a piece of white poster board with the window on the left, and I taught myself that too, from a woman on YouTube who sold ceramics and said one true thing that has stayed with me: the customer is not buying the object, they are buying the photograph, so the photograph has to be honest or you will spend your life on refunds.

I sold sixty-two pieces in that first year. I was thrilled about it. I told everyone the number.

The bad afternoon was in October, and it was not a customer's fault. A woman ordered four pairs of the same earrings for her sisters, which was the largest order I had ever had, and I said yes immediately and I stayed up two nights making them. When I finished I did the thing I had never done properly, which was add it all up. Wire, ear wires, the beads which were the expensive part, the packaging, the little cards I had printed, the postage. Then I did something I had genuinely never done in two years: I counted the hours.

Nine hours. Four hundred and eighty rupees of materials. I had charged her one thousand two hundred.

So I had made seven hundred and twenty rupees for nine hours of skilled work, and I had been so pleased about the order that I had not noticed I was working for less than the boy who fills bags at the shop below our flat.

I sat on my floor with the calculator open and I changed my mind about the whole enterprise. Not about making things. About what I had been doing, which I had been calling a business and which was actually a very elaborate way of giving away my time while feeling successful. Sixty-two pieces sounded enormous. Sixty-two pieces at that margin was, over a year, less than nothing, because I had not counted the time and the moment you count the time the number goes negative.

What I did was raise everything by about eighty percent, which took me two weeks to work up to and which I was certain would end it. Sales dropped for a month. Then they came back, not all the way, but back — and the people who came back were different people. They asked questions. One of them asked whether I could make a version in silver and whether I would charge more for the design work, and I remember staring at that message because nobody had ever suggested that the thinking was a thing you could bill for.

Teaching myself without a teacher meant I learned the hand skills fast and the invisible skills two years late, and I think that is the actual shape of self-teaching. You learn what the videos are about. Nobody makes a video about the thing that would have saved you, because the thing that would have saved you is boring and involves arithmetic.

I still make them. I have a spreadsheet now with a column for minutes. My aunt thinks the spreadsheet is very funny. She has not seen the version from before.
