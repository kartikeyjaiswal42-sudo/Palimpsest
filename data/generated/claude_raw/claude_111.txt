The object is a laminated fingerspelling card, the kind with the alphabet printed in little hand diagrams down both sides, edges gone soft and rounded from two years in my backpack. A classmate named Wren gave it to me the second week of freshman year, not as a gift exactly, more as a tool, the way you'd hand someone a wrench if you wanted their help with something specific.

Wren is deaf and had transferred into my school that fall with an interpreter who came for core classes but not for lunch, not for the hallway between periods, not for the ten minutes before the bell when everyone else was talking over each other about nothing. I noticed her sitting alone at a table near the vending machines for about a week before I did anything about it, mostly because I didn't know what to do that wouldn't feel like an intrusion. Eventually I just sat down, pulled out my phone, and typed "hi, can I sit here." She smiled, typed back "sure," and then, after a pause, pulled that card out of her own bag and slid it across the table.

I learned the alphabet from that card in about two weeks, spelling out words letter by letter at lunch, painfully slow, Wren correcting my hand shapes with more patience than I deserved given how often I got J and Z backwards. Fingerspelling everything took forever, but it meant we could talk without her interpreter, without my phone, without anyone doing the work of translating us to each other. By October I'd learned maybe forty actual signs beyond the alphabet. By December I could follow most of a real conversation if she signed slowly and didn't mind repeating herself.

The card itself became something between a cheat sheet and a keepsake. I stopped needing to look at it regularly by winter break, but I kept carrying it anyway, and at some point it stopped being mine to use and started being evidence of something — proof, if anyone asked, that this particular friendship had a beginning I could point to and hold in my hand.

In the spring, Wren gave me a sign name, which I understand now is not something you're supposed to ask for or give yourself; it has to come from someone in the Deaf community who knows you well enough to choose one. Mine is a W-shape traced near my temple, a nod to my last name, and she signed it to me for the first time on the same bench where she'd first handed me the card, without any real ceremony, just a small correction to how I'd been signing my own name up to that point, replaced with something that was actually mine.

I still have the card, though it's been retired from active duty for a while now, held together at one corner with a strip of clear tape I put there myself after it started to tear. I keep it in the drawer with the few other objects I can't quite explain to people, the ones that don't look like much until you know what they were standing in for. It reminds me that learning a language for someone is not really the point of learning it — the point is what becomes possible once you can talk to a specific person without anything standing between you, no phone, no interpreter, no vending machine table she has to sit at alone while everyone else's conversation moves too fast to include her.
