Room 9 is not a nice room. It is the small one off the maths corridor with a whiteboard that keeps a ghost of everything ever written on it and a radiator that comes on in June. Tuesday and Thursday, half three to half four, I sit in there with between two and five students from Year Nine and Ten who are failing maths, and that is the place where I feel most like myself.

It was not supposed to be that. I need to say that clearly, because the version where I have always wanted to teach is not true.

I started because I needed something for the personal statement. That is the entire reason. Mrs Kaur put a sign-up sheet on the noticeboard for peer tutors and I wrote my name on it in October thinking it would be an hour a week of feeling clever in front of people who were worse at something than me. I am not proud of that sentence but it is what I thought.

And the first term was awful. Not dramatic — just awful in the way that a thing is awful when you are bad at it and cannot see why. I would explain fractions. I would explain fractions again, more slowly, in the same words, which is the single most useless thing a person can do. I had a boy called Ryan who would go completely still when I started talking, and I read that as not caring, and I got quietly annoyed with him for weeks.

The moment I changed my mind was in January and it was about ten seconds long.

I had asked Ryan to do something with negative numbers and he had done it wrong, and instead of explaining I asked him — mostly out of tiredness — how he'd got that. And he told me. He had a whole method. It was internally consistent. It was wrong in exactly one place, near the beginning, and everything after that was correct reasoning built on a broken step. He had not been failing to think. He had been thinking hard, the whole time, in a direction nobody had checked.

I remember feeling something reorganise itself. I had assumed that failing maths meant an absence — an empty space where understanding should be, which I could fill by pouring in explanation. It is not an absence. It is a structure. There is always a structure, and it is doing its best, and it is usually broken somewhere small and load-bearing.

After that the job became interesting in a way it had not been. Not rewarding, exactly. Interesting. Because now the work was diagnostic: get them talking, find the one wrong beam, and everything above it fixes itself faster than you would believe. Ryan got a five in the summer. I would like to claim that but honestly I think most of it was that somebody finally asked him what he was doing rather than telling him what to do.

So Room 9. Bad chairs, terrible radiator. I feel most myself there because it is the only place in my week where I am not performing understanding, I am investigating somebody else's. Nobody is marking me. There is no correct method to demonstrate. There is just a person and a wrong answer and the question of where the wrong answer came from, and I find that I could do that for a very long time without getting bored.

I came for the personal statement. I stayed because January.
