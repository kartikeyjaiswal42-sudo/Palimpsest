The first time I saw the lighting board for the school play, I thought it looked like something from another world. All those switches and dials, the thick cables running along the ceiling, the way the light technician moved through it all with such certainty. I was in tenth grade, and I'd volunteered for the job because I needed community service hours, not because I actually wanted to do it. I figured I'd learn the basics, show up on performance nights, push some buttons.

By the third rehearsal, I realized I'd been completely wrong about what this job meant. The lighting board wasn't just equipment you operated. It was a language the director used to tell the story. A single cue could make the audience feel something entirely different about what they were watching. When the actor arrived at the crucial moment in the second act, if the light came up even a half-second late, it ruined the whole impact. If it came up too bright, it killed the intimacy.

I remember the moment I changed my mind about the whole thing. We were running through a scene where the main character realizes something devastating about her family. The director had gone over this moment with me maybe five times. The lights needed to dim slowly, very slowly, while staying warm. Not cold, not dramatic, just warm and human and sad. I'd been thinking about it technically, like checking off a task. But then I actually watched the actor's face as the light changed around her, and I understood what it was really supposed to do. It was supposed to make the audience feel what she was feeling.

That's when I stopped thinking of it as a favor I was doing to get my hours done.

The work became incredibly demanding after that. The show was complex, with forty-seven lighting cues across two acts. I had to memorize every one, understand the exact timing, know which combinations of channels would create which effects. I started asking the director questions during rehearsals instead of just showing up to run cues. Why does this moment need to be brighter? Why is this transition happening over two seconds instead of five? She never made me feel stupid for asking.

There were nights I messed up. During dress rehearsal, I missed a transition in the third scene and had to scramble to catch up without the director stopping everything. I sat there during intermission feeling absolutely sick about it, convinced I'd ruined everything. But then the technical director came by and said it was fine, most people wouldn't have noticed, and we just needed to clean it up for the next run. The show went on.

Opening night, I was more nervous than the actors probably were. I sat in the dark booth with my script and my cue marks and my muscle memory of forty-seven moments when the stage needed to transform. I thought about how many things could go wrong, but I also thought about how if I did this right, I was helping to create something that mattered to a lot of people. Families were going to come watch. People were going to feel things.

When the final light cue came in the last act, and the stage went dark on exactly the right beat, and the applause started, I felt something I hadn't expected. It wasn't just relief. It was something closer to pride, or maybe accomplishment, though those words seem too small for what I actually felt. I'd been part of creating an experience for hundreds of people. I'd done that with my attention and my effort and my willingness to care about details that most people would never even notice.

I'm still doing lighting for the school productions. I'm the head technician now, and I help train new people who come in wanting community service hours. I always tell them what I wish someone had told me on my first day: this job sounds like it's about pushing buttons, but it's actually about seeing how light can change what people feel. Once you understand that, you can't do it any other way.
