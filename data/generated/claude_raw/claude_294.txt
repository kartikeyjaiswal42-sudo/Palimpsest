I've moved six times before I turned seventeen. My parents' job situation meant schools in three different countries and four different cities. Most people ask if it was hard, and the answer is that it was normal because I never knew anything else. What's harder to explain is what I started doing during these moves, something so small it barely counts, but something that has turned out to be the truest thing about who I am.

I keep the first drawing made at each school. Not the best drawing, not the one I'm proud of—the very first one, usually something quickly done in art class or a notebook, crude and unrefined. From my first school in London I have a pencil sketch of a playground. From the school in Singapore I have a watercolor landscape painted in the first week. From the school in Mumbai I have a charcoal study of my classroom. From each transition, one drawing.

I didn't plan this as some kind of project. It started with the drawing from London, which I kept because I didn't know I was leaving until suddenly I was packing. I found it at the bottom of a folder and something made me save it instead of throwing it away. When I moved to Singapore, I realized I was looking for the equivalent—that first artifact of being somewhere new. I started collecting without naming it as a collection.

What these drawings taught me is how much the first moment of arrival matters. Each one captures not the place but my relationship to the place at the instant I arrived in it. The London drawing is careful and precise because I was nervous. The Singapore one is loose and experimental because I was trying too hard to seem fine. The Mumbai one is dense with detail because I was paying attention in a way that I didn't in other places. Over time, I started to notice this before I finished the drawing—that I was drawing from a particular place of arrival, not from skill.

This habit became something bigger when I realized what it was actually doing for me. Moving is something that happens to you, something you don't choose. But collecting these drawings was something I chose. It meant I was taking ownership of the transition. Instead of just enduring the move, I was marking it. I was saying: this is where I arrived, this is what I saw, this is how I felt about being somewhere new.

By the time I moved to my fifth school, I was doing it deliberately. I would arrive and spend the first week looking for that first drawing. It became my anchor. On days when everything felt overwhelming or unfamiliar, I would go back to that drawing and remember that I had been new before. I had moved before. The panic of not knowing anyone, the weight of being the new person, these were things I had survived. The drawing was proof.

Now I have a collection of six. I keep them in a folder in my backpack. They're not good drawings, most of them. They're not impressive or technically skilled. But they're incredibly important to me because they represent something I've learned from being displaced repeatedly. I've learned how to arrive. I've learned that the new place, the strange school, the unfamiliar city—these aren't things that have happened to me. They're things that I have practiced handling, that I've gotten good at, that I carry evidence of surviving.

This matters to me for university because moving between schools has taught me something that doesn't show up on a transcript. I know how to adapt. I know how to find my place when there isn't an obvious place for me. I know how to look around a new environment and find something to anchor myself to, someone to befriend, a class or activity that makes sense. Most of all, I know that the arriving is a skill, and it's one I'm good at.
