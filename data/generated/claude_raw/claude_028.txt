For the first eleven weeks, the only person listening to Radio Fenn was my sister Orla, in her bedroom, forty feet from the studio, on purpose, so that the number would not be zero.

The station lives in what used to be a stock cupboard off the sixth-form corridor. I found it in Year Ten looking for a place to eat lunch away from people, and it had a broken projector and — this is the part I could not get over — a proper acoustic ceiling, because it had been a music practice room in the eighties. I asked Mr Halloran what would happen if I put a microphone in there. He said write it down and put it in my pigeonhole, which I have since learned is what teachers say to make a thing go away, and which did not work on me.

What I had was two hundred and forty pounds from a summer of shelf-stacking, a second-hand mixer off eBay with one dead channel, and a plan to stream it over the school's network at lunchtimes. The first broadcast was on a Tuesday in November. I talked for twenty-two minutes about nothing in particular and played four songs, and the listener count in the corner of the screen said one. It said one the next day too. It said one for most of a term.

I did it every weekday anyway. I want to be honest that this was not perseverance in any noble sense; it was that the cupboard was the first place in that school where I had felt entirely all right, and I would have gone in there and talked to nobody regardless of whether it was being broadcast.

It grew slowly and then not slowly. A teacher mentioned it. Two girls in Year Nine asked to do a Thursday slot about football. By the second year we had eleven presenters, a scheduling spreadsheet, and a Friday show where people submitted anonymous questions and we tried to answer them badly. The listener number is a few hundred now, which is not many, except that it is a few hundred people who chose it.

Orla and I do not agree about what any of this is.

She is twenty-two and she has known me longer than anyone, and her position, put plainly, is that the radio station is somewhere I go instead of being with people. She has said versions of this since the beginning. That I will talk for forty minutes into a microphone about my grandfather's allotment and then not manage four sentences at a family lunch. That the reason I love the cupboard is the reason she worries about it: nobody can interrupt me, nobody can look at me, and I have complete control over when it stops. She thinks I built a machine for being heard without being met.

The worst part of the argument, from my side, is that the first half of it is true. I do find broadcasting easier than talking. Of course I do. And she is not being unkind when she says it — she stayed in her bedroom listening to a dead-air stream for eleven weeks so that a boy she loves would not have a zero on his screen. That is not the behaviour of someone attacking me.

But I think she has the direction wrong, and I have never been able to say why in a way that lands. The station is not where I hide from people; it is the reason I know any of them. I have had a conversation with almost every person who has ever presented on it, because somebody has to teach them how to use the desk, and there is no way to teach somebody the desk without sitting nine inches away from them for an hour. Two of my closest friends arrived that way. When I imagine the version of me without the cupboard, he is not at a party. He is at the back of a common room, alone, and nobody knows he has anything to say.

We had it out properly in August, in her car, outside our nan's house. It ended with her saying "I just don't want you talking to a room," and me saying "It isn't a room," and both of us leaving it there.

She still listens most days. She sends me a message afterwards, usually one word. She has never missed a Friday.
