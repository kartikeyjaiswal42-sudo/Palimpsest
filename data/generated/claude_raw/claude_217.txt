The court behind the Fenwick Street rec center didn't have nets for as long as I played there, just bare rims, and the concrete had a crack running diagonally through the top of the key that everybody knew to dribble around without thinking about it. I learned that crack before I learned most things about geometry. We called it Fenwick, never "the rec center court," like it was its own place separate from the building it belonged to, and in a lot of ways it was.

It ran on rules nobody wrote down. Winners stay, next team calls next, you call your own fouls and if you cheated on a call everyone knew it and you got frozen out for a week or two, no announcement, just nobody passing you the ball when you showed up. Dell, who was somewhere past sixty and hadn't played a real game in years, sat on the bench by the fence most afternoons and functioned as the court's memory — who owed who a game, whose little brother was old enough to run with the group now, whose temper needed watching. I was maybe twelve when I started going, and by fifteen I was one of the people other kids' parents recognized as safe to leave their sons with.

The city announced it was tearing Fenwick down the spring I turned sixteen, to put in a permanent lot for the clinic next door. There was a public meeting about it, and because I'd been going there for years and the rec center staff knew me, someone asked if I wanted to speak. I said yes. And then I stood up in front of maybe thirty people and said the court "wasn't really being used for much anyway," that a lot of us just went to the courts over at Prescott now, which was true some weeks and not true most of them, and that the parking would probably help more people than the court did.

I don't fully know why I said it. Part of it was that I was annoyed — there'd been noise complaints that year, someone had called the police twice on a Tuesday night run that got loud, and I was tired of the court being treated like a problem instead of a place. I think some small, dumb part of me wanted to seem reasonable in front of adults, wanted to be the kid who understood tradeoffs instead of the kid who was attached to a cracked slab of concrete. So I gave it away, basically. Nobody else spoke against the demolition. It happened that August.

The lot took eighteen months to get approved and, as far as I know, still hasn't been built. The space has been fenced off and empty since the summer they knocked the court down, weeds coming up through the same crack I used to dribble around.

I think about that meeting more than almost anything else from high school. Not because Fenwick would have survived if I'd fought for it — maybe it wouldn't have, the city had already decided — but because I had a chance to say what that place actually was to the people who used it, and instead I made it smaller than it was so I wouldn't have to sound sentimental. Dell doesn't sit anywhere anymore. I still don't know where half those guys play.
