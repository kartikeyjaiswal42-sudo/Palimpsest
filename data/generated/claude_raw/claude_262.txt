Everyone got their first phone in sixth grade except me. I got mine in seventh grade, which is not a long time but it felt eternal when you were the only kid in your friend group without one.

I think my parents thought they were protecting me from something. A bad influence, maybe. Predators. Addiction. There was probably a parenting blog somewhere that said kids shouldn't have phones until they were older and my mom found it and made this decision and stuck to it even when I begged. She said everyone else's parents were making a mistake and she wasn't going to do that to me.

By the time I got to seventh grade, the friendship groups had basically formed based on group chats. People made plans in Discord and on iMessage and I found out about things after the fact. I'd overhear people talking about jokes from their group chat and I had no context. My best friend would be texting with five other people and I'd be sitting there with nothing to do. They weren't trying to exclude me. It was just that without a phone, I wasn't really part of the infrastructure of how friendships worked anymore.

I felt like I was living in a different decade. Everyone around me was having these subtle conversations constantly and I was checked out of all of it. I thought I was fine with it at first. I told myself I was more independent, more present, less addicted to technology. I judged everyone else for needing their phones. I was very smug about the whole thing.

But honestly? I was miserable. I was lonely. I felt like I was watching my friends have a life that I wasn't invited to. It wasn't even that they were doing anything particularly exciting. They were sending stupid memes and making plans and saying dumb things that aren't worth remembering. But that's what friendship was at that age—it was the accumulation of stupid small moments. And I was missing all of them.

When I finally got a phone, nothing really changed with my friends. We were still the same people. But I could finally access the random moments that made up friendship. And I realized how much I'd been judging everyone for something that I didn't actually understand until I was part of it.

What I got wrong was thinking that being above technology was the same as being above the need for connection. My parents were trying to protect me, probably, but they were also accidentally isolating me. I learned that there's a difference between being intentional about technology use and just refusing to participate in the way everyone else is participating. The first one is about making choices. The second one is about opting out while pretending you're making a choice.

I'm grateful my parents eventually let me get a phone. And I use it way too much, probably. I get it. But I also think that year without one taught me a lesson that backfired. It taught me that I need connection more than I need independence. That sometimes fitting in isn't the same as selling out. That being different for the sake of being different is just another kind of trying too hard.
