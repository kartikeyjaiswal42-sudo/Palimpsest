My cousin Bhavna asked me to plan her wedding logistics eight months before the date, which sounds like an honor until you understand that what she meant was: seating charts for two hundred and forty guests, coordinating three caterers because half the family is vegetarian and half isn't and neither side would compromise, and managing a group chat of eleven aunts who each believed they had final say over the flowers. I said yes because I love her, and because I am, apparently, the family's designated organized person, a reputation I built by color-coding a group project in ninth grade and have never lived down.

For the first four months I ran it like an engineering problem. I built a spreadsheet with conditional formatting. I made a decision tree for the seating chart to avoid putting my uncle Rajan anywhere near his brother-in-law, with whom he has not spoken since a property dispute in 2019. I was, if I'm honest, a little proud of how well I was handling it, proud in the specific way that comes from being trusted with something and not wanting to be found lacking.

The trouble started over the guest list itself, which I thought was settled and Bhavna kept revising, adding people, moving people from the "definitely" to the "maybe" column and back, until I lost track of which version was current. I got frustrated. I told her, more sharply than I meant to, that if she kept changing her mind I couldn't guarantee the caterers would have accurate numbers, and that at some point indecision becomes its own kind of decision, one that costs real money. She got quiet, and then she said something I didn't expect: that this wasn't a spreadsheet to her, it was people she'd known her whole life, and that every name I wanted her to lock in permanently represented a relationship she was actually still thinking through, not a logistics problem to be optimized.

I was ready to argue that logistics don't stop needing to be right just because feelings are involved. I still believe that, honestly. Numbers have to be accurate or someone doesn't get a chair. But something in how she said it made me stop and actually picture what I'd been treating as noise in my system: her grandmother's old friend who might not make the trip, an estranged school friend Bhavna wasn't sure she wanted to invite yet, a family that had wronged hers years ago but who she couldn't bring herself to fully exclude. Those weren't rows I could clean up. They were her trying to decide, in real time, who she wanted standing near her on the most consequential day of her adult life.

I changed my mind about what I was actually doing for her. Not managing a headcount. Holding space for someone to be uncertain without being punished for it by a spreadsheet. I kept the caterers updated with rolling estimates instead of demanding a final number too early, and I stopped treating every revision as a failure of planning.

The wedding happened in October, chaotic in exactly the ways I'd tried to prevent, and beautiful anyway. Bhavna cried during her vows and then laughed at herself for crying. I sat two rows back with my spreadsheet finally retired, and I understood, watching her, that some things you organize and some things you simply have to let stay a little unresolved until the last possible moment, and that knowing the difference matters more than any color-coded chart ever could.
