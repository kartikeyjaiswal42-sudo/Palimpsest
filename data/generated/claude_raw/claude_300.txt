I trained for the national mathematics olympiad for two years and eleven months, and I did not qualify, and for a long time the only sentence I could say about it was that one.

The training was not glamorous. It was a room above a coaching centre in Kalyani Nagar with a ceiling fan that clicked, seven of us and a teacher called Ranade Sir who had qualified for the international team in 1994 and had, in the way of such people, never entirely come back down. Wednesdays and Saturdays and all of Sunday. Functional equations, then inequalities, then a whole winter of projective geometry that I loved more than anything I have ever studied. I did four hundred problems out of a photocopied Russian book with a cracked spine. I have the notebooks. Six of them, and the handwriting gets smaller as they go on, which I think means something.

In the second round I got two out of six. The cutoff was four.

What I want to describe is not the failure but a Tuesday about five months afterward, because that is where the actual event of my adolescence occurred, and it did not look like anything.

I had stopped doing mathematics entirely. Not dramatically — I just didn't open the books. I told people I had "moved on," which is a phrase we use for grief when we don't want to call it that. And then in April my cousin Ishani, who was in ninth standard and hated maths in the ordinary bored way, asked me why the angles in a triangle add to a hundred and eighty. Not how you prove it. Why it should be so.

I drew her the parallel line through the apex. I showed her the alternate angles falling into place along the base, the three of them lying flat in a straight line, which is the proof I have loved since I was eleven and which is, I think, the most beautiful small thing in the world. She said, oh. And then she said, but that only works because the lines stay the same distance apart, and if they didn't it would be different, wouldn't it.

She was fourteen and she had reinvented, in about nine seconds, the question that Gauss and Bolyai and Lobachevsky spent their lives on. She had no idea. She was asking about her homework.

I sat in that room with the fan clicking and I understood something that had been sitting in front of me for three years with a sheet over it: I had spent thirty-five months treating mathematics as a filter. Everything I did had been aimed at a cutoff. When I missed the cutoff, the subject had nothing left to be, so it became nothing, and I let it. But Ishani had walked into the same subject through a completely different door, without a score attached, and the thing on the other side of the door was still there. It had never been the olympiad's to give or to take away.

I began teaching her that summer, badly at first. Then two of her friends. By the time I was in twelfth standard there were nine of them, in my grandmother's front room on Sunday mornings, and I had learned that explaining a proof is a harder problem than the proof, and more interesting, because a proof has one correct form and an explanation has as many correct forms as there are people in the room.

I still can't do a functional equation under time pressure. I have made peace with an unusual amount of that. What I did not expect was to be grateful to the cutoff — not for the disappointment, which was ordinary and self-pitying and lasted about five months, but for the way it stripped the subject of its usefulness to me and left me having to decide whether I still wanted it. I did. I do. That turned out to be the only question, and I had been answering a different one for nearly three years.

Ishani got a ninety-four in her boards. She thinks that's the point of the story. I let her think so.
