I learned to translate for my parents by accident, one misunderstood appointment at a time. My mother's English was basic—survival English, acquired through years of watching television and talking to neighbors. My father's was worse. When they had doctor's appointments, they took me, and I sat between them like a bridge, carrying words back and forth.

The first time I made a real mistake, I was thirteen. My mother had been having migraines, and the doctor was asking detailed questions about the pain. Where exactly? How long? Did it come with other symptoms? I translated quickly, thinking I understood, but I confused the word for "sharp" and said her pain was "dull." It seemed like a small thing at the time. The doctor nodded and wrote something down. My mother didn't correct me, and I didn't think to check what I'd said.

She got prescribed the wrong medication. It didn't help. When she went back, the doctor looked confused. He asked more questions. This time my mother spoke slowly in her limited English, trying to make herself understood. The doctor listened carefully. She got different pills. They worked.

I realized I'd failed her. Not catastrophically, not in a way that caused permanent harm, but I'd failed. I was the bridge and I'd constructed it wrong. She'd trusted me to carry her words accurately, and I'd gotten lazy or careless or just not careful enough.

After that, I started learning seriously. Not from a teacher—I taught myself. I sat in waiting rooms and made myself translate everything correctly, asking my parents to repeat things so I could be sure. I read medical terms online at night. I wrote down words I didn't know. I asked my mother to use different descriptions of her symptoms and practiced translating all the variations.

What I learned was that translation isn't just knowing two languages. It's knowing the culture inside the words. When an American doctor asks "How are you feeling?" they want an answer about pain or weakness or specific symptoms. When my mother described her symptoms, she talked about them philosophically, about imbalance and disruption of her normal self. Those aren't the same thing. A literal translation would sound vague to a doctor trained to think in specific medical terminology.

I got better at bridging that gap. I learned to ask my parents probing questions that helped me understand what they were really experiencing, and then translate that into medical language the doctor would understand. I learned when to ask the doctor to repeat something, to be specific about what they meant. I learned to notice when my mother looked confused and to ask her, in Punjabi, if she understood what was being said to her.

But I still carry that first mistake. I know what it feels like to hold someone's health in your hands and not be sure you're holding it right. I know what it's like to realize, after the fact, that you got it wrong. Every time I translate now, even for small things, I feel that weight. I check myself. I ask for confirmation. I make sure.

It took me years to tell my mother about the migraine medication confusion. I expected her to be angry, or to lose confidence in me. Instead, she looked sad. She said she'd wondered about that visit, but she'd trusted me, so she didn't push back. She'd just assumed she was wrong about her own pain.

I learned something important in that moment, something I wouldn't have learned if I'd gotten it right the first time. Being the translator isn't just about knowing languages. It's about understanding that you're responsible for someone else's safety. That they're placing their trust in you to be accurate, to be careful, to care enough to get it right. It's a bigger responsibility than I realized when I first started doing it. And I think about it every time I see another bilingual kid in a waiting room, translating for their parents, and I wonder if they're making the same mistakes I did, or if they'll learn faster than I did to understand what accuracy actually costs.
