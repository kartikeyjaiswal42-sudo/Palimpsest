"He's not going in the second half," Coach Renner said, not looking at me, still writing on his clipboard like the decision had already been made somewhere I wasn't invited to.

I said Théo had to play. League rules were clear about it, minimum minutes for every registered kid, and besides it was a semifinal against a team we'd actually beaten once that fall, and benching our weakest defender for the whole second half felt like something I couldn't square with why either of us had signed up to coach nine-year-olds in the first place. Renner had coached this league for eleven years. I'd coached it for one. I still said it.

He put the clipboard down. Told me Théo had cried at practice the week before because another kid laughed when he missed a tackle, and that playing him forty-five straight minutes in a game this tight, in front of both sets of parents, wasn't fairness, it was cruelty with better paperwork. I said the rule existed for exactly this reason, so that coaches couldn't decide on a Saturday that some kid's feelings were less important than a trophy nobody would remember by October. He said I didn't know what it looked like yet, watching a kid you like get run over in front of everyone.

We didn't resolve it standing there. He played Théo the required minimum and not a second more, which technically followed the rule and violated everything I thought the rule was supposed to protect.

Théo went in for the last six minutes. The other team scored almost immediately off a play that ran straight at him, and I watched him not flinch, not look at the sideline, just get up and reset for the next play the way I'd seen him do at every practice all season. Nobody laughed this time. His own teammates were the ones clapping him back into position.

Afterward, in the parking lot, Renner didn't bring it up and neither did I. I watched Théo's dad lift him onto his shoulders anyway, both of them laughing about something I couldn't hear, and I kept turning over what I'd said in the second half, how sure I'd been that I understood what was owed to a nine-year-old better than a man who'd been doing this since before I could tie my own cleats.

I still think the rule matters. I've just stopped being certain I know, standing on a sideline at seventeen, what protecting a kid is actually supposed to look like from the inside of a game I'm not the one playing.
