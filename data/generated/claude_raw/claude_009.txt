"You're rounding down again," my mother said, not looking up from the invoices, and I said I wasn't, and she said I was, she'd watched me do it three times that week alone.

She wasn't wrong. When a customer's total came to something like $6.97 and they were fumbling in a coin purse for the last few cents, I'd hit a key and knock it to an even six. I'd been doing this since I was maybe fifteen, quietly, without telling anyone, and I thought I'd been careful about it. Apparently not careful enough.

"It adds up," she said. "You round down for Mrs. Alvarez, you round down for the Petrosian kid, you round down for whoever looks tired that day. That's real money, mija. That's real money that isn't in the drawer at the end of the night."

I told her it was pennies. She told me pennies were how a store like ours went under. And she wasn't being dramatic — I'd heard the actual numbers, the margins on a place that sells lottery tickets and off-brand cereal and phone chargers, the margins are thin enough that "pennies" is not actually a small word, it's a word that means something. I knew she was right in the way you know your parent is right about money, which is to say I knew it and resented knowing it at the same time.

I kept doing it anyway.

Not every time. I got smarter, or sneakier, depending on which of us you'd ask. I'd round down for the people I recognized as regulars going through something — the woman whose husband's car I once heard her mention had been repossessed, the boy who always paid in exact, grubby dollar bills he'd clearly counted twice before coming inside. I never announced it. I never made it a whole thing where the customer felt pitied, because that's its own kind of cruelty, making someone feel seen in their hard luck at a gas station register. I just hit six dollars instead of six ninety-seven and moved on to the next thing, casual, like it was a mistake I was too lazy to fix.

My mother caught me again in October. She didn't yell. She's not a yeller. She just stood there with the register tape in her hand, this long curl of paper with a highlighted line on it, and looked at me like she was trying to decide whether I was being generous or just bad at math.

"I raised you to be careful with money," she said. "This isn't careful."

"I know."

"Then why."

I didn't have a good answer that night. I said something about how it wasn't going to bankrupt us, one dollar here and there, and she said that's exactly what every person who bankrupts a small business tells themselves, right up until the year they don't make rent on the walk-in cooler. She wasn't entirely wrong about that either. I looked it up later. Small margins really do work like that, in aggregate, in a way that feels invisible until it isn't.

She started checking the register total against the receipt count more carefully after that, for a while. Not because she thought I was stealing — she never said that word, never came close to it — but because she wanted the number to make sense to her, and it stopped making sense when I was in it.

I don't round down as often now. I want to say I stopped, but that would be a small lie in a story about honesty, and I'd rather not tell one. What changed is smaller than that. I started doing the math first — actually doing it, in my head, before my hand went to the register — instead of just feeling my way into a decision and hoping nobody noticed. Sometimes the math still loses. Sometimes I still look at the total and look at the person and decide the four cents don't matter as much as whatever's happening on their face.

My mother knows, I think. She hasn't caught me in a while, but I don't think that's because I've stopped. I think it's because she's stopped checking as closely, which might mean she's decided this is a fight she's not going to win, or it might mean something else I haven't figured out yet.
