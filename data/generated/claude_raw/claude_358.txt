My grandmother taught me how to be patient with someone who is slowly leaving you, and she absolutely didn't mean to do it. She didn't mean to teach me anything because the dementia had already started taking the parts of her that knew how to teach or instruct anyone. But somehow I learned anyway, just by being around her and choosing how to respond to her confusion. And what I learned through this whole experience is that my grandfather and I have very different and conflicting opinions about what that learning actually means and how we should treat her.

When she first started forgetting, it was small. She'd ask the same question twice in one conversation. She'd lose track of where she'd put her glasses. My mom would gently reorient her, telling her the story again, finding the glasses again. It seemed patient and kind. My grandfather was less patient. He'd sigh and repeat himself. He'd get a little sharp with her, the way you do when you're tired of explaining something. I didn't think much about it.

By the time I was old enough to really notice it was getting worse, we were living in the age of internet articles about dementia. My mom read them and shared them with us. The one I remember most clearly was about using validation instead of correction. Instead of saying "No, Mom, that didn't happen," you were supposed to meet her in her reality. You were supposed to say "That must have felt scary" and just let her believe what she believed. It seemed kinder than insisting on facts she couldn't access.

I started practicing this approach with her. She'd tell me stories that weren't quite right, stories that didn't match what had actually happened, and instead of correcting her, I'd just listen quietly. I'd ask her questions about the version she remembered. I'd treat her reality as equally valid even when it wasn't factually accurate. And it worked. She seemed noticeably more comfortable. Less agitated and upset. I thought I was doing something genuinely generous and wise.

My grandfather hated it. He said I was enabling her. He said by not correcting her, I was letting her disappear further into confusion. He'd argue with my attempts at validation. He'd insist on facts. "No, Mother, that's not what happened. You're confusing it with another time." And then she'd get upset and confused and he'd get frustrated that she was upset.

We fought about it. I said he was being cruel. He said I was being naive and making things worse. I said he just didn't understand how dementia works. He said I was patronizing her. The fights weren't big but they were constant, this low-level disagreement about the most loving way to be with someone who's slowly vanishing.

I still think validation is the right approach. The research backs it up. Meeting people with dementia in their reality, rather than insisting they accept ours, actually reduces agitation and improves quality of life. I've read the studies. I understand the neurology. My approach was correct.

But what I'm less certain about now is whether being right about dementia care is the same as being right about love. My grandfather was insisting on facts because he was grieving. He was trying to hold onto the person she was by refusing to accept the person she was becoming. Every time he corrected her, he was saying "No, you're still you. You still know things. You still matter." Which is a kind of love too, even if it was the wrong strategy.

What I taught myself, by learning on my own instead of taking classes, was that there are different ways to love someone and they can be in direct conflict with each other. My way was gentler. His way was more true. Neither of us was really wrong, we were just loving her in different languages and neither of us knew how to help the other person understand our translation.

He died before she did. After his death, I wondered if he'd been right all along. If his insistence on facts was actually the braver thing—the refusal to let her go all the way under. But I still did it the way I did it. And I still think she was more comfortable with me than she was with him.

We never resolved it. We just both kept loving her in our separate ways until there wasn't anyone left to love.
