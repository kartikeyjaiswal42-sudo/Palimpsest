I believed India was my home in the way people believe facts. It was the place I had always been, where my family lived, where school was a comfortable routine I had learned to navigate without thinking. I didn't think of myself as someone with a home—home was just the air around me, invisible and constant. Then my father's company told us we were moving to Singapore, and I had to revise that belief, which turned out to be harder than I expected.

The news came in May, right in the middle of tenth grade. We would leave in July. I told my friends and their responses ranged from jealousy to sympathy, but nobody really understood what I felt, which was a kind of panic I couldn't name. Singapore was a real place on a map, a place I could find in my geography textbook, but it was also a theoretical place, not a place where I had memories.

I spent the first month there in a fog. The school was clean and organized in a way that made everything feel sterile. The students were nice in a polite way that felt distant. My accent, which I had never noticed before because everyone around me had always sounded the same, suddenly announced itself constantly. Teachers would ask me to repeat things. Classmates' heads would turn when I spoke. For the first time I could remember, I sounded like someone who didn't belong.

But something happened around month three. I was walking home from school—we lived close enough to walk, which surprised me because I expected Singapore to be different, but it was just a place where people walked and lived and complained about homework like anywhere else. A girl from my English class caught up with me and asked if I wanted to get food. Her name was Iris and she was from Hong Kong and had moved there last year. We went to a place that made noodles, and she told me that the weird thing about moving was that you don't get to choose when you stop feeling like a visitor.

"You just wake up one day and you're not," she said, eating her noodles carefully to avoid dripping them on her uniform. "It's not a moment exactly. It's just the first day you don't think about how much you miss home."

I thought that was the most depressing thing I had ever heard, and I said so. Iris laughed and told me I was thinking about it wrong. "You don't stop missing it," she said. "You just learn that your brain isn't big enough to hold two places with the same amount of reality. So one of them gets smaller. That doesn't mean it's not real. It just means you're not living in it anymore."

That was in October. By the time winter break came around, I noticed something strange. When I video-called my friends in Delhi, I could barely imagine the places they were calling from. The streets I had walked a thousand times looked flat and distant on my screen. And when I looked at where I was living—the apartment, the park near school, even the noodle place where I had first started to feel maybe okay—it had become realer than the place I used to be. I wasn't sure if that made me a traitor to my own past or just a person living forward into my own life.

I still miss Delhi. But I stopped believing it was the only place I could belong. Home turned out to be more portable than I thought.
