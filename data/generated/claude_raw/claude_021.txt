The object is a small spiral notebook, the kind you buy in a three-pack from the drugstore, its cover gone soft and rounded at the corners from living in my backpack for two years. Inside it, in my handwriting, is a running list of medical words translated into Vietnamese, next to my mother's handwriting, which corrects me sometimes when I get the tone wrong. Metformin. Insulin resistance. A referral. The word for "biopsy," which I had to learn the hard way, standing in a fluorescent hallway at age fourteen, translating a doctor's sentence to my father while trying to keep my own face from telling him what the sentence meant before I'd finished saying it.

I started going to my parents' appointments when I was eleven, not because anyone formally decided this was my role, but because I was the one who spoke English well enough and confidently enough, and my older brother had already left for college, and someone had to sit in the chair between the doctor and my mother and turn one language into the other, fast enough that neither side felt like they were waiting on a delay. Nobody trained me for this. I learned the vocabulary the way you learn anything under pressure, by getting it wrong once in front of people who needed it right.

The notebook started because I got a word wrong. My mother's doctor used the phrase "manageable but chronic" about her blood sugar, and I translated "chronic" using a word that in Vietnamese leans much closer to "permanent and worsening" than the doctor meant, and I watched my mother's face fold in on itself in the waiting room afterward, and I didn't understand why until she asked me, quietly, if she was going to get sicker no matter what she did. I'd frightened her with my own imprecision. That night I wrote "chronic" in the notebook with three different possible translations underneath it and a note to myself about which one to use in which context, and after that I just kept adding to it, every appointment, every new term, until it became this strange bilingual medical glossary that belongs to nobody but our family.

I am, in most of my life, a kid. I complain about homework and forget to unload the dishwasher and get embarrassed when my parents pick me up from things. But in that notebook, in those rooms, I am something else — I am the only bridge between what a doctor knows and what my parents are allowed to understand about their own bodies. That's a strange kind of community to belong to, if you can even call it a community. It's smaller than that word usually means. It's really just three of us, and a stack of paper, and a responsibility I didn't apply for and can't quit.

There's a particular kind of tiredness that comes from being needed this specifically. I don't say that to complain — I chose this, in the sense that I never once suggested we hire an interpreter, because interpreters cost money we don't reliably have, and because I think some part of me wanted to be the one who understood my parents' bodies better than they could explain them to a stranger themselves. But it means I've sat in rooms hearing things about my mother's health before she's fully processed them herself, translating bad news in real time while also being the daughter who has to keep living in the same house as that news that night.

The notebook is almost full now. I'll need a new one soon. I don't know exactly what I'll do with the old one — throw it away feels wrong, keep it feels strange too, like keeping a record of every hard afternoon in one place. For now it just sits in my backpack, soft-cornered, doing the only job it's ever had, which is making sure that when it matters most, my parents and their doctors are actually saying the same thing to each other.
