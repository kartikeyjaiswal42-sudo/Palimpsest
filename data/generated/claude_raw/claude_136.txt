There is a wall calendar in our kitchen that nobody has taken down. It is from four years ago. The months are printed in my first language, and under each date there are small squares where my mother used to write what needed doing, in that language, in her handwriting, which slants.

That was the year we stopped speaking it at home.

I want to be careful here, because I do not fully know why it happened, and I have never been able to point to a day. There was no decision. Nobody sat anyone down. My brother was starting school and struggling, and my parents were told, by somebody, that it would help if he heard more English at home. I think that was the start of it. But I am reconstructing. What I remember is that at some point in that year the language stopped being the one the house was made of and became the one that came out when someone was tired, or angry, or on the phone to my grandmother.

The calendar stopped being written on in about March. After that the squares are empty. The months keep going, printed, in a language that was still on the wall but not really in the room.

I am supposed to be describing a place where I feel most myself. I have thought about this for a long time and the honest answer is that I do not have one, and that the reason I do not have one has something to do with that calendar. Which is a difficult thing to write in an essay that is meant to end well.

In English I am fluent and I am funny and I am, I think, slightly a different person — quicker, more sarcastic, more likely to say something to fill a silence. In my first language I am about eleven years old. My vocabulary stopped growing the year the calendar stopped. I can talk about food and weather and family. I cannot talk about what I want to do with my life, or about how I feel about anything complicated, because I never learned those words as an adult would need them and I did not know, at eleven, that I was going to need them.

So there is no room where all of me is available. There are two rooms and I am partial in both.

The place I come closest is the kitchen, with the calendar in it, when my mother is cooking and my grandmother is on speakerphone. In that specific arrangement the language comes back for maybe forty minutes. It is not good. I make mistakes and my grandmother corrects them and my mother laughs at both of us. But it is the only situation where the two halves of the house are in the same air at the same time, and I am not translating, I am just in it.

I have thought about whether I should be angry about this, and I have decided that I am not, or not exactly. My parents did what they were told would help my brother, and my brother is fine now, and he does not speak the language at all, which is a separate fact I do not know what to do with. He is thirteen. He calls my grandmother and says hello and then hands the phone over. She is very kind about it.

What I feel is more like the thing you feel when you notice a door in a house that has been painted over. Not grief. Just the awareness of a shape.

I have started trying to fix it, in a small and inconsistent way. I have an app, which is not much use. Better is that I have started calling my grandmother myself on Sunday evenings, without my mother in the room, so there is nobody to fall back to. Those calls are slow and a bit awkward and I run out of things I can say in about twenty minutes. But they are getting longer. Last month I described my chemistry teacher to her, which took a very long time and involved a lot of pointing at nothing, and she understood, and she said something back about a teacher she had, and I understood most of it.

I do not have a conclusion about this. The calendar is still on the wall. Nobody has taken it down and nobody has said why, and I think if I asked, my mother would just take it down, so I have not asked.

If I am most myself anywhere, it is on the phone on a Sunday, badly, in eleven-year-old sentences, getting slightly better.
