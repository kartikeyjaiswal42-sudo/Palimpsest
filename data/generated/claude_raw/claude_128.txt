My aunt Rosalind taught me chess when I was eight, on a set with a missing white bishop that we replaced with a thimble, and she is the person I have been arguing with about it ever since.

The argument started properly two years ago, when I told her I was going to start a chess club at my school. She asked how many people at my school played chess. I said, honestly, none that I knew of. She said then what you are starting is not a chess club, and I said that was precisely the point, and we have not got past that exchange in twenty-two months.

Her belief — and it is a belief, held seriously, by a woman who was county under-18 champion in 1989 and still plays league on Thursdays — is that chess is not a hobby you distribute. It is a thing you fall into. Nobody talked her into it; she found a book at eleven and disappeared for a decade. She thinks clubs founded by enthusiasts for the benefit of non-enthusiasts produce a soft, social, tepid version of the game, where nobody studies, nobody loses badly enough to learn anything, and the whole thing dissolves when the founder leaves. She has watched it happen at three schools. "You will teach forty people to move the pieces," she said, "and none of them will ever play chess."

I thought she was wrong for a reason I could not articulate at the time and can now. Her account of how people find things assumes the book is on the shelf. She found chess at eleven because there was a chess book in a house where a chess book could plausibly be. There is no chess book in most of the houses I know. Our school library has one, published in 1978, on obscure endgame studies, which is roughly like introducing someone to music via a treatise on the fugue. The absence of chess players at my school was not evidence that nobody there would love chess. It was evidence that nobody there had been given the chance to find out.

So I started it, and I did it in a way that I knew she would disapprove of. No ladder, no ratings, no clock for the first term. I bought fourteen sets from a wholesaler with money from the student fund and left them out at lunch in the humanities corridor with a sign saying you do not need to know how. I taught the moves individually, badly, twelve or fifteen times over. We played a lot of games that were not chess: four-player nonsense, a variant where captured pieces come back, one afternoon that was mostly building towers out of rooks.

By the second term we had thirty-one people on a list and about twelve who came every week.

Here is what I have to concede to her. Of those twelve, I would say two actually play chess in the sense she means it. One is a Year 9 called Devan who is now better than I am and who studies openings on his phone on the bus, and one is a girl in my year who took up correspondence chess and will not tell anyone her rating. The other ten come for the room. They play a game and a half in an hour and talk the whole time and one of them still hangs his queen most weeks. Rosalind, if she saw it, would say: exactly.

But I have stopped thinking that the number two is a failure. Before the club there was a zero. Devan did not exist as a chess player in 2023 and would not have existed in 2033 either, because there was no shelf and no book. Two out of thirty-one is a terrible conversion rate and an infinite improvement.

What I actually changed my mind about, in the middle of all this, was subtler and it is the thing I would want to argue with her about now if she would let me. I began believing that the ten who chat and hang their queens were the cost of finding the two. I have come round to thinking they might be the point, or at least a point of equal size. I do not think chess is doing anything mystical for them. But there is a room, at lunch, on a Wednesday, where the entry requirement is nothing and the activity gives your hands something to do while you talk, and about a third of the people who come to it are people who were previously eating lunch alone.

Rosalind still says I am running a youth club with boards in it. She may be right about what it is. We disagree about whether that is an insult.

She sent me a chess book for my seventeenth birthday. It was for Devan. She had written his name in it.
