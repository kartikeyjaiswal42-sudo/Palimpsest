In the summer after tenth I spent eight thousand rupees on phone cases, and there are still thirty-one of them in a carton under my bed. The money was mine, from two years of birthday envelopes and a set of tuitions I took for a boy in seventh, but my mother had to co-sign the order because the website wanted an adult's name. That detail matters later.

The idea was simple and I thought it was clever. A supplier in Delhi sold blank cases at forty rupees and printed whatever design you uploaded for another thirty. I made eleven designs on my laptop, mostly line drawings of things from our town, the clock tower, the bridge, a chai glass, and I priced them at two hundred and fifty. The margin looked beautiful written down. I ordered a hundred and ten pieces because the per-unit rate dropped at a hundred. Ninety of them were for the iPhone 11 and the iPhone 12.

I sold nine. Two to my cousin, one to a teacher who I think felt sorry for me, and six at a stall I set up outside school on a Saturday, which the guard shut down at noon anyway. Almost nobody in my class has an iPhone. They have Redmis and Realmes and one boy has a Vivo he has dropped so many times it is held together with tape, and none of them were going to pay two hundred and fifty rupees for a drawing of a clock tower they see every day.

For about six months I told the story with the supplier as the villain. The print on the first batch was slightly off-centre, and the edges of a few cases were rough, and that was true, and it let me say the word quality in a tone that made it sound like the failure had happened to me. I said it to my mother, to my friends, to an uncle who asked at a wedding.

The thing I actually got wrong is smaller and worse. I never asked anybody. Not one person. I had a hundred and forty classmates and a phone full of their numbers, and it would have cost me an afternoon to walk around asking what phone they had and what they would pay, and I did not do it, because I did not want an answer that would stop me. I wanted the ordering part. I wanted the box arriving. That is the part I still think about, because it is not a business mistake, it is a thing about me that shows up elsewhere.

What I did next was unglamorous. I dropped the price to eighty, below cost, and sold about forty more over two months, mostly through my cousin's college group. I gave a dozen away at Diwali, which felt like cheating until my mother pointed out that a gift is not a loss if you were never going to sell it. I went back to tutoring, two students instead of one, and put the money in the same envelope until it was more than eight thousand, which took until March. Nobody had asked me to do that.

Last month a friend told me about an idea for selling custom cricket-team jerseys and asked if I wanted in. The first thing out of my mouth was a question about who had already said they would buy one, and he did not have an answer, and I heard myself being annoying in exactly the way somebody should have been annoying with me. I still have the carton. I keep one case on my desk, the clock tower one, on a phone model I do not own and never did. It is a good drawing. That was never the problem.
