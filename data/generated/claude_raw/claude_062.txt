I was nineteen lines into the code when I realized I had no idea what I was doing. Not the syntax—I had looked that up in the tutorials. I meant the architecture. I had decided to make a game for my cousin Sanjay, who was eight and constantly told me he was bored with the games he already had. The game was a simple side-scroller, just something to move him around a screen and have him collect things. In my head it was clear. On the screen, it was a mess.

The problem was I had started coding without planning. I had jumped straight into writing the player movement because that was the exciting part. I wanted to see the character move. I wanted Sanjay to come over and see something playable immediately. What I didn't do was think about how the player interacted with the world, how gravity worked, what happened when he touched a wall. These things weren't exciting to plan. They were just logical scaffolding. So I had skipped them.

I spent two weeks fixing things that should have been set up from the start. A wall that should have been solid wasn't. The character clipped through platforms. When you collected items they disappeared but also sometimes didn't, and I couldn't figure out why. Every time I tried to fix one problem I created three more. The code was getting longer and messier. I was reading forums and Stack Overflow posts trying to debug things that experienced programmers said were fundamental problems—problems that wouldn't exist if I had planned.

When Sanjay finally played it, he was kind. He played for maybe five minutes and then lost interest. The game was janky and confusing. The character moved in a way that felt wrong. There was no feedback for what he was supposed to do. It wasn't fun. He went back to his old games without saying anything, and I felt the specific shame of someone who had gotten a commission from a child and failed to deliver.

What gets to me is that I knew better. I had read about game development. I understood that architecture mattered. I just didn't want to do the boring part. I wanted to jump to the part where things worked. I wanted to be a person who made games without being a person who thought through how games should work.

I did it differently the next time. I planned everything before writing a single line. I drew out the structure, thought through the physics, decided what would happen in different scenarios. It took a week of planning. It felt like the game was already finished before I started coding, which seemed wrong somehow. But when I actually coded it, things went smoothly. The architecture held. When I fixed a bug, it stayed fixed. When I added features, they integrated cleanly.

I asked Sanjay to try again. This version he played for twenty minutes. He asked questions about where to go next. He actually cared whether he won or lost. It wasn't a good game—I'm not claiming that. But it was a real game.

The thing about that first failure is I still think about it. Not with shame anymore, but with the understanding that the invisible work is the most important work. Every game Sanjay plays or every piece of software I use is mostly planning that nobody sees. The exciting part is the smallest part. I learned this by failing to do it, by trying to skip to the good part and discovering there is no good part without the boring part first.
