Every Saturday, in the last ten minutes before we packed up the stall, I wrote down the things people had asked for that we did not have. I did it in a spiral notebook that lived in the cash box, and for a long time I could not have told you why. It began as something to do with my hands while my mother counted coins.

We sold pickles and chutneys at the weekend market from a folding table under a green tarpaulin. This was my mother's business, technically, but by the second year it was mostly mine, because she worked Saturdays at the clinic and could only come for the last hour. Nobody taught me how to run it. There was no course. There was my mother's recipe book, a price list she had written once in marker, and the observable fact that the woman two stalls down selling honey sold about four times as much as we did.

So the notebook. Someone asks if we do a garlic pickle. Write it down. Someone asks if the mango one is sweet or sour, and then buys nothing when I say sour. Write it down. Someone picks up a jar, turns it over looking for something, puts it back. Write down: looking for something on the back. It took me maybe two months of Saturdays to work out that the last one meant an ingredients list, and that people who cannot find an ingredients list assume you are hiding something. I printed labels at a shop near the station for four rupees each. Sales went up by about a fifth within a month and I have never been so pleased with anything in my life.

That is the whole method, and I want to be honest that it is not clever. Write down what people say. Read it back in the evening when you are tired. Notice which sentence has now appeared eleven times. The notebook is not full of insight; it is full of dull repeated phrases, and the insight only exists in the repetition. Do you have anything smaller. Do you have anything smaller. Do you have anything smaller — and eventually you introduce a hundred-gram jar, and it becomes a third of your sales because people want to try a pickle before committing to a kilogram of it.

What learning this without a teacher showed me is that I had badly misunderstood where knowledge comes from. In school, understanding arrives from above: someone who knows explains it to someone who does not. I had assumed that any real business skill worked the same way, and that there was a book somewhere I had not read. There was not. What there was instead was a green tarpaulin, a folding table, and roughly six hundred strangers a day walking past me and telling me, without meaning to, exactly what was wrong with my stall.

It also showed me something less flattering. My first instinct, whenever sales were bad, was to explain it — the weather, the festival, the new stall selling the same thing cheaper. Explanations felt like understanding and cost nothing. The notebook did not let me do that, because it was written in other people's words, not mine.

I still keep it. Three notebooks now, actually, rubber-banded together in the cash box. My mother thinks I am sentimental about them. I am not, particularly. I just have not found anything that works better.
