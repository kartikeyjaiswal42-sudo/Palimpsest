The jacket has been in our hallway closet for seven years now, and I know exactly when my father stopped wearing it. He bought it for his last job interview before the layoffs started, a charcoal gray thing with a subtle weave that made him look sharper, more hopeful. He wore it to exactly three interviews. After that, he stopped going.

I found it last month while looking for my winter coat. The fabric still smells like his cologne from back then, the specific one he wore to work. I pulled it out and held it like it was evidence. Which, I guess, it is.

My father worked for the same company for eighteen years. He was good at what he did. Efficient, reliable, the kind of employee who didn't make waves and produced consistent work. When the restructuring happened, they let go of everyone over fifty in his division. He was fifty-two. They gave him a severance package and a letter wishing him well in future endeavors.

For the first month, he treated job-searching like a job. He woke up at six in the morning anyway, wore the new jacket, sent out resumes, made calls. He was optimistic in a way I hadn't seen him before, talking about this as an opportunity to find something better. But nothing came. The interviews dried up. The callbacks stopped. By month three, he was applying for positions he was overqualified for and still not getting offers. By month six, he'd stopped applying altogether.

The jacket stayed in his closet. He started staying up late watching television instead of sleeping, and going to bed after we'd all left in the morning. He stopped shaving on weekends. My mother started working overtime, her salary becoming the thread that held us together. My brother and I didn't have to change our lives visibly, but we knew. We could feel the weight of it, the way everything had shifted without any of us being consulted about it.

What I understand now, and what I didn't fully grasp then, was that losing a job after that many years wasn't just losing money. It was losing the structure that organized his whole identity. He was the person who went to work. He was good at his job. And then suddenly he wasn't anyone's employee and wasn't good at anything that seemed to matter in the job market.

The jacket represents all of that. It was meant to signal a comeback. It was the physical thing that said, "I am still a professional. I still matter in this world." When he stopped wearing it, he wasn't just giving up on the job search. He was giving up on the idea that he still fit into that world.

He's been working part-time at a hardware store for the last two years. It pays less than half what he made before. Sometimes I catch him looking away when we talk about money, and I know he's thinking about all the years of contributions he made, all the loyalty that apparently meant nothing when the company decided to restructure.

I've never asked him why he doesn't wear the jacket anymore. I think we both know the answer. Wearing it would require believing again that he might need it, that there's still a version of his life where he's interviewing for something that values what he's learned to do. The jacket hangs in our closet like a memorial to that hope. I don't think he could bear to put it on and then take it off again.

He's still my father. He still makes breakfast on Sunday mornings, still listens when I talk about school, still cares about the things that matter in our house. But there's something in him that's quieter now, something that moved aside to make room for a hurt I'm still learning to see clearly. And that jacket is the artifact of that moment when he stopped believing it would work out.
