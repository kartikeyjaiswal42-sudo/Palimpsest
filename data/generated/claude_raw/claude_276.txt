The tin is an old Amul milk powder tin, the blue one with the label mostly scraped off, and it holds two hundred safety pins, a spool of black thread, four spare blouse hooks, a roll of double-sided tape, and a pair of nail scissors. My mother gave it to me on the first morning of my cousin Meera's wedding and said, you are in charge of the back room. She meant the small storeroom behind the stage at the banquet hall, the one with the mop bucket and the stacked chairs and the tube light that buzzed. That is where I spent three days. That is, I think, where I am most myself.

I was sixteen and I had not asked for the job. My aunts had decided that because I was calm and had no other function at a wedding — too old to be cute, too young to be consulted — I would be the person who solved things. Within two hours it turned out this was an actual job. A pleat came loose. A garland split at the knot. A great-uncle's kurta button went missing somewhere between the car and the door. Someone's grandmother needed to sit down urgently in a room that was not full of noise. Each time, somebody would find me and I would go into the storeroom with the tin.

What I noticed, standing under that buzzing tube light with a pin between my teeth, was that I was not nervous. Out in the hall I was terrible. I did not know what to say to relatives who had last seen me at eight. I hovered near the food. But three feet inside the storeroom door, with a specific problem in front of me and a tin of things that might fix it, I was completely comfortable, and people spoke to me differently — quickly, plainly, telling me what was wrong instead of asking me how school was.

The best moment came on the second evening. Meera came in by herself, still in the heavy red, and sat on a stack of chairs and asked me to unpin a section of her dupatta that had been fastened too tight across her shoulder for four hours. She did not want her mother, who would have fussed. She wanted somebody who would just do it and not make it a conversation. I unpinned it, put the pins back in the tin, and she sat there for maybe ninety seconds in the quiet with her eyes shut, and then she went back out. Neither of us said anything about it. That silence felt like the highest compliment I have ever been paid.

I have since noticed that I look for that room everywhere. It is behind the counter at the canteen on sports day, the sound desk at the back of the auditorium, the corner of the lab where the glassware is washed. Any place slightly to the side of the event, where the work is concrete and the only test of success is whether the thing is now fixed.

I still have the tin. It sits above my desk and I have added to it — superglue, a screwdriver, spare shoelaces. When I picture myself at my best, I am not on a stage. I am three feet behind it, under a bad light, with the lid already off.
