The bolt would not come. I had my father's adjustable wrench and I had been at it eleven minutes and the head of the bolt was rounded off and rusted into itself, and behind me Deshawn was saying we should go, that the fence guys came at seven, that it was already six-forty. The chain net hung off the rim at an angle, three links torn at the top where somebody had hung on it years ago. I got the wrench on it sideways and pulled with both hands and my feet against the pole, and the whole rim shifted a quarter inch, and then the bolt turned.

We took the net. Just the net, because we could not take the rim. It weighs about a pound and a half and it is the color of a nickel that has been in a fountain, and the torn links at the top are sharp enough that I have cut myself on them twice.

The court was between the Hedgepeth Apartments and the AME church parking lot, and it had been there since before my mother lived on that block. Two hoops, one working. The pavement had a crack that ran diagonally from the free throw line into the corner, and everybody who played there knew that a ball crossing the crack at speed would kick left, and everybody who did not play there did not know that, which was worth about two points a game against kids from Fairview. There was no three-point line, so we used the seam in the asphalt where they had patched it in 2011.

The city took it in April. The letter said the parcel had been sold and that a mixed-use development was going in and that the recreation department was "evaluating alternative sites in the district." There was a meeting. Eleven people went. My mother went and spoke for two minutes about her brother, who is fifty-one and still plays there on Sundays, and a man wrote it down.

They brought the equipment in on a Thursday. I was at school. Deshawn's brother texted us a video, nine seconds, the backboard folding in on itself like cardboard.

The net is on a nail in my room, above the desk, next to nothing. My mother asked once whether I wanted to frame it and I said no. It is not clean. It smells faintly of rust and of the inside of my gym bag, and the bottom four inches are lighter than the rest because that is the part the ball touched.

I have taken it down maybe six times. Once to show my uncle, who held it and did not say much, and turned it over the way you check whether a thing is really the thing. Once when my father was fixing the closet door and asked if I still had that wrench.

The development has a sign now with a rendering on it. There are people in the rendering. There is a woman with a dog and a man carrying a coffee and they are standing roughly where the free throw line was, at what would have been the top of the key, and the crack does not appear in the drawing because the drawing does not have asphalt.

There is a court at the middle school on Cardinal, and it is newer, and the rims have real nets, the white nylon kind that flip up. I go there sometimes. The pavement is flat. The ball goes where you send it.

Deshawn keeps a photo on his phone from the last week, all of us on the pavement in the heat, and he sent it around in July, and I looked at it for a long time and then I put my phone face down on the desk and went and shot in the driveway until it got dark.
