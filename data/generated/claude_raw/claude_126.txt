The queen had one eye missing. I'd found her in my grandfather's office drawer, buried under some old tax documents, and I knew immediately that I was going to build an entire chess club around her.

Nobody at my school played chess. That wasn't a guess—I'd asked around. I knew everyone in my small school, and none of them had ever sat down to a real game. When I tried to explain the rules to people, their eyes glazed over the way they did in math class. The idea of staring at a board for an hour seemed to physically pain them. I got that. I understood why chess seemed boring if you didn't already understand why it was beautiful.

But I had this queen with the missing eye, and I had this absolute certainty that if I could just get five people to learn the game, some of them would fall into it the way I had.

I made a poster for the club meetings using a photo I took of the queen. Blurry, probably too artsy, but it made her look like something from a mystery novel. Someone had damaged her. Someone had loved her enough to keep her anyway. I posted it outside the library before school started in the fall, and I didn't honestly expect much. Maybe one person would show up out of curiosity.

Three people came to the first meeting. By week four, we had seven. By the end of the year, we had a waiting list.

I should say they all became brilliant players or that I discovered some hidden talent in my school or that we went to regional tournaments or something inspiring like that. We didn't. We had one tournament in the spring and we did badly. Some of the kids quit after a month because, as predicted, they found it tedious. One person only showed up because their girlfriend was coming. Two of them never got past the opening moves.

But there were these two kids who came every single Thursday and by the end of the year they were playing each other with this intensity that reminded me of the feeling I had when I first understood what chess was about. They were learning the language. They were beginning to see the board the way I see it, as this universe of possibilities where your mind gets to stretch itself in ways that the rest of the day doesn't allow.

I still have the one-eyed queen in my locker. The club is still going, led by someone else now because I'm graduating. It's smaller than it was at its peak, which makes sense. Most hobbies don't last beyond the novelty. But the fact that it existed at all, that I convinced even a handful of people to care about something they had every reason to ignore—that's the thing I keep thinking about.

I found something that nobody else was looking for. And I was stubborn enough to insist it mattered until a few other people believed me.
