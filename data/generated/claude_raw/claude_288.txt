The camera came from my uncle Devendra, who gave it to me the summer I turned fourteen, and who has never once agreed with how I use it. It is a Zenit-E, Russian, heavy as a brick, and its selenium light meter died sometime before I was born. The needle sits at the bottom of its little window and does not move, no matter how much light you throw at it.

My uncle's position is simple. He thinks I should have bought a cheap digital camera, or used my phone, and spent the four years I spent learning exposure on something that would actually get me somewhere. He is not unkind about it. He says it the way you'd tell someone their shoelace is untied.

What I taught myself was how to guess. There is a rule photographers used before meters, called Sunny 16: on a bright cloudless day, set your aperture to f/16 and your shutter speed to whatever your film speed is, and you'll be roughly right. Everything else works outward from there. Overcast, open up two stops. Shade under a building, three. Late gold light an hour before dark, another two. I found this on a forum, written by a man who signed his posts with the name of a town in Wales, and I did not believe him for about six months.

The way you stop not believing is you waste film. I wasted a great deal of it. A roll of thirty-six frames cost about two hundred rupees to buy and another two hundred to develop, and my first eleven rolls came back with maybe four usable pictures each. My mother thought I was throwing money into a hole. My uncle thought so too, and said so, and I remember the specific afternoon he stood in our kitchen holding a strip of my negatives up to the tube light and saying, gently, that a machine could do this in a hundredth of a second and never be wrong.

He is right about that. That is the part of the disagreement I cannot win and have stopped trying to.

What I would say back, if I could say it in a way that landed, is that being wrong slowly taught me something being right instantly would not have. By roll twenty I could walk out of a building and know, before I lifted the camera, that it was f/8 at a two-fiftieth. Not because I'd calculated it. Because my eyes had been corrected four hundred times. I learned that a skill without a teacher is mostly a long argument with your own confidence, and that the feedback loop matters more than the instruction — a week between shooting and seeing is a brutal way to learn anything, and it is also why the lessons stuck.

We still argue about it. He asked me last month what I plan to do with it, meaning the camera, meaning the four years. I said I didn't know. He nodded like that proved his point, and maybe it does.

I have a picture of him from that kitchen afternoon. The light through the window was doing something I'd never have caught with a meter telling me the average. I never showed it to him.
