The coat is navy, three sizes too big in the shoulders, and it's got a faded Roots logo on the chest that's mostly worn off now. I still have it, folded in the bottom of my closet in Ohio, even though I haven't lived in Toronto in two years and it doesn't get cold enough here to need it. I can't explain why I packed it in the move. I just did.

We left Manila in October of my sophomore year, which if you know anything about Canadian weather is possibly the worst month to arrive with a suitcase full of clothes meant for 85-degree heat. My dad's job transferred fast — six weeks between the offer and the flight — and there wasn't time or, honestly, money to plan around what I'd wear once winter actually hit. I remember standing at a bus stop my second week of school in a hoodie, genuinely shaking, thinking I might actually be in danger, which sounds dramatic but felt very real at the time.

A girl in my English class, Adaeze, noticed. I want to be clear I hadn't said anything to her about it — we'd talked maybe twice, mostly about an essay we were both stuck on. But she must have clocked the hoodie situation, because on a Thursday she just handed me a shopping bag before class and said, kind of fast, like she didn't want to make it a whole thing, "I grew out of this, my mom's making me get rid of stuff, you can have it if you want, no big deal." And walked to her seat.

It was the coat. Obviously it wasn't actually something she'd "grown out of" — it fit me fine and she was, if anything, smaller than me. I figured that out about a month later and never said anything, because I think we both understood that pretending it was a hand-me-down was the whole point. It let me take it without owing her a thank-you speech in the middle of English class.

That's the part I keep coming back to, honestly, more than the coat itself. She'd basically watched a stranger struggling and decided to solve it quietly, in a way that cost her a lie and cost me nothing, not even the discomfort of being visibly helped. I'd moved countries with the assumption that I'd have to handle everything alone now, that nobody owed a new kid anything, and she just — didn't operate that way. It threw me off more than the cold did.

I've tried to pass that particular move along since, in smaller ways — noticing before someone has to ask, and then making it easy for them to say yes. I'm not always good at it. Sometimes I overthink the "no big deal" part and it comes out awkward instead of casual. But I think about that Thursday a lot, about how being cared for by someone who barely knew me changed what I expected from new places after that, in a way that a hundred well-meaning "let me know if you need anything"s never would have.
