Ms. Chen wrote on my eighth grade essay: "You have good instincts but you're not a natural writer. Focus on your strengths elsewhere." I was thirteen. I remember reading those words and feeling like something had been officially diagnosed. Not a writer. Got it. I'd wanted to be one but apparently that want didn't matter. Instinct and talent matter. I didn't have talent.

I showed my parents and my mom immediately said she disagreed. She said teachers don't know everything. She said I wrote interesting things all the time. She said one critical feedback from one teacher didn't mean anything. It felt like she was being nice to me the way you're nice to someone who's trying their best but not actually succeeding. I appreciated it but I didn't believe it.

What followed was a three-year period where I basically stopped trying to be a writer. I took my mom's advice to focus on my strengths—math, science, debate. I was good at those things. Why bang my head against something I wasn't naturally good at? I wrote for school when I had to but it was purely functional. No creativity. No risk-taking. Just getting through the assignment.

Freshman year I took an English class with Mr. Patterson and we had to write something personal. A memoir piece, maybe two pages. I treated it like homework. Got a B. He wrote a comment about it being competent but wondering if I was afraid to take risks. That's when I actually got angry at him. Not because I was afraid to take risks but because I already knew I wasn't a writer. Why was he pointing out all the ways I was failing at something I'd already given up on?

He asked me to come see him after class and I was so defensive, so ready to explain that I knew I wasn't good at this and that was fine. He said that actually, he thought there was something there. That the hesitation in my writing was a choice I was making, not a limitation I had. He said I wrote like someone who was afraid of saying the wrong thing, which he thought was probably true about me in general but that I could write past it if I wanted to.

That conversation changed something. Or it didn't change something and just pointed out that something could change. I can't tell the difference anymore.

I started writing more after that. Taking more risks. Turning in things that weren't perfect. Things that were raw. Things that might be bad but were at least honest. Most of them were not good. But Mr. Patterson was right—the bad writing was better than the competent writing. There was something in it.

By senior year I was actually writing. Not like a professional writer or anything dramatic like that. But I was thinking like one. Taking feedback differently. Revising. Understanding that being a writer wasn't about some innate talent that you either had or didn't. It was about showing up and doing it.

The thing is, I'm still angry at Ms. Chen sometimes. Not angry like I'm holding a grudge but angry like I'm grieving the three years I lost to her one comment. I wonder who I would be if she'd written something slightly different. Something encouraging instead of dismissive. I wonder how many other kids just accepted her diagnosis and gave up.

But I'm also grateful to Mr. Patterson for saying something different. For suggesting that being not-naturally-good-at-something didn't mean I shouldn't do it. That's the thing I'm trying to hold onto now—that my identity isn't determined by my natural talents. It's determined by what I choose to do anyway.
