My grandmother has a laminated card by her front door. It is A5, printed in eighteen-point Arial, and it says: THE DOOR IS LOCKED. YOU LOCKED IT. THE KEY IS IN YOUR LEFT COAT POCKET. Underneath, in a different font because we ran out of ink and reprinted the bottom half, it says: IT IS TUESDAY. NAOMI COMES ON THURSDAY.

I made it. I laminated it in the school library on the machine you have to ask for at the desk, and the woman there asked if it was for a project, and I said yes because the true answer was too long.

The card is the third version. The first one just said the thing about the key. The second added the day. The third added my name, because in November she rang my mother at eleven at night to ask who the girl who comes on Thursdays is, and my mother put the phone down and cried in the kitchen and I stood in the doorway holding a glass of water like an idiot.

Vascular dementia is not the kind where everything goes at once. It is the kind that goes in steps. She will be entirely herself for six weeks — sharp, funny, unkind about the neighbours in a way I find delightful — and then something drops, and it does not come back, and then she is stable at the new level for a while. So the card gets longer. Each line on it is a small stone marking where something used to be.

I have thought a lot about the physics of that card, which sounds like a strange thing to say. When I made the first one I thought of it as a note. A message from us to her. But it does not work like a message, because a message assumes a person who receives it and remembers receiving it. What the card actually is, I think, is a piece of her mind that we built outside her head and screwed to the wall. The knowledge that the door is locked used to live in her. Now it lives on the wall, and she goes to the wall to get it, the way you would go to a shelf.

That reframed something for me. I had been thinking of her as diminishing — as a smaller and smaller version of the person she was. But nobody keeps everything inside themselves. I do not know my own phone number without looking. I know approximately eleven facts and outsource the rest to a device in my pocket. The difference between her and me is one of degree and of dignity: I chose my prostheses and hers were chosen for her, by us, and put in eighteen-point Arial by a granddaughter who could not think of anything else to do.

The Thursdays are the good part and I want to be accurate about them rather than sentimental. Sometimes she knows me. Sometimes she thinks I am my mother at nineteen, which is not distressing for either of us, and I have stopped correcting it because correcting it helps nobody except my sense of being an honest person. We do the same things: we do the shopping list, we water the spider plant that is enormous and possibly indestructible, and she tells me about a dance in 1961 at the Locarno in Sheffield where a man called Vincent stood on her foot. She has told me about Vincent perhaps forty times and the details do not change, which the consultant says is normal: that memory is stored differently from the one about Tuesday.

I know things about my grandmother that my mother does not know, purely because I have heard the Vincent story forty times and my mother heard it twice in 1988 and stopped listening. That is what repetition gives you if you let it. The fortieth telling has details in it that the second one did not.

The card is getting full. I have been thinking about what happens when there is not, and whether we make a second card, and whether a wall of laminated A5 is a kind of person or a kind of loss, and I do not have an answer, but I keep noticing that I have started thinking about memory as something built rather than something possessed.

She is not diminishing. She is being redistributed — into a card, into a spider plant, into a girl who will be able to tell you exactly what Vincent did at the Locarno in 1961 long after there is nobody left who was there.
