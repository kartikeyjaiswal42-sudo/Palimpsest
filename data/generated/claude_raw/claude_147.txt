For nine years, our street ran something called the Thursday Table, six families rotating who'd host dinner each week, and my place in it was simple: I was the kid who showed up early to steal rolls before dinner was announced. Then my dad lost his job at Corbin Tool & Die after thirty-one years, and my place in that community became the thing I got wrong, quietly, for over a year, in a way I still think about more than almost anything else from high school.

Corbin closed in March of my sophomore year, the whole plant, not just my dad's line, and he went from leaving the house at 5:40 every morning to being home all day in a way that made our kitchen feel smaller. Our turn to host Thursday Table came up six weeks later. I told my mom to tell the Ferraros we were skipping it — travel soccer, I said, even though I didn't play travel soccer that season and everyone on the street knew it. I did this again the next time our turn came, and the time after that, until eight months had gone by and we'd quietly stopped being part of something that had been part of us since I was in third grade.

I want to say I did this to protect my dad, and maybe some of that's true. But mostly I did it because I didn't want anyone from that table walking into our house and doing math about why the good chairs weren't out anymore, why the freezer was mostly stocked with what the church pantry gave us. I told a friend from the street that we were "just really busy this year." I said it enough times that I started to believe there was nothing dishonest about it, which is its own kind of dishonesty.

What I got wrong wasn't the caution. It was assuming that my place in that community was something I had to earn back with a full table and full cabinets, instead of something that had already been offered to us for free, nine years running, precisely for a year like the one we were having. The Ferraros showed up anyway, eventually, on a Thursday we hadn't signed up to host, with a casserole and a six-pack and no explanation needed, and my dad answered the door in the shirt he'd been wearing since two job interviews that week had both gone nowhere, and I stood behind him bracing for the moment to be humiliating. It wasn't. Mr. Ferraro just said he'd missed our meatballs and asked if my dad had heard back from the place in Grafton.

My dad found work seventeen months after Corbin closed, at a smaller shop for less money, and by then Thursday Table had kept going without us often enough that I understood something I wish I'd understood at month two: a community isn't a group of people you perform stability for. It's the group that keeps a seat open at the table whether or not you can afford your turn to host. I spent a year guarding a door that nobody on the other side of it was trying to break down, and the thing I still think about isn't the unemployment. It's how much of that year I spent alone in a house that didn't have to be lonely at all.
