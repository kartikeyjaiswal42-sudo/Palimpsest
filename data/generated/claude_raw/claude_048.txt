I stopped playing the piano in March, after eleven years, and my mother has not forgiven me, and I am not sure she is wrong.

I want to put her case first, properly, because when people hear this story they arrive already on my side and that annoys me. Her case is not that she paid for it, though she did, for eleven years, forty minutes a week with Mrs Petrov and then with Mr Ainsworth after Mrs Petrov's hip. Her case is not even that I was good, though I was — Grade 8 at fifteen, distinction, the Wigmore Hall thing with the county youth orchestra where I did not play a wrong note in the Poulenc and have never once been able to enjoy the recording.

Her case is that quitting is a decision made by a version of me that will not be here in ten years, on behalf of a version of me who will be, and who will want it back and will not be able to have it.

She has evidence for this. She played the violin until she was nineteen and stopped when she went to university, and she has said, more than once, in a completely flat voice, that it is the thing she regrets. She has a violin in the loft. She has not opened the case since we moved.

So when she says you will regret this she is not guessing. She is reporting.

My case is harder to say without sounding ungrateful. It is not that I hate the piano. I do not hate the piano. It is that I had stopped being able to tell the difference between playing and being seen to play. Somewhere around Grade 7 the instrument became a thing I was measured by, and after that every hour at the keyboard had a small auditor sitting in it. I would learn a piece and my first thought at the end of it was never that was beautiful, it was that's about eighty per cent.

And it was consuming everything. Two hours a day, which is not a boast, it is an accounting. Two hours a day is fourteen a week, is the reason I have never had a Saturday job, is the reason I stopped drawing at thirteen.

The argument itself was not one argument. It was about eight, over four months, in kitchens and cars. She said: you are throwing away eleven years. I said: I am not throwing them away, I have them, they are in my hands. She said: they will go. And this is the sentence I have not been able to answer, because she is factually right. It does go. I am worse now than I was in March. I sat down at my grandmother's in August and my left hand had already got stupid.

What I said back was that I would rather lose something I had than keep spending my life defending it. She said that was the kind of thing you say at seventeen. Which — again — is a fair point that I cannot disprove for about a decade.

We do not really talk about it now. She has stopped mentioning it, which is worse than the arguing. The piano is still in the front room and nobody has suggested selling it and I do not know what that means. Sometimes I play in the evenings when the house is empty, badly, sight-reading things I have no business attempting, and I like it more than I liked any of the exams, and I have not told her that because she would take it as proof.

Maybe it is proof. Maybe in fifteen years I will have a violin in a loft of my own and I will understand exactly what she was trying to save me from, and I will not be able to explain it to my own child either.

But I am seventeen now, and it is my two hours a day, and the person who has to spend them is me. That is the whole of my argument, and I know it is not enough, and I did it anyway.
