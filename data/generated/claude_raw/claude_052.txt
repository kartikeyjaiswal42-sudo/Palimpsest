Our kitchen is nine feet by eleven. I have measured it, because when I was fourteen I wanted to prove to my mother that there was not enough room for two people to cook at once, and I was right, and it did not help my case at all. She said the kitchen was fine and I was the problem, which was also true.

It is where I feel most like myself. Not because I am good in it. Because it is the only place I know where being wrong is immediately, undeniably visible, and nobody has to explain it to you.

The first dinner I cooked alone was for my grandmother's birthday. There were nine of us. I was fifteen and I had spent a summer watching cooking videos with the sound off during class, and I had decided that a roast chicken, potatoes, green beans, and a lemon cake were within reach. I wrote out a schedule on the back of an envelope. I still have it somewhere. Every line on it is wrong.

The problem was not the chicken, though the chicken was a problem. The problem was that I had built the whole plan around the oven as if it were three ovens. The cake needed 180 degrees. The chicken needed 200. The potatoes needed 220 and space around them, which they did not get, so they steamed instead of roasting and came out pale and loose, like something that had been rescued from a flood. I took the cake out early because I needed the shelf. It sank in the middle. I put it back in later and it developed a crust over a wet centre, which is a thing I did not know was possible.

The chicken was underdone at the thigh. I discovered this at the table, in front of everyone, when my uncle cut into it and there was that pink at the joint and the thin bright liquid, and he did the thing where he said nothing and quietly moved on to the beans. My father took the plate back to the kitchen without a word and put the whole bird back in for another thirty-five minutes while we ate green beans and sunken cake and my grandmother told a story about a wedding in 1968 to fill the time.

Nobody was cruel about it. That is what I still think about. If someone had shouted I could have been angry and the anger would have carried me somewhere. Instead there was a table of people being kind, eating beans, waiting, and I sat there understanding for the first time that I had made a plan that could not have worked, that no amount of trying harder in the moment would have saved, because the failure was already sitting in the envelope before I turned the oven on.

I go back to that kitchen constantly now. I cook most Sundays. I have learned the things you learn: that the oven runs about fifteen degrees cold, that you cook the thing that can wait first and the thing that cannot wait last, that resting meat is not a suggestion. But the thing I actually took from that night is smaller and less about food. It is that I am the kind of person who will confidently build an elaborate structure on top of an assumption I never checked. That is my particular flaw. I know it now. I still do it — in essays, in group projects, in a physics coursework last spring where I wrote nine pages before realising I had misread the question.

The kitchen is where I feel most myself because it is where I catch myself fastest. Nine feet by eleven, too small for two people, and honest about everything. The chicken tells you. The cake tells you. You cannot argue with a sunken cake.

My grandmother turns eighty in March. I am cooking. I have started the schedule already.
