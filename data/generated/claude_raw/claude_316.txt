There is a mug on the windowsill in our kitchen that leans about four degrees to the left. My dad drinks out of it on purpose, which I think is a joke and also isn't. It was the eleventh mug I made and the first one that held water.

The wheel came off Gumtree for forty pounds, from a woman in Sale whose mother had died. It's a Shimpo, or it was, in about 1980. The pedal sticks. And the head — the metal disc the clay sits on — has a wobble. Not a big one. If you spin it empty and put a pencil next to it you can watch the gap open and close, maybe two millimetres. The woman didn't mention it. I don't think she knew.

Nobody taught me. There's no pottery at my school and the studio in town charges thirty-five an hour, so it was YouTube and a bucket and the shed. And for about four months I was completely, spectacularly bad.

Here is what happens. You centre the clay — you push it down and in with the heel of your hand until it stops fighting you and just sits there spinning as if it isn't moving at all. Then you open it, you pull the walls, you get a cylinder. Except my clay would never quite stop moving. There'd always be this slow lurch, a pulse, once per rotation. And every video I watched said the same thing: if it's off-centre, you haven't centred it. Push harder. Brace your elbow on your thigh. Breathe out.

So I assumed it was me. For four months I assumed it was me. I have a whole cardboard box of collapsed walls in the shed, every one of them lurching in the same direction, and I sat there taking it personally.

The thing that fixed it wasn't insight, it was boredom. I was waiting for the shed to warm up one Saturday and I spun the empty head and watched it, and I saw the gap between the head and the splash pan open and close. Once per rotation. Same rhythm as the lurch.

It wasn't me. It was never entirely me.

I want to be careful, because the tidy version of this is that I learned to stop blaming myself, and that's not right either. Some of it was me. My hands were weak and my clay was too dry and I was rushing the pull. The honest version is messier: the machine was broken and I was bad, at the same time, and I couldn't tell the two apart because I had no one to ask.

That's the real answer to what teaching yourself shows you. Not persistence. Not resourcefulness. It's that without a teacher you have no way of knowing which of your failures are diagnostic. A teacher would have spun that head in ten seconds. Instead I spent a third of a year building a very confident theory about my own hands.

I never fixed the wobble properly. I shimmed it and it's better and it's still there. What I did instead was learn to throw with it — to feel the pulse coming and let my fingers give slightly on that quarter of the turn, which is apparently not a technique anyone teaches, because why would you.

So the mug leans. Four degrees, always the same direction, because I was compensating for a machine and slightly overdid it. My dad says it's the best one.
