Every night for seven months my mother and I walked one loop of our block after dinner, and I asked her civics questions. It was eleven minutes if we walked normally and fourteen if we stopped for the Ferreiras' dog. She would answer, and if she got one wrong I would say it back to her and we would keep going. That was the whole routine. Sneakers by the door, the same loop, the same order of streets.

I started it because I thought the test was the problem to solve. My mother has lived in this country for nineteen years, and she was studying a hundred questions about the Federalist Papers and the number of amendments, and I remember thinking, out loud, more than once, that it was absurd. She could name her city councilman. She had paid taxes for nineteen years. The test seemed to me like a hazing ritual dressed as a civics quiz, and I said so, and I believed it with the certainty of somebody who was not taking it.

She disagreed with me on the loop one night in November. Not dramatically. I had said something like "none of this actually means anything," and she said, "It means something to me."

Then she told me she had not known what the Bill of Rights was. Not the details — the thing itself. She said she had lived here for nineteen years assuming that whatever protections she had were extended to her by permission, and could be withdrawn the same way, and that learning the First Amendment was written down and applied to her had changed how she thought about her own job, where her manager had been shorting hours. She had gone in and asked about it in September. I had not known that either.

I had been so sure the test was empty that I never asked her what was in it for her. My argument was about dignity, which is a nice thing to argue about, and hers was about information she had actually been missing, which is less romantic and more useful.

We kept walking the loop. What changed was that I stopped quizzing and started asking her what she thought about the answers. Judicial review took us three nights. She has opinions about the electoral college that I do not share.

She passed in June. I was expecting to feel vindicated on her behalf, and what I felt instead was that the eleven minutes had been the point and the certificate was the receipt.

I still think the test is a strange instrument, badly designed, and that it filters for the wrong things. But I no longer say it means nothing, because I said that to a person for whom it meant a great deal, and she was standing right next to me, and I had not thought to ask.
