The object is a carburetor, specifically the left one off a 1978 Honda CB550, and if you'd told me two summers ago that I'd be able to identify a part like that on sight, disassembled, sitting in a coffee can of gasoline on my uncle's workbench, I would have laughed at you, because at the start of that summer I could not have told you what a carburetor even did.

My uncle Ray had the bike in pieces in his garage for what he claimed was "temporary," though judging by the layer of dust on the gas tank when I first saw it, temporary had been going on for roughly four years. It had belonged to my grandfather, who died before I was old enough to really remember him beyond a few photographs and the smell of a specific brand of pipe tobacco that apparently still lingers, according to my mother, in one corner of Ray's garage. Nobody had asked me to fix it. I offered, mostly because I was seventeen and bored and my uncle looked tired of feeling guilty about a bike he couldn't bring himself to sell and hadn't touched in years.

We started with the engine, because Ray said an engine that won't run makes everything else pointless, and for the first three weeks I mostly held flashlights and handed him wrenches of the wrong size until I learned to tell a nine-sixteenths from a half-inch by feel instead of by squinting at the little numbers stamped into the metal. But it was the carburetors — there were two, one for each cylinder — that actually taught me something, because they were small enough that Ray eventually just handed me one and said, essentially, good luck, ask before you force anything.

I took it apart on a card table in the driveway with a manual open beside me, laying every screw and gasket out on a towel in the exact order I removed them, because Ray told me on day one that a mechanic who doesn't lay parts out in order is a mechanic who's about to have a very bad afternoon. The float, the needle valve, the tiny brass jets with holes so small I had to hold them up to the sun to see if they were actually clogged or just looked clogged. I soaked it in gas, scrubbed the varnish out of channels smaller than a pencil lead with a guitar string because that's what Ray used, apparently that's a real technique, and put it back together twice because the first time I put the float in upside down and it flooded.

When the engine finally turned over — not that carburetor alone, obviously, months of other work besides — it didn't roar to life like in a movie. It coughed, caught, died, coughed again, and then, on the fourth try, settled into an idle that shook the whole garage floor, and Ray let out a sound I'd never heard him make before, something between a laugh and something else.

I keep that carburetor now, not on the bike — it's back where it belongs, doing its actual job — but a spare one Ray gave me afterward, cleaned and reassembled, sitting on my desk at home. I turn it over sometimes when I'm trying to think through something unrelated, homework, an argument with a friend, whatever. There's something in the specific, patient logic of it that I find calming: every tiny passage exists for a reason, every part only works because every other part is exactly where it should be, and if something's wrong, the fix is almost never mysterious, it's almost always just dirt in a channel that needs clearing.

My grandfather rode that bike for eleven years before his knees gave out and it went into the garage. I never knew him well enough to miss him the way my mother does, but I think I understand something about him now that I didn't before, something I only got access to through the specific stupid patience of cleaning out a jet with a guitar string in a hot driveway. Ray still doesn't ride it much. Neither do I, honestly — I'm not licensed yet. But some nights he starts it up in the driveway just to hear it idle, and I go outside and stand there with him, and neither of us says anything, because I don't think there's anything that needs saying.
