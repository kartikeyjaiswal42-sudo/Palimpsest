I didn't learn to read music until I was fifteen, which is stupidly late in the music world. Most people learn as kids. They're taught it alongside learning an instrument. They just absorb it the way you absorb any language that people around you speak. But my childhood was not a musical one, so at fifteen I was sitting in jazz band without being able to read a single note of sheet music.

I learned by ear. That's what I told people when they asked. I learned by ear. Which sounds cool and natural and like I have some kind of musical instinct. What it actually meant was that I waited to hear other people play and then I copied them. I watched the trumpet player's fingers. I listened to the rhythm. I played back what I heard. It worked fine for jazz. It worked less fine when we played new pieces I'd never heard and everyone was sight-reading off the sheet and I was the person holding up the section.

The teacher suggested—very kindly—that I might want to learn to read music. She offered to teach me. She said it would take maybe a month to get the basics down. A month. And I think that's when I decided I was insulted. A month? I'd spent seven years playing music without needing to read it. I was fine. I could play complex pieces by ear. I understood theory. Reading music seemed unnecessary. Reading music seemed like something you had to do if you weren't naturally musical. I was naturally musical.

For about six months I remained stubbornly illiterate on the subject. Then we got a new piece that I literally could not figure out by ear because it was too complex and I hadn't heard it before and nobody could play it slow enough for me to figure out the notes individually. I bombed the first rehearsal. Completely fell apart. And that's when something shifted.

I realized that not being able to read music wasn't brave or natural or independent. It was limiting. It was like refusing to learn written English because you could speak fine. It didn't make you special. It just made you unable to access things. Sheet music is a language and I had decided I didn't need to learn it, and that decision was starting to cost me.

So I learned to read music at fifteen in about six weeks. The teacher was right—it's not that hard. The basics are simple. The note on the bottom of the staff is E, then F, G, A, B, C, D, E again at the top. Lines and spaces. Treble clef and bass clef. Time signatures. It's like learning a code. Once you have the code, everything just translates.

What changed after I learned was less about the music and more about my understanding of myself. I realized how much of my identity had been built on things I was naturally good at. When something required actual learning and effort and time, my impulse was to reject it. To convince myself that learning it would diminish the natural talent I already had. But that's not how learning works. Learning doesn't erase instinct. It enhances it. And refusing to learn isn't brave or pure. It's just fearful.

I still have good instincts with music. But now I can also read. And being able to do both things makes me better than I ever was at just doing one.
