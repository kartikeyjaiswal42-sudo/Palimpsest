I met Sofia through a forum about literature when I was fourteen. She was in Portugal, same age, and we started messaging about books we were reading. That was four years ago. She's the person I send screenshots to, the person I think of when I want to share something I've found. The person who knows my bad jokes and laughs anyway.

Our friendship exists entirely online. We've never met. We probably never will, at least not for several years. But this digital space, this place where our conversations happen, has become where I feel most myself.

The internet can feel like a lot of places to different people. For some it's performative, a stage where you're always aware you're being watched. For others it's escapism, a break from reality. For me, it's become the most honest place I know. And that's almost entirely because of Sofia.

With Sofia, there's no pretense. We started talking at an age where we were both still figuring out who we were, and we've done a lot of that figuring out together. She knows about the anxiety I get before presentations. She knows which teachers I dislike and why. She knows my family drama, my friend conflicts, the things I'm insecure about. And I know the same about her—her immigrant parents' pressure, her love of math, the boy she's been quietly in love with for two years.

We have a ritual. Every Sunday evening, my time, we video call and just talk. Sometimes for ten minutes. Sometimes for hours. We might be doing homework in the background or making tea or just lying on our beds looking at the ceiling. Often we don't even talk much. We just exist in the same space, digitally, and it's enough.

This friendship has taught me something about belonging. I have friends here, at school. I'm not isolated or lonely in the way that questions sometimes imply. But there's something different about Sofia. She sees me when I'm not performing. When I'm not thinking about how my words will land or what impression I'm making. She knows me when I'm tired or frustrated or just being weird.

What strikes me most is that none of this requires physical proximity. We don't hang out. We don't have memories of doing things together. We exist through conversation alone, and somehow that's been enough to build something real. It's made me question what friendship actually requires. It turns out it doesn't require shared geography or weekly meetups or even being able to grab coffee. It requires attention. It requires someone who chooses to know you.

When I think about where I feel most myself, I keep coming back to my bedroom, late evening, calling her. To the feeling of being able to say whatever I'm thinking without editing. To the knowledge that someone in another country has chosen to know me thoroughly, to witness my confusion and growth and weirdness, simply because they want to.

It's changed how I think about connection. I used to believe that real friendship had to look a certain way, had to happen in person, had to include the markers I'd been taught to recognize. But Sofia and I have built something that feels more authentic to me than friendships that follow all those rules.

She's probably the person who knows me best, even though we've never sat in the same room. In some ways because of that. There's an honesty in a friendship that exists purely through words and choice. No social pressure. No pretense. Just two people deciding, over and over, to keep choosing each other.
