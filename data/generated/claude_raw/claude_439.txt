I lose track of time the same way every time I do it, which is by picking up a single cedar strip, running my thumb along its edge to feel for a rough spot, and telling myself I'll only sand that one spot before I stop for dinner. That was the whole rhythm of the summer I built a canoe with my uncle Sal in his garage, and it's the closest thing I have to a genuine obsession — not canoes exactly, but the specific, repetitive, almost meditative labor of shaping wood by hand into something that has to hold its shape against actual water.

We started in June with a stack of quarter-inch cedar strips and a set of forms Sal had built from plywood, a skeleton the canoe's hull would eventually wrap around. The habit that ended up mattering more than anything else in the whole project was small and boring on its own: every single evening, whether we'd made real progress that day or not, we sanded. Just sanding, forty-five minutes to an hour, going over strips we'd already glued the day before, smoothing the glue lines, checking for gaps. It wasn't the exciting part of boatbuilding. Nobody posts videos of the sanding.

I didn't understand why Sal insisted on it every single evening until about six weeks in, when we found a strip near the bow that had cupped slightly from humidity, a warp so small I wouldn't have noticed it if I hadn't been running my hands over that exact section every night for weeks and building a kind of physical memory of what it was supposed to feel like. We caught it early enough to steam it flat again and reglue it properly. Sal told me, matter-of-factly, that boats fail in places nobody checks because nobody's touching that part of the hull regularly enough to notice a change, and that the nightly sanding wasn't really about smoothness at all — it was about knowing the boat well enough, strip by strip, to feel when something had gone wrong.

That idea is the thing I actually can't stop thinking about, the reason I now spend hours reading about traditional boatbuilding techniques that have nothing to do with any canoe I'm currently working on. There's something about a discipline where the correct method is inseparable from repeated physical attention — where you can't shortcut the knowing by reading about it, you have to actually run your hands over the material, night after night, until your hands know more than your eyes do. I've since read most of a book called The Wood and Canvas Canoe that Sal lent me, cover creased from decades of his own use, and I follow a retired boatbuilder in Nova Scotia on a woodworking forum who posts long, meandering explanations of steam-bending ribs that I read start to finish even when I have no immediate use for the information.

We finished the canoe in August, seventeen feet of cedar and fiberglass that still smells faintly of the epoxy when it sits in the sun too long. It floats straight, doesn't leak, holds two adults comfortably, and none of that would be true, I genuinely believe, without those forty-five minutes every evening that felt, most nights, like the least important part of the whole project. I've kept the habit going in other places since — I sand my chess set every week now even though nobody asked me to, I go over my bike chain by hand instead of just spraying it, small unnecessary maintenance rituals that I now understand aren't really about the object at all. They're about staying in close enough contact with something that you'd actually notice the moment it started to go wrong, which turns out to be a much harder skill than building the thing in the first place.
