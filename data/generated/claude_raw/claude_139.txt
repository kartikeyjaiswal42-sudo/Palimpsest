There's a notepad by our toaster. It's nothing special to look at — spiral bound, about the size of a coaster, the kind you'd buy in a three-pack at the drugstore without thinking twice. My mom writes one Korean word on it every morning, has for as long as I can remember, and for a stretch of years I barely looked at it. I want to explain why that stretch happened and why I look at it now.

I was twelve when I decided we should speak English at home. Nobody made me decide this. I just got tired, I think, of the half-second lag before I answered a friend on the phone because I'd been mid-sentence in Korean with my mom, tired of feeling like two different kids depending on which room I was standing in. So I asked. Not even asked, really — I sort of announced it, the way twelve-year-olds announce things, like the decision had already been made somewhere above my head and I was just the messenger. My parents went along with it faster than I expected. Looking back, I think they'd been waiting for permission to stop working so hard at something that clearly embarrassed me.

For the next four years, our house ran in English. Not perfect English — my dad still counts in Korean, still yells numbers at the TV during soccer games without noticing he's doing it — but the day-to-day stuff, dinner, homework, arguments about curfew, all of it switched over. I got good at English fast, better than I already was, which I guess was the point. And somewhere in there, without me clocking it, my Korean started going soft around the edges. Words I used to grab without thinking started needing a half-second search. I stopped noticing the notepad by the toaster the same way you stop noticing a clock ticking in a room you sit in every day.

Except my mom never stopped writing on it. Every single morning, before anyone else was up, one word. Sometimes a simple one, sometimes something weird and specific, like the word for the smell right before rain. I didn't ask her why she kept doing it during the years we weren't even speaking the language at the table. I think I assumed it was just a habit she hadn't gotten around to dropping, like a nail-biting thing, not a decision.

It took me embarrassingly long to understand it as a decision. Sometime last winter I picked the pad up off the counter, mostly out of boredom, and started flipping back through old pages she'd torn off and kept in a drawer instead of throwing away — hundreds of them, rubber-banded, going back years. Years I hadn't spoken more than ten words of Korean to her. She'd kept writing anyway. Not to teach me, not really, since I wasn't reading them. More like she was keeping a door propped open in case I ever wanted to walk back through it, even if that meant propping it open alone for a really long time.

I read one word off that pad every morning now. I say it back to her, badly, and she corrects me, and some mornings that's the only Korean either of us speaks all day, which still isn't much. But it's not nothing. That notepad is maybe forty cents' worth of paper and it sat by our toaster through four years of me deciding our first language was something to leave behind, and it was still there when I decided otherwise. I don't think I'd have found my way back at all without it just sitting there, boring and small and stubborn, waiting.
