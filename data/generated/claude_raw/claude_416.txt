There is a green iron chair on the terrace of our building, and the paint has come off it in patches, so from a distance it looks like a map of a country nobody has named. It belonged to the family in 402, who left for Pune when I was in fourth standard and never came back for it. Every evening I carry it about two feet to the left so it stays in the last of the sun. That chair, and the twenty square feet of concrete around it, is where I feel most like myself.

The terrace is not a nice place. Three clotheslines, a cooler with its fan pulled out, pigeons who consider the water tank their property. But it is the only address in my life where nobody is watching me become anything. Downstairs I am a boy who has to answer about marks. At school I am a boy who is expected to have an opinion ready, quickly, in a corridor, between periods. Up there I am allowed to be slow.

Ishaan has been coming up with me since we were eleven. He sits on the plastic stool because he says the iron one gets hot. In March we had an argument on that terrace that I still have not finished thinking about. It started as nothing, something his uncle had posted, something about a protest, and about who the people in it really were and who was paying them. I said his uncle was wrong. He said I only thought that because of what I read, and that my family had never had to worry about anything the way his had.

I do not remember all of what I said next. I remember that I stood up and the chair scraped, and that the scrape was louder than either of us. I remember wanting very badly to win, and noticing that I wanted to win more than I wanted to be right. He went down the stairs and did not slam the door, which was worse.

He came back four days later with two Frooti packets and sat on the plastic stool without saying anything about it, and we watched a wedding tent going up in the plot behind our building. Neither of us has changed our mind. That is the honest part. I still think his uncle is wrong. He still thinks I am comfortable in a way that makes me careless. We just have somewhere to put it now.

What I understood, sitting back down on the warm iron, is that the terrace works for me because it is a place where I do not have to finish. In a classroom an argument ends when the bell goes and somebody has been shown to be correct. On the terrace an argument can just be left there, next to the broken cooler, and picked up in August. I get to say a thing badly, hear how it sounds outside my head, and take it back without an audience.

So the chair is not a symbol of anything. It is a chair, and it is slightly too low, and one leg has been standing in the same rust stain for years. But when I move it into the sun I am telling myself that I have an hour in which I am not required to be sure of anything. That is the closest I get to being myself.
