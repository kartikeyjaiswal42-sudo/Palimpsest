My grandmother and I have been arguing for four years about a notebook. It is a plain black composition book, half-filled with my handwriting, and she hates it more than she hates the parking meters outside her apartment, more than she hates the neighbor's dog. Every time I bring it into her kitchen she sighs the specific sigh she reserves for things she considers a small betrayal.

She came to live with us the summer I was thirteen, after my grandfather died, carrying two suitcases and a language I did not speak and she barely spoke back. My Cantonese was kitchen Cantonese, a few nouns and please and thank you. Her English was numbers, some curse words she'd picked up from my uncle, and the word "no," which she deployed with tremendous versatility. We should have had nothing to say to each other. Instead we had the stove.

She started teaching me by putting a knife in my hand and moving it herself, her fingers closed over mine, guiding the angle until I could feel in my wrist what she meant instead of hearing it. She never once told me a temperature or a time. She would touch the side of the wok, listen to the oil, watch the color of the scallions, and nod, and that nod was the whole recipe. When I asked how much ginger, she held up her hand and shrugged, which I eventually understood to mean: enough. Enough is not a measurement I could write down, but I tried anyway.

That's where the notebook started, and where the disagreement started with it. I wanted to fix what she was doing so it wouldn't disappear when she did. She wanted the opposite — she wanted it to live only in hands, mine included, the way it had lived in hers since her mother taught her in a village kitchen with no electricity. To her, writing it down was a kind of coffin. To me, not writing it down felt like standing next to something on fire and doing nothing.

We have never settled this. I still write in the notebook when she isn't looking, sneaking notes about ratios she'd deny existed. She still finds it sometimes and makes the sigh. Last spring I tried to explain, badly, in the mixed language we'd built between us, that I was scared of forgetting her exact way of doing things once she wasn't there to correct me. She went quiet for a long moment, then said, in English, "You already know it," and put more oil in the pan like the conversation was over.

It wasn't over. It still isn't. But somewhere in four years of failing to win that argument, I learned to cook — really cook, the kind where you stop checking a page and start checking the food — and I learned it from someone who gave me hours of her attention with almost no shared vocabulary, which is a strange, specific kind of generosity I did not expect and have not gotten over. I don't know if I'll ever convince her that the notebook matters. I do know that whatever she taught me is going to outlast both of us arguing about it, on paper or off.
