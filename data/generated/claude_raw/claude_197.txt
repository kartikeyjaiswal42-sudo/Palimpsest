Mr. Halvorsen's rule was you didn't touch the MIG gun until he'd watched you dry-run the joint three times. I hated that rule. I was fifteen, I'd wanted to weld since I was about nine years old and watched my uncle patch a trailer hitch in our driveway, sparks going everywhere, and here was this guy making me practice moving a fake torch over a piece of scrap steel like some kind of ritual. Felt like a waste of a class period. I wanted to burn metal, not pretend to.

So the first time he actually let me strike an arc, I rushed it. Didn't hold the angle he'd shown me — dragged the gun instead of pushing it, which he'd explained maybe four times, and which I'd nodded along to without really absorbing. The bead came out ugly. Humped in the middle, undercut on one side, the kind of weld that looks like a scab. He picked it up, turned it over once, didn't say anything for a second, then handed it back and said, "Grind it off and do it again." Not mean about it. Just flat.

I did that probably nine times that semester. Not exaggerating much. Same mistake, roughly — I'd get impatient two seconds into the bead and speed up, and the whole thing would go wrong right where I sped up, like the metal was keeping score. Somewhere around attempt six I started getting mad about it, at him a little, mostly at myself, because I could see exactly what I was doing wrong while I was doing it and I still couldn't make my hand slow down.

What finally worked wasn't advice, honestly, or not new advice anyway. He'd said the same three things to me the whole time. What worked was he stopped fixing it for me. He'd walk by, glance at a bad weld, and just keep walking. No comment. And somehow that was worse than the grinding-it-off speech, because now I had to decide for myself whether it was good enough, and I couldn't lie to myself the way I could sort of half-convince a teacher.

By December I could lay a bead that didn't look like it belonged in a horror movie. Nothing fancy — a flat fillet weld, nothing structural, nothing anyone would trust to hold up a bridge. But consistent. And I still think about the gap between knowing what I was doing wrong and actually being able to stop doing it, because those turned out to be completely different skills, and nobody had told me that going in. I'd assumed if you could name the mistake you were basically done fixing it. You're not. Not even close.

I think about it more than I probably should for something that's just a welding class. I catch myself doing the same thing in other places now — rushing the part where I already know I should slow down, math homework, an essay draft, whatever — and some part of my brain goes back to that ugly humped bead and Mr. Halvorsen not saying anything, just walking past. It's a weirdly specific memory to keep returning to, a guy in safety glasses not commenting on a piece of scrap steel, but it's the one that actually stuck, more than any speech anyone gave me about effort or grit or whatever word gets used for this stuff. I still have the bad one, the sixth attempt, sitting in a drawer in the garage. I don't know why I kept it instead of the good ones. Maybe because it's the one that's honest about how long it actually took.
