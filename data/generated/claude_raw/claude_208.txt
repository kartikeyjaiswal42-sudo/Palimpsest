The podcast was called Second Shift and it ran for four episodes. My cousin Elias and I recorded it in his mother's laundry room on a borrowed microphone, on a folded quilt to kill the echo, and the premise was that we would interview people in our town about the job they had before the one they have now. Episode one was a woman who had been a court stenographer and now runs the bakery on Delmar. Episode two was a man who repaired pinball machines and now sells insurance. Three was my own grandfather, who left a shipyard in 1979. Four was a substitute teacher who had spent eleven years as a long-haul driver, and it is the best thing either of us has made.

Then it stopped. Not with a decision. We recorded a fifth in November, and I never edited it, and Elias asked about it in December and I said soon, and he asked in January and I said soon, and in February he stopped asking. The file is still on my laptop. It is forty-one minutes long and it is fine.

I want to describe the failure accurately, because for a long time I described it wrong. For most of a year I said we ran out of time, which is the version that lets me off. What actually happened is that Elias and I disagreed about what the show was, and neither of us ever said so out loud, and the disagreement ate it.

Elias thought we were making a document. He wanted to talk to anybody who had a before, and he did not care whether the episode was good, because the point was that the interview existed and would exist after the person didn't. He said this once, sort of, about my grandfather — that he was glad we had his voice on a hard drive. I thought we were making a show. I wanted the fourth episode standard every time. I wanted the pacing right and the cold open sharp and I wanted people who were not related to us to listen to it, and forty-seven people did, and I checked that number more than I have admitted to anyone.

So when the fifth interview was fine and not good, I did not edit it, and I told myself it was a scheduling problem. Elias would have released it that week. He would have said that a fine conversation with a man who used to install church organs is worth more on the internet than in a folder. And he would have been right about that, and I would still not have released it, and the fact that I can hold both of those at once is exactly why I never brought it up.

What I did next was not noble. I let it die and I let him think I was busy. That went on for months. The thing that broke it was our grandmother's birthday in July, where the whole family was in one backyard, and my aunt asked if the podcast was still going, and Elias said "ask him," and it was not cruel, it was just tired, and I heard it.

I drove over the next week and we sat in the laundry room, which still had the quilt in it. I told him the truth, which is that I had killed it on purpose because I did not think episode five was good enough and I had not wanted to say that to him about an interview he had set up. He said he already knew. He said the part he minded was not the standard, it was the eight months of soon.

We did not resolve the disagreement. That is the honest ending. He still thinks I care too much whether a thing is good, and that this makes me slow and a little bit of a coward, and he says so cheerfully. I still think a document nobody finishes listening to is not a document, and that his version would have produced forty episodes nobody remembers. We have argued about it since, in a way that is now enjoyable, which is different from resolved.

What changed is procedural. We restarted in September under a rule we both agreed to: he publishes, I edit, and I get one veto per season and have to use it in writing within four days. I have used it once. I have released three episodes since that I would not have released before, and one of them, about a man who used to tune pianos for a funeral home, is better than anything from the first run, and I would have buried it.

I do not think I was right and then wrong. I think I was right about the standard and wrong about what I owed the person holding the other end of it, and the second thing was the one that cost us a year.
