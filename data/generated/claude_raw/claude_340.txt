My father sells cut flowers on Sundays at the market by the river, and from the age of twelve I went with him because there was nobody at home to leave me with. For four years I thought of this as something that was happening to me. I woke at half past four. I carried buckets. I stood behind a trestle table for six hours in whatever weather there was, and when people asked me what I did at weekends I said nothing much, because saying I worked on my dad's flower stall felt like admitting something.

I was good at the work and I resented being good at it. I could wrap a bunch in paper in about nine seconds. I knew that lilies had to go at the back because the pollen stains, that the tulips would open too far if you left them in the sun and then nobody wanted them, that a bucket of gypsophila sells out on the last Sunday before exams because parents buy it for teachers. None of this seemed like knowledge. It seemed like the residue of a Sunday I had not chosen.

The thing that changed my mind was not dramatic and I have thought about that a lot since. It happened in October last year. My father had a kidney stone and could not lift anything, so he sat on a folding chair at the end of the table and I ran the stall. Around eleven a woman came and stood in front of the roses for a long time without touching them. I asked if she wanted a hand. She said her mother had died on Thursday and she did not know what you were supposed to buy.

I did not know what you were supposed to buy either. But I had watched my father do this maybe two hundred times, and what he did was never to answer the question directly. So I asked her what her mother had grown in her garden. She said sweet peas, and a rose that was a horrible orange colour that everybody had hated. And I said we did not have sweet peas in October but we had freesias, which smell like them, and we did have a spray rose in a sort of apricot that was arguably horrible. She laughed in the way people do when they have been crying. We put them together on the table. It took about fifteen minutes. She paid nine pounds fifty.

After she left, my father, who had heard all of it from his chair, said: that is the job. Not the flowers. That.

I have turned that over more than I expected to. For four years I had been measuring the stall by the wrong thing entirely. I thought the skill was the wrapping and the arithmetic and the not getting cold, and those are skills, but they are the ones that would be replaced tomorrow by a machine and nobody would mind. What I had actually been learning, in a slow and unnoticed way, was how to stand opposite a stranger who wants something they cannot name, and help them find it without making them explain themselves. That is not a Sunday job. That is most jobs, and most of the good parts of being a person.

What grew out of it is a bit more embarrassing to describe. I got interested. I started asking my father why he buys what he buys, and it turns out he has an entire theory of the market — who is buying for a birthday, who is buying because they feel guilty, why the stall two down does better on Mother's Day and worse every other week of the year. He has never written any of it down. He left school at sixteen. He is, without any doubt, the most careful observer of people I have ever met, and I had spent four years standing beside him wishing I were somewhere else.

I still wake at half past four on Sundays. I have stopped saying nothing much.
