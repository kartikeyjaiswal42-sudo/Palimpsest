The belief I grew up inside was that sleep is the first thing you spend. Nobody in my family ever said it as a sentence, but it was in everything. My father sleeping four hours in the harvest weeks. The cousin who cleared his exam and whose whole legend was that he studied until three. At my coaching class there was a boy who kept a tally on the last page of his register of how few hours he had managed, and we admired him for it, and I was one of the people who admired him.

In October of eleventh standard I stopped being able to sleep, and it lasted about a year. It did not feel dramatic. I would lie down at eleven, do nothing wrong, and at twenty to three I would still be lying there with my eyes open, listening to the fridge. Then the alarm at five past five, and a day where I could read a paragraph four times and hold none of it. My marks fell from the high eighties to the low seventies over two terms, which is not a catastrophe, but it was the first time I had ever gone down.

This is where I questioned the belief, and this is also where I got it wrong. I decided, correctly, that sleep was not a luxury and that the tally boy was an idiot. Then I did something that took me a long time to see clearly: I attacked the sleeping the same way I had been taught to attack everything. I made a schedule. I set a target of eight hours and got into bed at half past nine to give myself margin. I bought melatonin from the chemist near the bus stand, five milligrams, without telling anybody, on the advice of a video. I lay in bed on Sunday mornings until noon to make up the debt. Every one of those was wrong, and the last two were wrong in a way that made the whole thing worse, and I kept at them for months because stopping felt like giving up.

What finally moved me was a chapter in a library book about how the body builds pressure to sleep across the hours you are awake, and how lying in bed awake teaches you to be awake there. Nine and a half hours in bed to get five hours of sleep was not margin. It was the problem. I cut my time in bed down to six and a half hours, deliberately, which felt insane, and got up at the same time every day including Sundays, and got out of bed when I had been awake twenty minutes. It took about seven weeks. It was not a cure. I still have bad weeks, usually before exams, and last month I had four bad nights in a row.

The outcome I am supposed to write about is that I sleep better now and I understand something about the body. That is true and it is not the part I think about. What I think about is a Tuesday in ninth standard, before any of this, when my cousin Aarti, who is two years younger and takes me far too seriously, asked me how I managed my time. I told her that sleep is for after boards. I said it the way you repeat something clever you have heard, and she wrote it in the front of her notebook, and I saw her write it and felt good about it. She is in eleventh now. I have told her the other thing since, twice, and both times it came out sounding like an older brother being boring, which is the trouble with corrections.

So the belief I challenged was not really the one about sleep. It was the belief that a person can push through anything, that the body is a subordinate, that if something is not working you have not applied enough force. I still catch myself believing it. I caught myself believing it while writing this, at half past midnight, on a school night.
