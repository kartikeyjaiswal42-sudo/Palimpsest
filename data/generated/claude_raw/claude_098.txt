My grandfather tells stories about a place that doesn't exist anymore, and I don't know how to tell him that I'm not sure I believe they're real.

He came to live with us two years ago after my grandmother died, and he came with this entire archive of stories about Kasoor, a village in what is now Pakistan that his family left during partition. He has a thousand details—the way the monsoon rains sounded on the tin roof of his house, the exact sequence of streets you'd walk to get to the market, the name of the boy who was his best friend when he was seven years old. He tells these stories like they're the most important thing he has, like they're keeping something alive.

My mother listens to these stories and cries sometimes. They're tied to her sense of who she is, where she comes from. For her, the stories are a connection to a past she never knew directly but knows through him. She'll prompt him with questions. "Grandfather, tell them about the orchard," and he'll launch into a twenty-minute story about apple trees and his cousins and a specific afternoon that he somehow remembers with photographic detail.

I listen too, but I'm listening differently. I'm seventeen years old. I was born in America. I have no connection to Kasoor except through these stories, and I've started to wonder if the stories are accurate or if they've been polished and reshaped over seventy years until they've become more like historical fiction than memoir. I wonder if he remembers what actually happened or if he remembers a version of what happened that he's told himself enough times that it's become real.

I made the mistake of asking him once. We were alone, and he was telling me about his school, the specific layout of the classrooms, the teacher's name, and I asked him how he remembered these details so exactly. Didn't memories change? Didn't they get distorted? He got very quiet. He said that when you lose a place forever, you hold onto the details like they're the last thing keeping it alive. That if he stopped remembering exactly how the light came through the windows in his school, then the place truly would be gone, not just physically but completely.

My mother was upset when I told her about that conversation. She said I was being insensitive, that I didn't understand what it meant to have lost something so fundamental. She said his memories weren't less real because they were in his head instead of on the land. They were still true. They still mattered.

But I don't think that's what bothers me. I think what bothers me is that I can't connect to this history the way she does. I listen to the stories and they're interesting, but they don't feel like they're about me. And there's this unspoken expectation that they should, that this village and his loss should matter to me as much as it matters to him and to my mother.

My grandfather has never said it directly, but I know he wants me to carry these stories forward. He wants them to mean something to me so that they don't die when he dies. He wants me to be connected to this place that only exists in his memory now.

I don't know how to tell him that I'm already so disconnected from it that asking me to hold onto the stories feels like asking me to memorize someone else's dreams. I have roots in America. I have a different history. I love him, and I do listen to the stories, but they don't anchor me the way they anchor him.

We haven't said this to each other. My mother would be hurt if I tried. My grandfather would be disappointed. So I listen, and I record some of them on my phone without him knowing, because I know that someday when he's gone, I might regret not having them. But I resent being expected to feel the loss of a place I never knew, to feel homesick for a home that was never mine.

This is the unresolved part. I know he's dying, probably within the next few years, and I know the stories will be gone when he is. I know my mother wants me to step in and carry that responsibility. And I can't do it, not authentically. I can only pretend, and pretending feels like disrespect.
