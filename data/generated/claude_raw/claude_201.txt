The tuning fork is cold against my palm even in August, when the sanctuary's air conditioning fights the heat outside and mostly loses, and I strike it against my knee the way Mrs. Castellano taught me freshman year, then press the stem to my front teeth so the hum runs straight into my skull instead of getting lost in the room's noise. A440. The other altos are still filing into the loft with their folders, and I'm off to the side doing this small private ritual that has nothing to do with God and everything to do with the fact that I can feel a pitch in my jaw before I properly hear it in my ears, which is a thing I have never stopped finding strange.

I should say plainly that I don't believe any of what we sing. I joined because the choir was short two altos the fall of freshman year and Mrs. Castellano cornered me after a school assembly where I'd been humming along without realizing it, and by the time I understood what the words in the hymnal were actually asking me to believe, I was already too far in to quit gracefully, and also, if I'm honest, I didn't want to. Nobody in the loft has ever asked me to pray and mean it. They've only ever asked me to listen, and listening turned out to be the part I couldn't stop doing.

Here is what happens, on the good nights, maybe one rehearsal in four: all four parts lock into a chord, really lock, not just correct but tuned to each other instead of to a piano, and a fifth note appears that nobody is singing. It sits low, underneath the sopranos, a hum with no mouth behind it, and the first time I heard it I turned around convinced someone in the back had joined in on a note that wasn't written. Nobody had. It's called a combination tone, or sometimes a Tartini tone, and it happens when two frequencies are close enough to a clean ratio that the difference between them becomes audible on its own, a note built entirely out of math, summoned by four teenagers holding their pitch steady enough not to wobble it away.

I have chased that sound in a way I've never chased anything assigned to me in a classroom. I hold the tuning fork after rehearsal, once everyone else is stacking chairs and talking about the parking lot, and I test intervals against it, a fifth, a fourth, a major third, trying to feel where the ratio goes clean and the ghost note threatens to show up. I emailed Mr. Osei, my physics teacher, over winter break with a question about beat frequencies that turned into a forty-minute conversation the first day back, and he loaned me a battered library book on the physics of just intonation that I have renewed six times and probably owe him an apology for. I've watched more acoustics lectures on my phone at 1 a.m. than I'd ever admit to a teacher directly, chasing the same handful of seconds, the four-part chord resolving into a fifth part that isn't there.

Nobody in the loft is thinking about ratios when it happens. They're thinking about the hymn, about their grandmother in the third pew, about getting the soprano entrance right. I'm standing there with a tuning fork in my blazer pocket, mouthing words I don't believe, waiting for four voices to accidentally build a note out of nothing, and for those few seconds I could not tell you what time it is or how long I've been standing there or whether the sermon that follows will be forty minutes or ninety. I only notice again once the chord breaks apart and the ghost note goes with it, and I'm back to being a girl in a loft on a Sunday, tuning fork already cooling in my hand, already thinking about the next hymn on the page.
