We moved from Manila to a town outside Sacramento in January of my sophomore year, which is the worst possible month to arrive anywhere, and the thing I got wrong took me about two years to see and I still think about it most weeks.

What I got wrong was my mother.

The obvious story is about me. Fifteen, halfway through a school year, dropped into a building where everyone had known each other since they were six. I had the accent. I had the wrong shoes, which I did not understand until March. I ate lunch in the library for eleven weeks and told my parents I was eating with people, which they believed because they wanted to and because they were drowning in their own version of it.

I was miserable and I was extremely loud about being miserable. Not to my friends — I did not have any — but at home. Every evening. I had a whole argument prepared and I delivered it in pieces over about four months: that they had asked me nothing, that a person's life is not a suitcase you can pack, that they had traded my last two years of school for a job my father could have found in Manila if he had actually looked.

That last one was untrue and I knew it was untrue when I said it, which is a particular kind of cruelty that teenagers are unusually good at.

My mother was a paediatrician in Manila. She had a practice with two other doctors and a waiting room with a fish tank in it that I remember from being small. In California her licence was worth nothing without a process that would take, she was told, somewhere between three and six years — exams, a residency she would have to compete for at forty-four against people half her age. She took a job doing medical records at a clinic in Roseville. Data entry, essentially, at a desk without a window, for a fraction of what she had made.

She did not tell me any of that. She told me she was working at a clinic. That was the sentence, and I accepted it for two years, because I was fifteen and it fit the shape of what I expected — that adults have jobs and the job is a detail.

I found out in my senior year, from a cousin, at a birthday party, who mentioned it the way you mention weather. Your mom's studying for her boards again, right? Third time?

Third time.

I had been in that house for two years. I had heard her get up at four in the morning and I had assumed she was a person who got up early. I had complained to her, specifically to her, at that table, about how the move had ruined my life, and she had listened to all of it, every night, and said sorry, and asked whether I wanted more rice.

The thing I got wrong was not that I was sad. I was allowed to be sad. What I got wrong was that I assumed I was the only person in the house who had lost something, and I built that assumption out of nothing except my own volume. Nobody else in that house was talking about what it cost them, and I read the silence as evidence there was nothing to say.

I have not been able to fix this, exactly. I did not have a scene with her where I apologised and we cried; when I tried, about a week after the party, she waved it off and changed the subject, which I have come to understand was not forgiveness but a refusal to let me make it about me again.

What changed instead is smaller and permanent. I now assume, as a rule, that quiet people in a room are carrying something and that I will not find out what it is by waiting. I ask my father about his work. I ask my sister direct questions she does not want. It is often awkward and about half the time there is nothing there.

My mother passed her exam last spring, on the fourth attempt. She is doing a residency now, nights, at forty-seven. She still has not told me about the first three times. I found out about the fourth from a form on the counter.
