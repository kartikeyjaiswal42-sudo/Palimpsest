My mom's clothing business was an accident. That's how she always tells it. She didn't wake up one morning and think "I'm going to buy and sell used clothes on eBay." What actually happened is that she cleaned out her closet one Saturday and had this pile of things she'd never wear again. She'd gained weight after having me. Her style changed. Some of it was just from 2005 and didn't fit anymore. And she realized that selling it online might make a few hundred bucks for a trip or something.

She made fifteen hundred dollars in the first month. She couldn't believe it. People, apparently, wanted vintage Banana Republic and barely-worn coach bags from the 90s and early 2000s. She kept the business going, started going to thrift stores and estate sales, buying things cheap and reselling them for a markup. Within a year she'd turned it into an actual business with structure and goals. She had inventory organized in our basement with a whole system—things sorted by condition, by season, by brand. She'd wake up at five in the morning to take photos and list things. She was making real money—like, genuinely more than her part-time job was bringing in. My dad thought it was funny at first, then he thought it was impressive, then he stopped thinking about it altogether because it had become normal.

For about two years, the business was everything. She talked about profit margins at dinner. She'd calculate the cost per item and spend actual hours researching comps on eBay to make sure she was pricing things right. It was ambitious and kind of beautiful—she was building something. Making something. And it was working. We had more money. She had this thing that was hers. My younger brother joked that she loved her business more than she loved us.

I believed him completely. I thought she was avoiding her actual life by hiding in the basement with packages and business spreadsheets. I'd ask her to come to my soccer games or my school events and she'd be there physically but distracted, thinking about inventory numbers and profit margins. She'd miss dinner sometimes because she was at Goodwill hunting for deals on a Thursday night instead of being home with us. It felt selfish to me. Not evil or anything. Just selfish. Like she'd found something more interesting than us.

Then one day she just quit. She listed everything she had at basically break-even prices on the Facebook marketplace and got rid of it all in about two weeks. She was done. She said she didn't know why anymore. That she'd lost interest. My dad thought she was crazy. I thought maybe she'd finally realized what everyone had been trying to tell her—that her family was more important than a side hustle.

But here's the thing I didn't get at the time. She wasn't just quitting a business. She was grieving something much deeper. She was grieving the person she thought she could be. The version of her that was an entrepreneur, that could build something from nothing, that had these hours that belonged just to her and her own ambitions. The business had mattered to her in a way that seemed to threaten all of us. And she'd killed it because she felt like she was supposed to—like being a good mother meant abandoning that version of herself. Not because she'd stopped loving it. The opposite. She loved it so much that she felt guilty.

Years later we talked about it. She said she'd made a mistake. That she shouldn't have listened to the guilt she felt about choosing something for herself. That maybe if she'd kept going, if she hadn't let everyone make her feel like she was failing as a mother, she wouldn't spend so much time now wondering what happened to her. Not to the business—to her. To the person who wanted to build something and thought she could.

I think about that a lot. About how sometimes the unsuccessful thing is just quitting when things get hard or feel wrong. And sometimes the unsuccessful thing is not even trying, because you convince yourself you're not allowed.
