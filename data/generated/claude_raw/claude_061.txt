The object is a chewed-up plastic dinosaur, a triceratops missing one horn, that lived on my desk for the better part of a year while I taught myself to code. It belonged to my cousin Theo, who is seven, who handed it to me one afternoon and said it was for luck, and who is the entire reason I know anything about programming at all.

Theo has a rare condition that keeps him out of school more than most kids, long stretches at home some months, and he gets bored in a way that I think most people don't fully understand until they've watched a smart, restless kid stare at a ceiling for the fourth straight day with nothing new to do. I visited him one weekend and he was going on about a video game he liked, the way seven-year-olds go on about things, total unfiltered enthusiasm, and I said, half joking, that I could probably make him his own game if he told me what he wanted in it.

I had never written a line of code in my life. I said it anyway, because his face did something when I said it that I didn't want to take back.

So I taught myself, starting that week, mostly from free tutorials and a very patient online forum full of strangers who apparently enjoy answering questions from confused seventeen-year-olds at eleven at night. I lost entire evenings to it without noticing, which had never really happened to me with schoolwork, ever — I'd sit down after dinner meaning to fix one small bug, and look up to find it was one in the morning and I'd forgotten to eat the sandwich sitting next to my keyboard. I don't fully know how to explain what pulled me in so completely except that programming turned out to be the first thing I'd tried where being wrong wasn't embarrassing, it was just information — the code either ran or it didn't, and if it didn't, there was always a reason, findable, fixable, not a judgment on me as a person.

Theo set the dinosaur on my desk on one of his visits, right next to my laptop, and told me it was watching to make sure I finished. I left it there after he went home. Every time I hit something I couldn't solve — and there was a lot I couldn't solve, for weeks at a stretch sometimes — I'd look at that missing horn and feel a specific, stupid pressure to not let a seven-year-old down.

The game I eventually built for him is not good by any real standard. It's a simple platformer where his character, based loosely on a description he gave me over the phone, jumps between clouds collecting the specific candy he likes best. It has bugs I never fixed. It crashes if you press two keys at exactly the same time. He has played it, by his own count, "a hundred million times," which I choose to believe.

When I want to learn more now, I go back to that same forum, and increasingly to a small local group of people my age who meet on Saturdays to build things together, trading tricks the way Theo trades stickers. But mostly I go back to him. I ask what he wants added next, and he tells me with total confidence, no hedging, the way only a seven-year-old can, and I go figure out how to actually build it.

The dinosaur sits on my desk now missing its second horn too — Theo again, a later visit, some story about a dinosaur fight I didn't fully follow. I haven't fixed it and I don't plan to. It doesn't need fixing to still be doing its job.
