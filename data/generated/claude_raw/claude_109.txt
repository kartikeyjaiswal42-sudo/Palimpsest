I entered the Hargrove Essay Prize under the name "M. Reyes" for three years running, starting in ninth grade, and I never told anyone, not even my best friend, until this year.

Here's the dumb origin of it. Freshman year I wrote something for the contest — an essay about my dad's job loss, pretty raw, more honest than anything I'd turned in for a grade before — and I chickened out at the submission form. There's a box where you type your name and it shows up on the printed program if you place, read out loud at the little ceremony they do in the library. I typed M. Reyes instead of my actual name, hit submit before I could think about it more, and then more or less forgot about it until the results went up and M. Reyes had gotten an honorable mention.

Nobody knew who M. Reyes was. A few people guessed, wrongly, at lunch. I sat there and said nothing, which felt strange and also kind of great, like getting away with something, except the thing I was getting away with was just being seen without being known.

So I did it again the next year. And the year after. Same fake name, three different essays, none of them about anything I would've felt okay putting my real name next to — the year my parents almost split up, a specific bad week with an eating thing I still don't fully talk about, a friendship that ended badly and mostly my fault. I placed all three years. Second, then first, then second again. The librarian who ran the contest, Ms. Ferris, pinned the print-outs to a corkboard by the front desk every spring, "M. Reyes" typed at the top, and I used to walk past it on purpose, most days, just to look at it for a second.

It wasn't really about winning. I think if I'm honest it was closer to the opposite — I wanted to say true things and not have anyone attach them to me walking down the hallway. M. Reyes could write about the eating thing and nobody looked at me differently at lunch the next day. That mattered more to me than the ribbon did.

This year I finally told Ms. Ferris, mostly by accident, mentioning something in passing that only M. Reyes could've known, and she went quiet for a second and then said she'd wondered for three years and had a running guess with another teacher, both wrong. She asked if I wanted my name on the board this time, for the essay I'd write senior year, and I said yes before I'd really decided anything, surprising myself.

I wrote about the eating thing again, for real this time, my actual name typed into the box, and I stood at that little library ceremony and read four minutes of my own handwriting out loud to about thirty people I see every day. My hands were shaking. It was worse than any test I've ever taken and also, somehow, the first time in four years I felt like the thing I'd written and the person standing there were actually the same person.

I still don't fully know what to make of the fact that it took a fake name, repeated three separate springs in a row, before I could get to that. But I think that's the part I'd actually bring somewhere new — not confidence, exactly, more like the long, slow habit of practicing honesty in private until you're ready to do it in front of people, one submission box at a time.
