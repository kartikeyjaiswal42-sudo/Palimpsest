My uncle had a farm two hours outside the city, and every summer starting when I was nine, my family would drive out there for a week. The farm was the kind of place where there was always work to do, and the work was often physical in a way that felt both tedious and necessary. By the time I was fifteen, I had done enough of the tasks to know the rhythms—when things needed watering, when animals needed feeding, when the fencing needed checking.

Learning to drive started as the smallest of habits. My uncle had a tractor, and he used it to pull a trailer around the property, moving equipment and feed and whatever else needed moving. One summer, he asked if I wanted to help, and helping meant driving the tractor while he guided from the back. I was fourteen, far too young to legally drive anywhere, but on his property, nobody was watching.

That first time, I was terrified. The tractor was huge and loud and nothing like the car I would eventually learn to drive in. My hands shook. I couldn't figure out the gears. The tractor lurched forward and I nearly threw my uncle off the trailer. He laughed and told me to try again. So I did.

The second time was less terrifying. The third time, I knew which gear I needed. By the end of that first summer, I could drive the tractor smoothly enough that my uncle stopped holding on and just sat in the trailer, letting me take him where I needed to go. It was the first time I had felt competent at anything physical, which sounds strange, but I had always been a clumsy kid. Bad at sports, bad at any activity that required coordination. But something about the tractor made sense to me.

For three summers, the first thing I did when we arrived at the farm was ask my uncle if he needed the tractor moved. He always did. There was always a reason to drive it—moving hay, clearing a field, pulling out stumps. It became this small routine between us. We didn't talk much while I drove. We just existed in the comfortable silence of people doing necessary work together.

I think what happened is that something in me shifted without me noticing. I stopped being the kid who was learning to drive the tractor and became the person who could drive the tractor well enough that my uncle trusted me with it completely. The habit wasn't just about the driving. It was about showing up every summer and doing the same task over and over until it stopped being a task and became just something I did.

This sounds small, like it doesn't matter. But when I actually learned to drive a car at sixteen, it was almost boring. I already knew how to operate a machine, how to think about space and speed and what was happening around me. I already understood that learning something took repetition and patience and that most days it wasn't going to feel dramatic or special.

My uncle died when I was seventeen, before I had a chance to show him that I had learned to drive a real car. He never got to sit in the passenger seat of something I was driving legally. But I think about those summers a lot, about how the most important thing I learned from him was that showing up over and over, doing the small things that nobody notices, is actually what makes you capable of doing the big things when they matter.
