I started a radio station at my school in tenth grade, WKHS, broadcasting over a low-power FM signal that reached, by my best estimate, about a six-block radius, and for the first four months almost nobody listened to it.

I got the idea after finding a box of old broadcast equipment in a storage closet near the auditorium — a soundboard, some tangled cables, a transmitter that technically still worked once I got it cleaned up and running again. The school had apparently had a radio program decades earlier that quietly died out sometime in the nineties, according to a yearbook I found referencing it. I petitioned the administration to let me revive it, sat through two meetings I was fairly sure would end in a no, got a faculty sponsor who mostly let me run things myself, and spent that whole fall building a small schedule from scratch: a morning announcements slot, a music hour after school, a Friday sports recap nobody had asked for.

For those first four months, our listener numbers, as far as I could tell, hovered around nobody. I checked a stream counter I'd rigged up and most days it read zero or one, and the one was probably me testing it from my phone in the hallway. I remember sitting in the broadcast closet reading sports scores into a microphone, alone, for an audience I was fairly sure didn't exist, and thinking the whole project had been a mistake — that I'd spent a semester's worth of afternoons building something nobody wanted, and that the smart move was to shut it down before I wasted more time on it.

The moment that changed my mind happened on an ordinary Tuesday, not a dramatic one. I was doing the after-school music slot, and a kid I didn't really know, a freshman, poked his head into the broadcast closet and asked if I could play a request. He said he listened most days while he did homework in the library next door, where you could apparently pick up the signal clearly. I hadn't known anyone could hear us clearly from there. I played his song, some pop track I'd never heard of, and he left, and that was basically the whole interaction.

But something in me shifted after that. I'd been measuring the station by a number on a screen, and the number had told me it was a failure. The freshman told me something the number couldn't: that even one real person, doing homework in a library, with the station on in the background because it made the room feel less quiet, was worth more than I'd been giving the whole project credit for. I stopped checking the counter as often after that. I started asking around instead, and found out slowly that there were maybe fifteen or twenty regular listeners scattered around campus, mostly in the library and the art room, none of whom had ever told me because there was no reason for them to.

The station is still running now, two years later, still small, still not something most of the school would say defines it. But I think about that freshman a lot, because he's the reason I didn't shut it down in November of tenth grade, and everything that came after — new hosts, a bigger schedule, a listener base you could maybe still count on two hands and some toes — grew out of that one Tuesday when I decided a library full of one quiet, half-listening kid mattered more than a stream counter reading zero.
