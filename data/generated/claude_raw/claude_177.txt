I got the internship at Bell Voss Engineering through my uncle, who knows one of the partners, and I spent the first two weeks of it doing almost nothing. I mean that almost literally. I organized a supply closet once. I watched three CAD tutorials I could've watched at home for free. Mostly I sat at a desk that used to belong to someone named Carl — his nameplate was still in the drawer — refreshing my email and waiting for a task that never came.

My supervisor was a structural engineer named Priya Anand, maybe thirty-five, sharp, calm, the kind of person who says exactly as much as she means to and not one word more. I liked her immediately. I respected her professionally in a way I didn't respect most adults yet, honestly, because she was clearly good at her actual job, not just good at managing a teenager. So when I finally worked up the nerve, at the start of week three, to ask her directly why I wasn't being given anything real to do, I expected either an apology or a fix. I got neither. She told me, pretty evenly, that sometimes the job is waiting — that a firm mid-project doesn't always have a clean task to hand a seventeen-year-old, and that patience was part of what she was actually testing.

I did not like that answer. At all. It felt like a nice way of saying deal with it, and I was seventeen and annoyed and fairly sure I knew better, so I did something I still think about more than almost anything else from that summer: I emailed one of the partners directly, a man I'd met exactly once, and asked — politely, I thought, though rereading it later I'm not sure "polite" is the word — whether there might be more substantive project work available, since my current assignment wasn't using my skills.

He forwarded it to Priya. Of course he did. Why wouldn't he.

She came to my desk that afternoon, and she wasn't angry, which somehow made it so much worse. She just asked why I hadn't come to her again first if I still had concerns after our conversation, and I didn't have a real answer, because the honest one — that I didn't trust her answer, that I thought going over her head would get results faster than waiting on her — wasn't something I was proud enough of to say out loud. She told me, still calm, still not raising her voice at all, that she'd now have to explain to a partner why her intern had gone around her in week three, and that this wasn't actually about me.

Nothing dramatic happened after that. I wasn't fired. I wasn't even really punished, not officially. But something in how she talked to me shifted, just slightly, for the rest of the summer — more formal, a little more distant — and I don't blame her one bit.

Here's the part I keep turning over. About two weeks later, real work did show up, a drainage assessment for a site near the highway, and it turned out the reason nothing had come earlier wasn't some test of my patience at all — the project had genuinely been stuck in permitting, for reasons that had nothing to do with me and that she couldn't have explained without breaking client confidentiality. She'd been telling me the truth the whole time. I just hadn't been willing to sit inside an answer I didn't like long enough to find that out.

I think about that email a lot. Not because it ended my internship or ruined anything permanent — it didn't — but because it's the clearest example I have of a specific kind of mistake: mistaking my own impatience for good judgment, and being willing to undercut someone I actually respected rather than just sit with not knowing for two more weeks.
