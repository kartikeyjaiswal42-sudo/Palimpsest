There is a table in the reference section of the public library on Marsh Street, second floor, third from the window, and the leg on the far side is short so it rocks unless you fold a bus ticket under it. I have folded a bus ticket under that leg perhaps four hundred times. It is where I feel most like myself, and explaining why means explaining a failure first.

I trained for the national mathematics olympiad for two years and I did not qualify. Not narrowly, either. In the round that mattered I solved one problem out of four and made a sign error in the second that I noticed on the bus home with the sick, precise clarity you only get about twenty minutes too late. There were about a hundred and fifty people who did better. I had genuinely believed I would be one of them.

The training was a habit before it was an ambition. Saturdays, half past nine, the Marsh Street library, because our flat has four people in it and one of them is a seven-year-old. I went because it was quiet. Then I went because I had started an old problem book and wanted to see the end of the geometry chapter. Then I went because it was Saturday and that is where I go on Saturdays. Ticket under the leg, coat on the back of the chair, phone in the bag rather than the pocket, and then four hours.

What I did in those four hours was mostly fail. That is what olympiad practice is, and nobody tells you beforehand how much of your life it will consist of sitting still while nothing happens. A hard problem gives you nothing for ninety minutes. You try the obvious thing and it collapses. You try the clever thing and it turns out to be the obvious thing wearing a hat. Occasionally, maybe once a session, something opens and you get twenty minutes of the best feeling I know of.

After I failed I stopped going for five weeks. I told myself I was busy. What was happening was that the table had become a symbol of the thing I had not achieved, and sitting at it felt like paying for something I had already lost.

I went back in November because I could not stand the flat. And here is the point: within about forty minutes I was completely happy, working on a problem that was not going to be marked by anybody, and I understood — sitting there with my coat still on — that I had been wrong for two years about what I was doing on Saturdays.

I had told myself the training was for the olympiad. It was not. The olympiad was the excuse that let me take the habit seriously; the habit was the thing I actually wanted. If someone had said at the start that I would spend two hundred Saturdays at that table and finish with no certificate and no place, I would have called that a waste, and I would have been wrong by a distance I find embarrassing.

So the library. Third table from the window. There is a man who reads the newspapers at the next table on Saturday mornings and we have exchanged perhaps nine words in three years. The reference section has an entire shelf of directories of British companies from 1998 that nobody will ever open again, and I find that comforting for reasons I cannot defend.

I feel most myself there because it is the one place where I am not being measured, and where being stuck is not a failure of state but the ordinary condition of the work. I know exactly how long I can hold my attention on something with no reward in sight, because I have tested it in that chair, and the answer is longer than I would have guessed and shorter than I would like.

I still have the sheet from the round I failed. I do not look at it much. But I am going back on Saturday, and there is a functional equations problem I have been carrying around since June, and I have not solved it, and I have a fairly strong feeling that this is going to be a good weekend.
