I got into sewing costumes for the Marlow Players the way most people get into anything at fifteen, which is that my mother volunteered me. Their wardrobe mistress had broken her wrist three weeks before a production of Blithe Spirit, and my mother, who had once heard me say I could use a sewing machine, offered my Saturdays without consulting me.

I could use a sewing machine in the sense that I had made a drawstring bag in Year 8.

For about eighteen months after that I thought of the whole thing as a chore I was too polite to get out of. I was good at it fairly quickly — I have decent hands and I can read a pattern — and I did not enjoy it. It was a church hall with a strip light that hummed, and a rail of donated clothing that smelled of other people's houses, and a group of adults between forty and seventy who had opinions about hemlines. I did what I was asked. I altered things. I turned a 1980s bridesmaid dress into something that could pass for 1927 if the audience was more than four rows back. I let out trousers for a man called Gordon three times in one season and did not ask why.

I told people I did it for the UCAS form. I think I believed that.

The thing that changed my mind happened during a production of A View from the Bridge, which is a play I did not know and had not read. I was making a work shirt for Eddie, the longshoreman, from a pattern I had drafted myself because we could not find anything that was right. The man playing him was called Ray, mid-fifties, a plumber, not a good actor in any technical sense — he had two volumes and a habit of looking at the floor on his own lines.

At the third fitting I had put the shirt on him and was pinning the shoulder, and I asked him to lift his arm so I could check the range, and he lifted it, and something happened to how he was standing. I do not know how to describe it better than that. He stopped being a plumber in a church hall wearing a shirt with pins in it. His weight went into his heels. His hands hung differently. He said, to nobody, "this is what he'd wear," and then he did about four lines of a scene we were nowhere near rehearsing, quietly, into the middle distance, and they were the first lines I had heard him say that sounded like a person.

He apologised afterwards. He said he'd got carried away.

I went home and thought about the shoulder seam. I had put it about two centimetres wider than his actual shoulder, deliberately, because a workman's shirt from 1955 is cut for movement and not for fit, and the effect of a wider shoulder seam is that the sleeve hangs from further out, and the arm swings from a different place, and you cannot hold yourself the way a man in a modern shirt holds himself. I had made a decision about a seam allowance and it had walked into the room ahead of him.

That was the turn. Before that I thought costume was decoration — the thing you put on top of the acting so the audience knows what year it is. After that I understood it as the opposite: a set of physical constraints and permissions applied to a body, which the body then has to answer. A corset does not make you look like a Victorian woman. It makes you breathe like one, and everything else follows from the breathing. A cassock does not signify a priest. It makes you take shorter steps.

I have done nine productions now. I have made a hooped skirt out of curtain wire and a great deal of swearing. I have learned to ask actors how they intend to move before I cut anything, which nobody taught me and which most amateur wardrobe departments do not do. I have started keeping a notebook of what I call solved problems: how to make a fat suit that does not slide, how to age a jacket with sandpaper and cold tea, how to build a quick change into a garment that also has to look like it has buttons.

I would like to study this properly, and I am aware that "I sew" sounds like a hobby. It is not a hobby. It is the study of how clothing writes instructions to a body, and I found it in a church hall under a humming light, and I did not notice what I had found for a year and a half because I was too busy resenting my mother for signing me up.

Gordon still needs his trousers let out. I still do not ask.
