There is a desk on the third floor of the Carnegie branch library, in the corner where the local history shelves meet the window, and it has a burn mark on it shaped roughly like Portugal. I have sat at that desk more hours than I have sat anywhere except my own bed. It is where I feel most like myself, and I have never been able to explain this to my mother without it turning into the argument we have been having for two years.

The desk is not special. The chair is bad. There is a radiator underneath it that makes a knocking sound in November. What the desk has is that nobody there knows me. The Carnegie is two bus routes from my school and serves a different part of town, and in three years of going I have run into exactly one person I knew, and she was there with her grandmother and did not see me. Everyone in that room is a stranger, and because they are strangers, I do not have to be the version of myself that everyone at school has already agreed on — the quiet one, the one who is good at maths, the one whose older brother was better at maths.

I wrote most of a novel at that desk when I was fifteen. It is bad, and I know it is bad, and I do not intend to show it to anyone. But it is 71,000 words and I finished it, which is more than the version of me that exists in a classroom would have believed.

Two years ago I entered a national essay competition from that desk. I entered it under a false name.

The name was Elena Marsh, which I chose because it sounded like someone who owned a coat. There is no Elena Marsh. I used an email address I had made for a video game account when I was twelve. The essay was about the way councils name new housing estates after the things they demolished to build them — Orchard Way, Mill View, Elmfield — and I had spent four months on it, at that desk, with the local history shelves right there, going through planning records that nobody had looked at in decades.

It came second. There was a small amount of money and a ceremony in London that Elena Marsh did not attend, and a note in a magazine that Elena Marsh did not respond to, and the money went to an account that does not exist. I got nothing. I have never regretted it.

My mother found out eight months later, because she found the printed copy in my room while looking for a phone charger, and she read it, and she was proud, and then she saw the name and did not understand, and then she did understand, and we have been arguing ever since.

Her position is this: you had something and you threw it away. That essay could have gone on a form. It could have been a line in an application, a thing a teacher could point to, evidence. She grew up in a place where opportunity was scarce and documented, where you did not get to be shy about a certificate, and she thinks that what I did was not modesty but a kind of cowardice dressed up as principle. She has said, more than once, that I am afraid of being seen and that I have built an entire personality out of that fear and called it integrity.

My position is harder to say and I am not sure it is right. I think I could only write that essay because nobody was going to know it was me. Not "it was easier" — I mean the sentences came out different. Sharper. I said things about the town I would not have said with my surname attached, because my surname is on a shop on Bridge Street and my father would have heard about it. Under Elena Marsh I was not anyone's son. I was just a person who had read the minutes of a 1974 planning committee and had an opinion.

My mother says that is a place you can visit but not live. I think she may be right about that. It is the part of her argument I cannot answer.

We have not resolved it. She still brings it up, usually sideways, usually in the kitchen. I still go to the Carnegie on Saturdays and sit at the desk with the Portugal-shaped burn, and I have started, cautiously, to write things I would sign.
