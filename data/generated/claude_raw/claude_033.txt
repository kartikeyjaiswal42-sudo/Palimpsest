My mother still keeps our old address written on a Post-it stuck inside a kitchen drawer in our new apartment, three thousand miles from the house it belongs to, and I found it by accident looking for a rubber band and didn't say anything about it, just closed the drawer and stood there for a second longer than I needed to.

We moved from outside Manchester to a suburb of Cleveland in October of my junior year, which is, as it turns out, an objectively terrible time to move a sixteen-year-old anywhere. My dad got the job offer in August. By October we were in a rental house with boxes still taped shut in the living room, and I was sitting in a new classroom being asked to introduce myself to twenty-six strangers while my accent, which I had never once thought about before, suddenly felt like the only thing about me anyone could see.

I don't lose track of time thinking about the move itself. What actually captivates me — and I recognize how strange this is going to sound — is the specific, almost geological process of how a place stops being home and a different place slowly starts being home instead, without your permission, without you noticing the exact day it happened. I've read more about migration psychology and identity formation in the last year than I've read for any actual class, which my history teacher, Mr. Alvarez, would probably find funny given how little I contributed to discussions on unrelated topics that semester. I found a study once, at eleven at night, about how people who relocate report "home" as a feeling that splits and exists in two places simultaneously for years, sometimes permanently, and I sat there reading footnotes until my dad knocked and asked why my light was still on.

My mom is the one I go to when I want to actually talk about it, which is strange, because she's also the one I fight with about it most.

She adjusted faster than I did, or she says she did, or maybe she just decided faster that adjusting was the correct thing to perform in front of me. She likes it here, genuinely, the space, the job my dad has, the quiet. I don't dislike it. That's not the disagreement. The disagreement is about whether it's allowed to still hurt this long later, whether missing a place is a thing with an expiration date on it that I've overrun.

"You have to let it go a little," she told me a few months ago, not unkindly, more like she was worried about me. "You can't keep living half in a place you don't live in anymore."

I told her I wasn't doing that. I was lying, a little. I still know the bus schedule to my old school by heart. I still, embarrassingly, sometimes go to type our old postcode into forms out of muscle memory. She thinks this means I haven't accepted where we are. I think it means something different — that a person can build a whole new life in a place and still keep a room, somewhere inside themselves, furnished exactly the way the old one was, and that this isn't a failure to move on, it's just what moving actually costs.

We haven't resolved this. We probably won't, not with a single conversation, maybe not ever completely, because I think we're both a little bit right and neither of us wants to fully concede the point. She wants me to be lighter about it than I am. I want her to admit that lightness isn't actually available to everyone on the same schedule.

What keeps me up reading isn't nostalgia, exactly. It's the mechanics of it — how identity apparently isn't a single location you can just swap out, how the research keeps using the word "liminal" in ways that finally made sense to me only after I lived it. I go looking for language that explains what's happening to me because nobody in my actual life, including my mother, including myself most days, has quite found it yet. The Post-it is still in the drawer. I don't think either of us is ready to throw it away.
