The rain gauge is nothing special to look at, a plastic tube on a metal stake with faded black markings for inches and tenths of inches, the kind you can buy at any hardware store for six dollars. My grandfather gave it to me the spring I decided to plant a real garden instead of the two sad tomato plants I'd kept alive in pots for years, and he told me the same thing he told me about most things, which is that you can't manage what you don't measure. I put it at the edge of the tomato bed and mostly ignored it for two months, because there was plenty of rain and the garden looked, by June, like something I could actually be proud of.

Then July came and the rain stopped almost entirely. I don't mean it slowed down. I mean I checked that gauge every morning for eleven days and it read zero, the plastic tube dry and a little dusty, and I could watch the soil in the beds pull away from the edges of the wood frames like it was trying to leave. My squash leaves went from green to that pale, defeated yellow-green that means a plant has stopped bothering to argue with the heat. I was watering by hand every evening with a hose that the county was threatening to restrict, and it still wasn't enough, and I remember standing out there one night doing math I didn't want to do about how many more zero readings the garden could survive.

I want to say I fixed it with some clever trick, but mostly what I did was fail slowly and adjust. I lost the squash entirely, all six plants, root systems too shallow to reach anything once the top few inches of soil turned to powder. I moved the tomatoes' mulch three inches deep instead of one, which I'd read about and never bothered doing when things were easy. I started watering at five in the morning instead of evening, so less was lost to the sun before it soaked in, which meant setting an alarm I resented every single day. None of it was dramatic. It was the kind of slow, boring correction you make when the emergency isn't going to end with one good decision, because the sky isn't cooperating and won't be told what to do.

The gauge read zero for thirty-one days that summer, and I know the number exactly because I wrote it down every morning in a notebook my grandfather also gave me, half out of habit and half because writing zero down felt like it should count for something even when nothing else did. When the rain finally came back in August, an inch and a half in one night, I went out at six in the morning in my pajamas just to look at the tube filled with actual water, which is a strange thing to feel emotional about and I felt emotional about it anyway.

I still have that gauge staked in the same corner of the garden, and I still check it most mornings, out of habit now more than anxiety. What that drought taught me wasn't really about plants. It was that most of the hard things worth doing don't get solved. They get outlasted, in small unglamorous increments, by someone willing to keep measuring even when the number refuses to change.
