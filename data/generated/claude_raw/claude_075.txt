I took the job at the distribution warehouse because my older cousin worked there and vouched for me with the shift supervisor, and because eleven dollars an hour sounded enormous when I was sixteen and had never made money doing anything besides mowing lawns. The shift ran ten at night to six in the morning, four nights a week, sorting packages off a conveyor into bins organized by zip code prefix. I told my parents it was temporary, just for the summer, and I meant it. I hated it for the first three weeks.

The hatred was mostly about the hours. My body did not want to be awake at two in the morning scanning barcodes under fluorescent lights that hummed at a pitch I could feel in my teeth. I'd come home as the sky was getting light, eat cereal standing at the counter because sitting down felt like admitting the day was starting, and sleep until early afternoon in a haze that never quite felt like real rest. I decided, sometime around the start of the fourth week, that I was going to quit as soon as I'd saved enough for the laptop I wanted. A finish line. I counted down to it.

What changed wasn't the hours or the fluorescent lights. It was a Tuesday night when the conveyor jammed for the third time that shift, and instead of just clearing it and moving on like I normally did, I stood there annoyed enough to actually look at the layout of the sorting line — where the jam kept happening, which bin it was near, whether there was a pattern. There was. The jam always happened at the same junction, where two belts merged at an angle that was slightly too sharp for boxes over a certain size, and packages would catch the lip and back up the whole line behind them.

I mentioned this to Dario, the night supervisor, mostly to complain, and instead of brushing me off he asked what I thought should change. Nobody had asked me what I thought about anything at that job before. I said maybe the merge point needed a diverter, something to slow the bigger boxes down before they hit the angle, and he shrugged and said I could try building something out of the scrap bin if I wanted, as long as it didn't get in anyone's way.

I spent the next several nights, on my own time before clock-in, cobbling together a guide rail out of a length of PVC pipe and some zip ties, adjusting the angle by feel, testing it against boxes of different sizes while everyone else was on break. It failed twice. The third version worked. The jams at that junction dropped from something like six or seven a night to maybe one, and Dario told the day-shift manager, who came and looked at my ugly little PVC contraption like it was something worth taking seriously.

That was the night I stopped counting down. I started reading about conveyor design and bin-packing algorithms on my phone during breaks, the kind of thing I never would have imagined finding interesting a month earlier. I started noticing inefficiencies everywhere in the building, not because I was told to but because I couldn't stop myself — the way scanners were placed just far enough from the belt to add a half-second of reach time per package, multiplied across thousands of packages a night, into an amount of wasted motion that genuinely bothered me. I did the math once, on a napkin, during a break. It came out to almost forty minutes of collective wasted reaching, every single shift.

I never bought that laptop with warehouse money, because I stayed through the fall, past the point where the job was making sense financially, because I wanted to see what else I could fix. I still don't fully understand why a jammed conveyor belt turned into the thing I read about instead of sleeping, why I'd rather sketch belt geometry in a notebook than watch television, but it did, and I do. I think about systems now the way I used to think about the clock ticking toward six in the morning — as something worth paying close attention to, because the small inefficiencies inside them are where the actual problem always turns out to be hiding.
