"It's asking me a question," Mrs Doran says, and holds the phone out at arm's length as though it has become hot.

I take it. It is the software update notification, the same one as last Tuesday, and I press Later, and hand it back. She takes it with both hands. She always takes it with both hands.

This is Thursday, quarter past four, her kitchen, which smells of the vinegar she cleans the worktop with. There is a mug of tea in front of me that I did not ask for and will not finish, and a plate of two biscuits, and a spiral notebook we started in October in which I write things down for her in capitals. WHATSAPP IS THE GREEN ONE. TO GO BACK, THE ARROW AT THE TOP LEFT, NOT THE ONE AT THE BOTTOM. The notebook is now forty pages long. Some of the pages have been rewritten three times because the first version turned out to be wrong about what she actually needed to know.

"Show me the pictures again," she says. Meaning: show me how to get to the pictures again. We have done this maybe thirty times. I have stopped being impatient about it, which took longer than I would like to say. What I have worked out is that she can do it perfectly on Thursday and not on Sunday, and the reason is not her memory. It is that on Thursday I am sitting here, and on Sunday she is alone in the kitchen and afraid that if she presses the wrong thing she will break it, or lose the photographs, or somehow spend money. The fear is doing the forgetting.

So now the first thing I do, every week, is break something on purpose.

I go into her settings and I change the wallpaper to something ugly, or I turn the text size up to enormous, or I open four apps and leave them open. Then I put the phone on the table and I say: fix it. And she says she can't, and I say I know, and I go and look out of the window at the bins for four minutes while she does.

She has fixed it every week since January.

Today it is the ringer volume, which I have set to silent, and she works it out in ninety seconds, and when I turn around she is not looking at the phone, she is looking at me, waiting.

"Was that right?"

"Doesn't matter if it was right. It's fixed."

"It's not the same thing."

"No," I say, "it isn't," and she laughs at me, because we have had this exchange enough times that it is now a joke rather than a disagreement.

Her son lives in Perth. That is the whole reason for any of this. He calls on Sunday mornings, and before October she missed about half of them, because picking up a video call wrong felt worse than not picking it up. I did not know that for six weeks. I found it out because I asked why she had a missed call from Perth on the screen, and she said, quite lightly, that she had not been quick enough.

We have not missed one since March.

The notebook runs out in a few weeks. I have bought another of the same brand, which was harder to find than it should have been, because she says the lines are the wrong width in the other ones, which is not true, I have checked, but I bought it anyway.

Next week I am going to change the language to Welsh and see what happens. She will be furious. She will fix it.
