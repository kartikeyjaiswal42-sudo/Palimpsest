I learned to drive sitting on a phone book in my grandfather's 1978 Ford, bouncing across the hay field behind his barn, both of us sunburned, him working the pedals with his left foot because my legs weren't long enough yet to reach the clutch and the brake at the same time. I was eleven. What I actually fell for that summer wasn't driving. It was the truck itself — why it lurched when I let the clutch out too fast, why it ran rough until it warmed up, why my grandfather could diagnose a problem with that engine just by listening to it through the hood with his eyes closed, the way other people listen to music.

That's the thing that captivates me, genuinely, more than anything in a classroom: the internal combustion engine as a system, all those small controlled explosions turning into rotation turning into motion, and the fact that a person with the right knowledge can listen to a machine and understand what's wrong with it the way a doctor listens to a heartbeat. I've read the Chilton manual for that truck so many times the spine is cracked in three places. I've spent entire afternoons I meant to spend on homework instead pulling the valve cover off just to look at it, then putting it back exactly the way it was.

My grandfather taught me everything I know about that truck, hands-on, over eight years, and he is also the person I have the longest-running disagreement of my life with, one we have never actually resolved. He believes you learn an engine by feel — by hours, by mistakes, by your hands — and that reading about compression ratios in a manual is close to useless next to just listening to the thing run. I believe both things are true at once, that feel matters and that I also want to understand the actual physics of what's happening in that cylinder, the math behind why a slightly different timing advance changes everything. When I told him last year that I want to study mechanical engineering, he didn't discourage me exactly, but he got quiet, and later I heard him telling my uncle that school was going to teach me to explain an engine without ever really knowing one.

I love him too much to let that go, and I'm stubborn enough not to let it go either. We've had version after version of the same argument, usually while working on the truck together, him saying something like "your hands will teach you faster than any book," me saying that his hands only work because forty years ago somebody's book taught someone the theory he inherited secondhand. Neither of us has ever won. He still calls the manual "the excuse" for not really knowing a machine. I still bring it out to the barn anyway, and he still, despite himself, ends up leaning over my shoulder to look at the diagram.

We haven't settled it, and at this point I don't think we're supposed to. What I know is that I can now do both — diagnose a rough idle by ear the way he taught me, and explain in a lab report exactly why it's happening. I think, somewhere under his stubbornness, he's a little proud of that. He has just never once said so out loud.
