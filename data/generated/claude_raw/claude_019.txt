Every night for the eight months before regionals, I wrote down what had gone wrong that day. Not what had gone right — I didn't have a habit for that — just the failures, in a green spiral notebook I kept in my backpack, half-illegible because I usually wrote it at eleven at night after four hours in the robotics lab. "Claw drops object 1 in 6 times." "Autonomous routine drifts left, unknown cause." "Battery voltage sags under load, check connectors." It wasn't glamorous. It was closer to a confession.

I started the notebook by accident, honestly, because our team lead made us log bugs for the mentors to review, and I was the one who volunteered to keep it since nobody else wanted the job. But somewhere around week three I noticed I was writing more than the assignment required. I'd add little theories in the margins, sketches of the gripper mechanism, arrows connecting one failure to another failure from two weeks earlier that I was suddenly sure was the same root cause. I'd lose entire evenings to it. My mom would knock on my door to ask if I knew what time it was, and I genuinely wouldn't.

That's the strange thing about debugging that people who haven't done it don't believe: it is not tedious, it is completely absorbing, in the way I imagine a puzzle is absorbing to someone who loves puzzles, except the puzzle fights back and changes shape every time you touch it. A robot that isn't working is a small, solvable mystery with real physical evidence, and I found I would rather sit on a concrete floor at ten p.m. tracing a wire with a multimeter than do almost anything else. I liked that the answer was always in there somewhere, in the machine, waiting to be found, and that finding it required nothing except attention and enough patience to write down what I'd already ruled out.

At regionals, none of it mattered, or that's what it felt like at the time. Our robot's arm seized up thirty seconds into our first match, in front of judges, in front of the other forty teams, in front of my parents, who had driven four hours to watch. It never moved again that day. We didn't place. I remember sitting behind the pit table afterward, not crying exactly, just very quiet, holding the green notebook like it had failed me personally.

It took me a few weeks to understand what actually happened, which was that the seized arm was a wiring fault we'd genuinely never seen before, a one-in-a-hundred failure that had nothing to do with anything in my notebook — and that the notebook was still the reason I could diagnose it that fast once we got the robot back to the shop, three days later, in about twenty minutes. Every failure I'd logged all season had built a kind of map in my head of how the machine failed, so when a new failure showed up, I already knew what it wasn't.

I still keep the habit. New robot, new season, same green-covered notebooks, now four of them deep. I don't think I'm chasing the stage moment anymore, the one where everything works and the crowd claps. I think I'm chasing the two hours before that, alone with a multimeter and a problem that hasn't given up its answer yet, writing down exactly what's wrong so that next time, whatever breaks, I'll already recognize it.
