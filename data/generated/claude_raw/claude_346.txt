The clipboard sits on the bench next to me, and right now I'm staring at it instead of at the game. It's a white board with a metal clip, nothing special, and the formations I've drawn with a dry-erase marker are probably smudged and barely legible. Nobody else seems to be looking at it, but I keep it there every Saturday because it represents something I can't quite explain to the parents who stand along the sideline and shout instructions at their kids.

I started coaching this under-eleven football team last year when the previous coach left and there was nobody else to take it. I was only sixteen myself. The league allowed it because it's a community team and it was that or the program shut down. I said yes without thinking through what this meant. What I got was eight kids aged nine to eleven, a field at a park that's usually occupied by people walking dogs, and the responsibility of teaching them a sport I played but had never tried to teach before.

The clipboard became my anchor. On it, I draw formations. We do drills. We talk about positioning and strategy, about how to move as a unit, about the difference between just kicking the ball and moving toward a goal. It's a simple diamond shape I usually go with, or sometimes a four-three-three spread, depending on the week. I spend time on Thursday nights drawing these out, thinking about how to explain them. The clipboard matters because it means I'm taking this seriously.

What surprised me is that the kids took it seriously too. I expected them to want to just run around kicking the ball, but they looked at the formations. They asked questions. One of the smaller kids, Priya, started asking if she could write in ideas of her own. Soon they were all contributing. The clipboard stopped being my tool and became something they owned. They'd gather around it before games and trace through what we'd talked about, and I could see them thinking through how their position related to everyone else's.

The field became the place I felt most myself without realizing it was happening. On other days I'm managing school and friendships and expectations and all the small politics of being sixteen. On Saturdays, the field is simpler. The goal is literally a goal. The team is eight kids who need me to help them understand how to work together. There are no ambiguities about what success looks like. If you move the ball into the net, you have succeeded. If you don't, you haven't. The clipboard keeps me honest about the strategy of how to make success possible.

There's one Saturday that sticks with me. We were playing a team that was clearly better—they had more coordination, better equipment, more confident kids. At halftime we were losing three to nothing. The other team's coach had actual credentials and experience. I sat with my clipboard and felt completely inadequate. But the kids didn't care. They gathered around to see the clipboard. One of the boys said he thought if we pushed the line forward more, we'd have a better chance at cutting off their passes. We adjusted. We didn't win the game, but we held them to four to one and scored our first real goal as a team. I remember Priya's face when the ball went in. I remember the other kids running to hug her. I remember that the clipboard, sitting on my lap with its smudged formations, had been part of that.

The field is where I understand myself. I'm someone who can take eight kids who don't know each other well and help them see how they fit together. I'm someone who can draw plans and hold them in my head and explain them until they make sense. I'm someone who matters to these kids in a specific way—not as a friend or a family member, but as someone who believes they can do something hard and helps them find the way.

I still have that clipboard. It's covered in the marker lines from a hundred different formations, strategy notes, starting lineups. It's battered and barely functional. But when I stand on that field on Saturday mornings, when I watch these kids arrange themselves into a formation we've practiced, into the shape we drew together on the worn white surface of the clipboard, I'm exactly where I'm supposed to be. The place where I know who I am and what I'm capable of.
