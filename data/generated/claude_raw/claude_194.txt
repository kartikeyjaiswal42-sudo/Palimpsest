I kept the scorecard from the cricket match where my father injured himself so badly that he couldn't play anymore, and I still don't know what to do with it.

He played in a local league. Nothing professional, just weekend cricket with men he'd known for years. It was his thing, the way he spent Saturday afternoons. He'd leave early in the morning with his gear, and he'd come back tired and happy, talking about the game, about a good shot he'd made or a close call with an umpire.

I'd started keeping score at his matches when I was maybe twelve or thirteen. Not officially—I wasn't the league's official scorekeeper. But my father liked having me there, and I liked having something to do that made me feel like I was part of his world. So I'd sit on the sidelines with a notebook and keep track of runs, wickets, all the details that make cricket match records.

It became a habit. Every match, I'd be there with my scorecard. I got better at it over the years. I understood the sport better. I'd know when to anticipate a certain batsman's style, or when a bowler was getting tired. My scorecards became detailed, not just numbers but small notes about good plays or unusual strategies.

The match where he got injured was like any other. I was in my usual spot with my notebook, keeping score. He was batting, playing well, hitting the ball with confidence. Then something went wrong with a delivery—it came in at an awkward angle, and he moved to avoid it but his foot caught in a way that feet aren't meant to catch.

He fell. The fieldside went silent in that specific way it does when an injury isn't just pain but might be serious. They carried him off. The game paused. Then it resumed because that's what happens—you keep playing, and the injured player goes to the hospital.

I finished keeping score, which seems monstrous when I think about it now. But I was sixteen and I didn't know what else to do. I finished recording the match, complete and accurate.

My father's back was damaged. The injury was bad enough that he couldn't play again. The doctors said he might have permanent limitations, that he should be very careful about strain. He accepted it with a kind of sadness that I'd never seen in him before. He came back to one match a few weeks later just to watch, but he couldn't sit comfortably in the stands for long. Eventually, he stopped going.

I stopped going too. The habit had been about him. When he was no longer playing, there was no reason for me to be there, no reason to keep score. The league found an official scorekeeper. Life moved on.

But I kept that scorecard. The one from the match where he was injured. I kept it in a box under my bed for years, and I still have it. I don't look at it often. When I do, I read my own handwriting, see the moment where I stopped recording cleanly—it gets messier, harder to read, the moment he went down. Even in real-time, my concentration broke. But I kept recording. The numbers are still there.

I don't know what the scorecard means. I don't know if I'm keeping it because I feel guilty for continuing to keep score after he was hurt. I don't know if I'm keeping it as a memorial to something that's gone, or as a monument to a habit that outlived its purpose. I don't know if I should just throw it away.

What I know is that I failed to recognize what was important in that moment. I measured success by whether I could accurately record a game, and I missed the more important thing—that someone I loved was being injured, and that my attention should have been on him instead of on my scorecard.

I don't even play cricket now. I haven't for years. But I think I would have, if he'd been able to keep playing. I would have learned the game more deeply. I would have eventually played with him. And maybe that's what the scorecard represents—a version of my life that ended when his ended. The life where I followed him into cricket, where his injury became the thing that redirected both of our paths.
