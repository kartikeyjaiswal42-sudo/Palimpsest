My family has owned the same small orchard for thirty-seven years. It's not big—maybe two acres of land with apple trees, a few plum trees, one lonely pear tree that mostly produces disappointment. My grandfather planted most of them. My parents have maintained them. Last summer, when I was sixteen, my father asked if I wanted to take over the summer care of the orchard.

I said yes without really thinking about what that meant.

It meant showing up at seven in the morning before it got too hot. It meant understanding what each tree needed—which ones were diseased, which ones had pests, which ones were stressed by drought. It meant pruning dead branches. It meant managing the weeds that grew around the base of each tree. It meant being alone in the orchard for several hours each day, just me and the trees and the work.

The key to understanding any summer of orchard work is understanding the notebook.

My grandfather had kept a handwritten garden journal for thirty years. When my father gave it to me, I opened it and found pages and pages of notes in his handwriting. "Apple tree, east row, fertilized." "Plum tree attacked by insects, treated with sulfur." "This year's harvest light, probably the frost." Every entry was dated. Every entry was specific.

I started my own journal that summer, and I found myself writing the same kinds of things. "The third apple tree lost a major branch, probably to wind damage." "The pear tree has some kind of fungal growth on the trunk, treating it with copper fungicide and watching carefully." "Best year for apples in several seasons, probably because we had good rain in June."

The notebook became a physical object that mattered. It wasn't just a place to track data. It was the conversation I was having with my family's land, written down. It was proof that I was paying attention. It was evidence that the work I was doing mattered and would be remembered.

Looking back now, I realize that the notebook was also how I fell in love with the orchard. I'd grown up knowing the trees existed, but I'd never actually understood them until I started writing down what I was observing. The act of writing it down meant that I had to really see it—not just notice that something was wrong, but understand what was wrong and what might fix it.

In September, when school started and my official orchard work ended, my father asked if I wanted to keep doing it into the fall. I said yes. In October, he asked about winter pruning. I learned how to prune correctly instead of just randomly cutting things. Every season had its own demands, and the notebook evolved with it.

The notebook sits on my desk now, and there are forty-six pages of my handwriting in it. It's not beautiful handwriting. I'm not particularly eloquent. But every single page represents hours of time in the orchard, noticing things, recording things, caring about things. It represents a relationship with a piece of land that I didn't know I wanted until I started paying attention to it.

What I think it means is that sometimes you inherit something and you don't understand its value until you spend enough time with it to really see it. The orchard isn't just a place my family owns. It's a living thing that requires attention and knowledge and care. I'm part of maintaining it now in a way I wasn't before.
