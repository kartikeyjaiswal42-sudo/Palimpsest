Every Saturday morning for two years I have gone to the hospice on Kalyan Road and read out loud to people who are dying, and my mother thinks I should stop.

This is the disagreement, and I do not expect to win it, and I am not sure she is wrong.

I started because of a school requirement, forty hours of service, and I picked the hospice because it was walking distance and I assumed I would be filing papers. The volunteer coordinator, a woman named Sister Teresa who is not actually a nun and finds it funny that everyone assumes so, asked whether I could read, and I said yes, and she said good, room four likes Wodehouse.

Room four was Mr Fernandes. He was eighty-one and had been an engineer for the railways and he could not hold a book anymore because of the tremor. I read him seven Jeeves novels over about four months and he laughed in a wheezing way that I was frightened of at first. On the last morning he was awake enough to say that I did the voices badly and should stop trying. He died on a Tuesday. Nobody called me; I found out on the Saturday because the room was empty and made up.

That was the first time. There have been fourteen since.

My mother's argument is this: I am seventeen, and a person my age should not be accumulating fourteen deaths, and she can see what it is doing even if I cannot. She points to specific things. That I do not cry at funerals anymore, including my grandmother's. That I talk about people who are actively dying in the flat conversational tone other people use for the weather. That when my friend's father was diagnosed last year I gave her practical advice about morphine timing when what she wanted was for someone to say it would be all right.

She is right about all three of those. I have checked, and she is right.

My argument is harder to say and I have said it badly every time we have had this conversation, which is maybe five times. It goes something like: the flatness is not damage, it is the job. If I arrive in a room and I am visibly upset, the person in the bed has to manage me. I have watched families do this — come in weeping, and the dying person spends their last strength being reassuring. What is actually wanted is somebody who will sit down, open the book at the marked page, and read for forty minutes as though this were an ordinary Saturday, because for the person in the bed it is not an extraordinary Saturday, it is the only kind they have left.

She says that is a very convenient thing for me to have concluded. She may be right about that too.

We had the conversation again in March, after Mrs Idnani, who I read the Gita to in English because her eyes had gone and she wanted to hear it in the language her son spoke. My mother said, you are seventeen. I said, I know how old I am. Neither of us said anything for a while and then she went and made tea, which is what happens in our house instead of resolution.

I am still going. She still drives me some Saturdays, which I think is the truest thing about her position — she has never once tried to stop me, she has only ever told me she thinks it is costing me something.

There is a man in room two now who wants Agatha Christie. I have to do the voices. He has not complained yet.
