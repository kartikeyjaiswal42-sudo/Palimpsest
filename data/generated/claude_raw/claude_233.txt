The Cedarbrook pool closed to the public at the end of June, some dispute between the HOA and the management company that never got fully explained to me, and I kept my job anyway, guarding a rectangle of blue water that nobody swam in for eight weeks straight. My mother thought this was absurd. She said so almost every night at dinner, some version of why are you still going, what exactly are you protecting, and I never had a good answer except that I liked it there, which she considered evidence that something was wrong with me rather than an actual argument.

I want to say I convinced her eventually. I didn't. That's not really what this is about.

I sat in the chair from nine to five, sunscreen sweating off by noon, whistle around my neck for emergencies that were never going to happen, and I watched the water do the small things water does when nobody's disturbing it — the way light broke up on the surface in the early afternoon, the way a stray leaf would drift the exact same slow arc across the deep end every single day because of some current in the filtration system nobody had bothered to fix. I brought books. I did math homework I actually understood for once because there was nothing pulling my attention away from it. My mother called this wasting a summer. I called it the first time in my life I'd had eight hours a day with genuinely nothing demanding anything of me, and I didn't know how to explain that this felt less like waste and more like the only rest I'd gotten in years of overscheduled Saturdays.

She'd ask what I was even guarding against, and I'd say emergencies don't announce themselves in advance, which is true and also not really the point I was trying to make. The point, if I'm honest, was harder to say out loud. I liked being useful in a way that didn't require producing anything. I liked that my job that summer was simply to notice, to keep my eyes moving across a surface even when the surface never changed, and something about that felt like it was teaching me a kind of attention I couldn't get anywhere else — not in a packed classroom, not in the club meetings I ran, not in the tutoring I did on weekends. She heard "sitting by an empty pool" and pictured a summer thrown away. I heard the same words and felt like I'd found a version of stillness I hadn't known I needed.

We never resolved it. She still brings it up sometimes, usually when she's annoyed about something else entirely, a shorthand for a summer she thinks I squandered. I still don't think I can make her see it the way I saw it, sitting up in that chair with the whistle doing nothing, watching a leaf circle the deep end for the eleven hundredth time. I respect her too much to just let the disagreement go quiet and pretend I've come around to her side, and she respects me too much, I think, to pretend she's come around to mine. We just live with it sitting between us, mostly unspoken, the way you live with a lot of things about people you love that you can't fully explain to them and they can't fully explain away.

I don't know if that pool changed anything about who I am in a way I could put on a resume. I know I think about that unbothered water more than I think about most of what I actually accomplished that year, and I know my mother still doesn't understand why, and I've mostly stopped trying to make her.
