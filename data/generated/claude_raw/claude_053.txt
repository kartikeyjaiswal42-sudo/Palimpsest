My mother was in the hospital for four days when I was fifteen, nothing life-threatening in the end, a gallbladder that decided to become an emergency on a Sunday night, but nobody knew that at the time, and my father spent those four days at her bedside instead of at home, which left dinner as a problem nobody had assigned to anyone. I decided it was mine to solve. I don't remember deciding, exactly. I just remember standing in our kitchen at five o'clock with my younger brothers watching cartoons in the next room, oblivious, hungry eventually, and thinking someone has to do this and realizing the someone was me.

I made my grandmother's chicken and rice, the dish I'd watched her make probably fifty times without ever once writing down amounts, because in our family recipes are transmitted by watching, not by measuring, which is a beautiful tradition right up until the moment you're alone in a kitchen trying to reconstruct it from memory at fifteen. I got the rice wrong twice. I burned garlic badly enough that I had to open every window in February. My brothers ate it anyway, complaining a normal, comfortable amount, which felt like the closest thing to a compliment I was going to get that night.

My father called that evening from the hospital to check in, and I told him, proudly, that dinner was handled, that he didn't need to worry about that part of things. He said thank you, and then he said something else, something about how I shouldn't have to be doing this, that it wasn't my job, that he'd figure something out — takeout, a neighbor, anything — starting tomorrow.

I told him I wanted to keep doing it. He disagreed, gently but firmly, the way he disagrees about most things, and I understood even then that his disagreement wasn't really about the dinner. It was about what it meant that his fifteen-year-old daughter had quietly stepped into a role he felt belonged to the adults in the house. He didn't want me to feel like I had to be the responsible one. I didn't want him to take away the one thing that made those four days feel less like waiting and more like doing something.

We never really settled it. He kept offering to order food; I kept cooking anyway, most nights, until my mother came home and the kitchen reverted to whatever its normal shape had been before. He still, to this day, brings up those four days sometimes with a kind of apology in his voice, like he owes me something for a week he thinks I shouldn't have had to carry. I've told him more than once that I don't see it that way, that I chose it, that choosing something under pressure is still choosing. I don't think he's ever fully believed me. I'm not sure I've ever fully convinced myself either, if I'm honest, because there's a version of that week where I was just a kid who got scared and cooked badly-seasoned rice as a way of managing fear I didn't have another outlet for, and that version is also true.

What I'd bring to a campus that wouldn't otherwise be there isn't some polished story about leadership under pressure, though I could probably package it that way if I wanted to. It's something closer to what happened in that kitchen — the instinct to notice a gap nobody's filling and just quietly fill it, imperfectly, without waiting for permission or perfect competence first. I burned the garlic. I got the rice wrong twice. I made dinner anyway, four nights running, and my brothers ate it, and my father still isn't sure whether to be proud of me or sorry for me, and I've stopped trying to make him pick one.

Most places I've been — clubs, teams, my job at the pharmacy — end up with someone in that role, the one who notices the gap first. I don't think I set out to be that person. I think it just turned out, somewhere around February of my sophomore year, standing over a pot of undercooked rice with the windows open in the cold, that I already was.
