When my older brother got tired of delivering newspapers, he passed the route to me like it was an inheritance. Fifty-three addresses along our street and three adjacent blocks, six mornings a week starting at five-thirty in the morning. It wasn't glamorous, but it was money, and more importantly, it was just something you did in our family. My brother had done it. My cousin had done it. Now it was my turn.

The first three months were actually fine. I'd wake up, throw on clothes, grab the stack of papers from the front porch, and go through the route on autopilot. The sleep deprivation was real, but I was sixteen and thought I could handle anything. Fifty-three houses, every single morning. Some mornings I'd be done by six-fifteen. I felt accomplished, like I was doing this real job while other kids my age were still sleeping.

But somewhere around month four, I started to resent it.

Not because it was hard, but because it was boring, and because I didn't see the point anymore. I was making maybe a hundred dollars a week, which sounded good until I realized I was essentially working every single day for that money. No weekends off, no vacations, no flexibility. My friends would want to hang out after school, and I'd have to say no because I had to sleep before my morning shift. I remember thinking that this route had been perfect for my brother because he had liked routine and liked early mornings. He had found something valuable in it. I was finding only exhaustion.

I told my parents I wanted to quit.

This is where my father and I had a real conflict. He said I couldn't just abandon the route whenever I felt like it, that people depended on getting their newspapers, that part of being in this family meant doing what you'd committed to. He said my brother had delivered papers for three years before moving on, and if I couldn't last more than six months, that said something about my character.

The way he framed it made me feel small. Like quitting was a failure, a sign that I was lazy or undisciplined or somehow less than my brother. I remember trying to explain that just because he had found meaning in the routine didn't mean I was supposed to, that we were different people, that commitment didn't have to mean forcing yourself to do something that was actively making you miserable.

My father didn't hear that. He heard me trying to quit, and it felt personal to him, like I was rejecting something he'd built or believed in.

We didn't resolve it well. I kept delivering papers, but I was angry about it. I'd rush through the route and not care if I put someone's paper in the wrong place. I'd wake up resentful, and I'd carry that resentment through my entire day. Eventually, after about eight more months, my parents just accepted that I was going to stop, and they helped me find someone else to take over.

Looking back, I think both of us were partly right and partly wrong. My father was correct that I'd made a commitment, and there's real value in following through even when something is hard. But he was wrong that my commitment had to look exactly like my brother's, or that quitting meant I was deficient somehow. And I was right that I was miserable, but I was wrong about how I handled that misery. Instead of just pushing through and being angry, I could have talked to my parents earlier, could have tried to find a real solution together.

What I'm still thinking about is the gap between what my father needed me to understand—that you finish what you start—and what I needed him to understand—that forcing myself to do something I hated wasn't actually building character, it was just building resentment. Neither of us was wrong. But neither of us was right either, not completely.
