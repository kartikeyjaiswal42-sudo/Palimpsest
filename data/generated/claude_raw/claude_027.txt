There's a microphone in my closet that doesn't work anymore, hasn't worked in about a year, and I still won't throw it out. It's a cheap Behringer, the kind you'd buy for a podcast you never finish, silver mesh gone a little tarnished on one side where I dropped it on the studio floor sophomore year. My mom asks about it sometimes. Why do you still have that thing. I don't have a great answer.

I started the radio station — well, "station" is generous, it was a closet off the library that used to store textbooks, plus one ancient mixer someone's uncle donated — because I'd gotten it into my head that our school needed one. Not needed like a real need. Needed like I personally couldn't stop thinking about it. I spent the whole summer before junior year writing a pitch, getting the principal to sign off, begging the AV teacher for equipment, and I was so sure, so completely sure, that once it existed people would just show up.

They didn't. First broadcast, I think four people listened, and two of them were my friends who I'd basically ordered to tune in. I remember sitting in that closet with dead air on the mic for what felt like forever because I hadn't planned enough content and figured people would just want to hear us talk. Nobody wants to hear you just talk. I learned that the hard way, more than once.

Here's the part I actually got wrong, though, and it's not the small stuff. I thought the problem was marketing — so I made flyers, did announcements, begged the student council for airtime during assembly. All fine. All fairly reasonable moves. But the real mistake was that I built the whole thing around what I wanted to hear. Music I liked. Topics I found interesting. I didn't ask a single other student what they'd actually want from a school station before I opened it, and by the time it was up and running, changing direction felt like admitting the whole idea was broken from the start. So I didn't change it. I just kept making the thing I wanted and getting quietly hurt that nobody else wanted it too.

It got better eventually — a sophomore named Priya started doing a segment where she read anonymous confessions people submitted through a Google form, and that thing blew up, relatively speaking, forty or fifty regular listeners by spring, which felt enormous to us. But it blew up because of her idea, not mine, and it took me embarrassingly long to hand her the reins instead of trying to fold her segment into my vision for the station.

I keep the microphone because it was there for the bad months, the four-listener months, and I don't want to pretend those didn't happen just because things worked out okay later. It sits on a shelf and it's basically junk and I still catch myself looking at it when I'm annoyed at myself for doing the same thing again in some other group project — deciding I know what people want before I've asked them. I'd like to say I fixed that about myself. I haven't, not all the way. I just notice it faster now, usually around the point where I've already made the flyers.

I don't know exactly what I'll do with the mic. Maybe nothing. It's not sentimental in a nice way, if that makes sense — it's more like a reminder I keep somewhere I'll actually see it.
