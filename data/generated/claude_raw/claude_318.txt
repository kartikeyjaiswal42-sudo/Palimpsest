The pottery studio smells like clay dust and old water, and the wheel that Mr. Kashani gave me wobbles in a specific way that no other wheel wobbles, which is probably why he gave it to me in the first place.

I wanted to learn pottery because my friend was doing it, and the studio seemed like a place where I could exist without being noticed. Mr. Kashani ran the studio like it was a sacred space, which meant everyone was focused on their own work instead of on social hierarchies or what you looked like or whether you were the right kind of person for high school.

He gave me the wheel after the third class, pulling me aside to say that this wheel wasn't being used anymore but that it had character, that I should take it home and practice with it. I thought he was being generous. My mother thought he was being dismissive, giving me the broken wheel instead of a good one.

We didn't fight about it directly. She just made comments. Every time I'd come home covered in clay dust, she'd ask why I was spending so much time on pottery when I should be doing things that looked better on college applications. She'd say the wobbling wheel was a metaphor for how I was wasting my time. She'd mention, very casually, that she didn't think pottery was a serious pursuit.

I loved the wobbling wheel. It made you adjust your technique constantly. You couldn't get lazy with a wheel like that. You had to be fully present, had to feel the clay and adjust to what it was doing. A perfect wheel would let you zone out. This wheel forced you to pay attention.

But my mother's comments made it harder to keep showing up. They made me question whether I was being serious or whether I was just hiding in the studio. They made me wonder if I should be doing something more impressive, something that would matter to people beyond the studio.

Mr. Kashani pulled me aside one day and asked me why I seemed less sure of myself. I told him my mother thought the pottery was a waste of time. He didn't seem surprised. He said that most parents worry about their children doing things that can't be quantified or turned into résumé points. He said that didn't make the things any less real.

But here's where it got complicated. He also said that if I didn't want to be there, I shouldn't be there. That pottery only works if you're doing it because you actually want to, not because you're trying to prove something to someone else. That I shouldn't let my mother's disapproval stop me, but I also shouldn't use pottery as a way to rebel against her.

I've never told my mother what he said. I didn't know how to turn it into an argument that would matter. She had legitimate concerns about my future. The pottery studio didn't pay for college. Time spent on pottery was time not spent on academics or volunteering or other things that looked impressive. She was right about that.

And she was also wrong, but I couldn't figure out how to explain the wrongness in a way that would land. I couldn't explain that the most important thing about the pottery wasn't what it would become but what it was teaching me about focus and attention and accepting imperfection.

The wobbling wheel is sitting in our garage now. I'm not using it as much anymore because school is demanding and my mother keeps suggesting that I should focus on my applications. I haven't quit the pottery studio, but I'm going less frequently. The wheel sits in the garage like evidence of something I couldn't quite commit to, even when I wanted to.

I don't know if I'm going to keep doing pottery after this year. I don't know if I'll keep the wheel. What I know is that my mother and I have never resolved the argument about whether it matters, and I'm not sure we ever will. I know that she loves me and is trying to protect my future. I also know that in protecting my future, she's maybe trying to protect her own idea of what success looks like, and those aren't always the same thing.
