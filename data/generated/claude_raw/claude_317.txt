Mr. Halvorsen kept the wheel exactly as it was, and I could not understand why. It wobbled on the downstroke, a visible little limp in the metal that threw off every pot I tried to center, and I told him, more than once, that the community center should just replace it. He ran the ceramics studio out of the back room of a converted garage two nights a week, mostly for retirees and a handful of us from the high school who needed something to do that wasn't a screen, and he had opinions about almost everything, delivered in the same unhurried voice whether he was talking about glaze chemistry or the wobble.

"That wheel taught me more than any straight one ever did," he said, the third time I brought it up. I didn't believe him. I thought he was being sentimental about a piece of equipment the way old people get sentimental about things that have simply outlasted their usefulness, and I said so, maybe not in those words but close enough that he raised an eyebrow at me over his glasses.

I respected him. That's the part that made it hard. He'd been throwing pots since before I was born, and when he touched a lump of clay it did what he wanted almost instantly, like the clay recognized something in his hands that it didn't recognize in mine. So when he wouldn't budge on the wheel, it needled me in a way that arguing with someone I didn't respect never would have. I spent three weeks convinced he was simply stubborn, attached to an old machine the way some people are attached to old cars that run badly.

I brought it up a fourth time in October, holding up a bowl that had gone lopsided again, blaming the wheel outright. He didn't argue with me that time. He just handed me a scrap of clay and told me to close my eyes and center it on the wobbly wheel while he watched. I did, badly at first, the clay slapping unevenly under my palms, but somewhere in the second minute my hands started making small corrections without me deciding to make them, adjusting pressure a half-second before the wobble arrived rather than after. I opened my eyes. The cone was crooked but centered, which shouldn't have been possible, and he was smiling the way he did when he already knew what was going to happen.

"A straight wheel lets you get lazy," he said. "This one won't let you."

I still think he might have half-invented that explanation on the spot, a good line assembled to fit a situation he liked the shape of. But it worked, and it kept working for the rest of the semester, and by December I could center clay on a straight wheel faster and steadier than kids who'd been throwing for two years, because the wobbly one had trained my hands to correct constantly instead of relying on the machine to hold still for me. I had wanted the studio to fix the equipment. He had wanted me to fix myself, and he was right, and I was so sure of myself that I nearly missed it.

What bothers me now isn't that I lost the argument. It's how sure I was while I was losing it, how the wobble seemed like such an obvious flaw that I never once asked what it might be teaching me instead of what it was ruining. I catch myself doing that still, in physics lab mostly, deciding a piece of equipment is broken before I've considered that the difficulty might be the point. I think about that wheel more than I think about most of the pots I actually finished that year, because the pots got thrown away eventually, chipped or cracked in the kiln, but the correction in my hands never left.
