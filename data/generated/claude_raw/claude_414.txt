I taught myself to read music the way most people learn things alone—slowly, with a lot of frustration, and only because I was too embarrassed to ask for help. I was fifteen and I had decided I wanted to play guitar in a real way, not just the three songs I already knew. Real musicians read music. So I had to figure out what those lines meant.

What I did was sit on my bed every day after school with a music theory workbook I bought for almost nothing at a used bookstore. The book was from the nineteen-eighties and smelled like mold and someone's old apartment. I didn't take it to school or work through it during scheduled study time. It was something I did alone, half-hidden, the way you do things when you're not sure you're smart enough to learn them. If I had somehow failed at reading music, nobody needed to know I had tried.

The first week I learned the treble clef. The lines from bottom to top are E, G, B, D, F. Every Good Boy Deserves Food. The spaces are F, A, C, E. I remember being frustrated because these mnemonics seemed patronizing, but they worked. I would look at a line of music and say the mnemonic out loud and the notes would come. Then I would look at a guitar and have no idea what to do with this information. The guitar has six strings. Music notation doesn't account for there being six strings. So I had to figure out what each string meant and how the dots on a staff corresponded to positions on the fretboard.

This is where teaching yourself gets hard. In a lesson, a teacher would show you this. You would understand the connection. Alone, you're drawing connections in the dark, guessing if you're right, not able to verify until later. I would learn something, try it on the guitar, get it wrong somehow, go back to the book and search for where my understanding had broken down.

I did this for weeks. Months. Every single day I would work through the workbook and then try to play simple melodies from printed sheet music. Most of what I played sounded wrong. But over time the number of things that sounded right increased. I started to understand patterns. The same note appeared in different places on the guitar. There were shapes to chords that made sense. The music notation began to feel less like a code I was cracking and more like a language I was learning.

By the time I was comfortable with it, I realized something important had happened. I wasn't just learning to read music. I was learning about how I learn. I was learning that I could work through confusion if I did it slowly enough. I was learning that the time investment wasn't punishment for not knowing something already. It was the actual cost of knowing something. There was no shortcut. There was just the daily work.

I started applying this to other things. When I took up coding later, I didn't ask for a teacher immediately. I sat alone with online resources the way I had sat alone with that music theory book. When I struggled with a concept, I didn't panic. I knew from experience that the panic would pass and understanding would come if I kept working. I knew this because I had lived it with music.

What teaching myself to read music showed me is that there's a particular kind of confidence that comes from learning something alone. It's not the confidence of having been taught well. It's the confidence of knowing you can persist through confusion. You've done it before. You've sat with not understanding and kept showing up anyway, and eventually the thing made sense. That feeling doesn't go away. Once you've learned it once alone, you know you can do it again.

I play well now. I read music fluently. I can pick up a score and know what it will sound like before I play it. But I think what I really learned was that reading music was just the excuse. The actual thing I learned was how to learn by myself. And that's a skill worth infinitely more than being able to play any song.
