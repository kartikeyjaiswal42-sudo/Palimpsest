It started because of a chain, and it stayed going because of a chain, and if I am honest about the whole thing, most of what I have learned in the last four years I learned from chains.

The first one belonged to a boy named Theo, who was seven, and who lived four doors down from us on Whitcombe Road. He had come off his bike at the bottom of the hill and the chain had jumped the front ring and jammed itself between the ring and the frame, which is a thing that looks catastrophic to a seven-year-old and takes about ninety seconds to fix. I was thirteen. I was outside with a football and nothing to do. I knew how to fix it only because my own bike had done the same thing a month earlier and my dad had shown me once, impatiently, on his way to somewhere.

So I fixed it, and Theo went away, and that would have been the end of it except that a week later his sister knocked on our door with a bike that had a flat, and asked if I did that.

I did not do that. But I said yes.

That is the entire origin. There was no decision. There was a girl at the door and a word that seemed easier to say than no.

What followed was four years of a Saturday morning habit so small that I did not notice it becoming anything. Between about nine and eleven I would be out the front with whatever had turned up during the week. Punctures, mostly — there is a stretch of the cycle path near the industrial units that has been shedding glass since before I was born, and I have probably patched two hundred inner tubes that all failed within a hundred metres of the same spot. Brake pads worn to the metal. Gear cables stretched so far that only three of the seven gears worked, which children accept as normal, the way they accept everything. Saddles at the wrong height, which is the most common problem and the one nobody ever asks about, because a child does not know that a bike is supposed to be comfortable. They think the discomfort is them.

I learned by looking things up the night before and by getting things wrong. I put a tyre back on with the tube pinched under the bead and it blew out in the road twenty minutes later. I overtightened a bolt on a cheap aluminium crank and stripped the thread and had to tell an eight-year-old that his bike would not be ready that day, and watch him try to be fine about it. I bought a cone spanner set with birthday money because I had spent three weeks failing to service a wheel bearing with a pair of pliers and a lot of confidence.

The tools accumulated. There is a plastic crate now, in the shed, that is worth more than any bike I have ever worked on.

Here is what I did not expect, and what makes me want to write about this rather than about anything else I have done.

I know that street better than I know anything. Not the buildings — the households. I know that the Kowalczyks have three children and one bike between them and that the middle one is fastest and therefore gets it most. I know that Amara's father works nights and that she is quiet on Saturdays if he is sleeping. I know which families will offer me money and which will offer me food and which will do neither because they cannot, and I know that I must take the food and refuse the money in a way that does not embarrass anyone, and that this is a skill, and that nobody teaches it, and that I got it wrong for about the first year.

A bike is a strange object to be trusted with. It is the only thing most of those children own that is worth anything, and it is also the machine that decides how big their world is — whether they can get to the park, whether they can get to their cousin's, whether they are allowed to go to the shop alone. When Theo's chain jammed, his radius was about four hundred metres. When I unjammed it, it was three kilometres. Nobody says that out loud. But the parents know. It is why a woman I had never spoken to knocked on our door in 2022 with a bike and an apology and no expectation whatsoever, and why I stayed out until two that afternoon.

The habit is still going. It is smaller now — most Saturdays there is nothing, and I do coursework instead, and I have started to think about the fact that I am leaving. Theo is eleven and has been helping me since he was nine. He can do a puncture faster than I can. He cannot do a bottom bracket and does not want to learn, and I have decided not to push him, because the point was never the bottom bracket.

I want to study mechanical engineering, and I could give the neat version of why: that I love systems, that I like the honesty of a machine, that a bicycle is the most efficient thing a human being has ever built. All of that is true. But the real reason is quieter. It is that I have spent four years learning that a small competence, held steadily, in one place, over a long time, turns into something that is not really about the competence at all. I fixed chains. What I built was a street where a child knows there is someone to ask.
