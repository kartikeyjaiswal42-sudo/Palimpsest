I've kept the scorebook for my dad's weekend cricket league since I was eleven, which means I have opinions about his batting that he does not want to hear, and I tell him them anyway, and we have never once agreed on the actual answer to the argument we keep having.

The scorebook is a real thing, a physical ledger with a cloth cover that the league has used for something like fifteen years, passed from scorer to scorer, and I inherited the job when the previous scorer's kid went off to college and nobody else wanted to sit on the sideline for four hours doing arithmetic. I took to it fast. There's something I like about the precision of it — every run accounted for, every dismissal recorded in a little code, byes and wides and no-balls all separated out so that at the end of the season you can point to a page and say, with total confidence, exactly what happened on a specific Saturday two months ago.

What I like less is that the scorebook makes my dad's decline as a batsman completely undeniable, in a way conversation lets him avoid. He's forty-nine. He still thinks of himself as the guy who used to open the batting for his college team, and some Saturdays he still is, more or less. But the numbers over the past three seasons tell a clearer story than he wants told — his average down almost eight runs, his strike rate slower, more dismissals to spin bowling that he used to handle easily. I know this because I wrote every single one of those numbers down myself, in my own handwriting, and I've shown him the book more than once when he's insisted a particular bad outing was a fluke.

He doesn't like it. We argue about it, actually argue, not the fake kind of arguing families do to pass time — real disagreement, him insisting the numbers don't capture "match situation" or the state of the pitch that day, me insisting that's exactly what averaging over a whole season is supposed to correct for. Neither of us has ever backed down. He still bats. I still write down what actually happens when he does, honestly, without softening it, even though I know some Saturdays he'd rather I didn't.

I've thought about whether I should just fudge it a little for him, once or twice, and I haven't, and I think that refusal is close to the actual center of what I'd bring somewhere new — not the specific skill of scorekeeping, which is not a skill anyone is desperate for, but the stubborn insistence on an honest record even when the person the record is about is someone I love and would rather protect. It would be easier, most Saturdays, to round a number in his favor. I don't do it. He knows I don't do it. It's part of why, underneath the arguing, I think he still hands me the book every single week instead of asking someone else to keep it — because an honest scorer, even an infuriating one, is worth more to him than a comfortable one, even if he'll never quite say so out loud.

We'll probably have the same argument next Saturday. I'll probably still be right, according to the book. He'll probably still disagree, according to himself. Neither of us is going to change on this, and at this point I think we've both stopped expecting to.
