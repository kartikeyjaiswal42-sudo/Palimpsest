The object is a Smith-Corona typewriter, dark green, missing the letter Q, which I bought for eight dollars at an estate sale two summers ago for no reason I could explain to my mother at the time. I bought it about a year and a half after my eighth-grade English teacher, Mrs. Kessler, wrote on my final portfolio that I was "a capable student but not, I think, a natural writer," and meant it as gentle, useful advice, the kind meant to redirect ambition toward something more realistic.

I believed her more completely than I've believed almost anything an adult has told me. I stopped submitting to the school's literary magazine. I switched my elective from creative writing to computer science the following year, told myself it was practical, told myself I'd never really loved writing anyway, just liked being praised for it in elementary school when the bar was lower and everyone got a gold star for finishing a paragraph. For a year and a half I did not write anything that wasn't required for a class, and I mostly didn't miss it, or told myself I didn't.

The typewriter sat in my closet for months after I bought it, unused, mostly because it needed a new ribbon and I never got around to ordering one. What changed my mind wasn't the typewriter itself but a shoebox I found behind it while looking for something else entirely — my own shoebox, from years earlier, full of stories I'd written in fourth and fifth grade, stapled construction-paper covers, illustrations I'd clearly spent more time on than the actual sentences. I sat on my bedroom floor and read through maybe a dozen of them, badly plotted, full of talking animals and unearned happy endings, and felt something I hadn't expected to feel, which was a kind of longing for the version of myself who wrote like that without asking whether it was any good.

I ordered the ribbon that week. I remember loading it wrong the first time, ink smearing across my fingers, and getting it right on the second try with the video tutorial paused every few seconds. The first thing I typed was not a story. It was one paragraph about the shoebox itself, about finding it, badly punctuated because the machine has no way to delete anything and every mistake just sits there on the page, permanent, staring back at you until you either live with it or start the whole page over.

I have written on that typewriter almost every week since, sometimes just a paragraph, sometimes pages that go nowhere and get thrown out. None of it has been graded. None of it has been shown to Mrs. Kessler, who I still see sometimes in the hallway and still like, despite everything, because I don't think she meant to be wrong about me — I think she was right about the writer I was at thirteen, careful and grade-hungry and terrified of an unfinished sentence, and simply couldn't have predicted the one I'd become at sixteen, typing badly punctuated paragraphs on a machine that punishes hesitation by design.

I don't know if I'm a natural writer. I've stopped needing the answer to that question before I'm allowed to keep doing it. The typewriter sits on my actual desk now, not the closet, missing its Q, ink on my fingers most Sunday afternoons, proof of the exact week I decided a teacher's honest guess about me in eighth grade didn't have to be the last word on the subject.
