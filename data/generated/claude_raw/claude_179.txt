Nobody at WKPR, the public radio station where I interned last summer, had actually planned for an intern. I found this out my first morning, when the program director realized there was no desk, no assigned task list, and no real use for a sixteen-year-old who couldn't legally operate the board alone. For the first three weeks I did almost nothing. I alphabetized a CD archive that hadn't been touched since the station switched to digital. I sat in on pledge-drive planning meetings where nobody asked my opinion. Around 4:45 every afternoon, the staff would start packing up for the day, and I would sit alone at the front desk for the last hour of my shift with genuinely nothing assigned to do.

I started answering the phone during that hour, not because anyone asked me to, but because it was there and I was bored. Almost nobody calls a small public radio station after five in the evening. Most days the phone didn't ring at all. But it turned out one person did call, most evenings, around 5:15 — a man named Mr. Halvorsen, eighty-one years old, who listened to the station religiously and had strong, specific opinions about the afternoon jazz program that he had apparently been trying to share with staff for years without much luck, since nobody was ever at the front desk that late to pick up.

I picked up. The first time, I mostly just listened, because I had no idea what else to do with a stranger telling me in detail why the Tuesday host played too much bebop and not enough swing. I could have transferred him to voicemail, which is clearly what the system was built for. Instead I started taking actual notes, and then I started calling him back the next day if I hadn't understood something, and by the second week I was doing this every single afternoon at 4:45, treating it like a real part of the job even though it appeared on no schedule anywhere and nobody had assigned it to me.

It became a genuine routine, the kind you don't notice reshaping you until it already has. I learned the names of six or seven regular late-afternoon callers that summer, people the station had effectively lost as a channel of contact once they stopped hiring anyone to sit at that desk after four. I started keeping an actual log — dates, names, what people said, which shows they were responding to — because it seemed wasteful not to write any of it down. By July, the program director noticed the log sitting on the desk and asked to read it, and by August she'd used three separate entries from it to make actual programming decisions, including moving the jazz block twenty minutes earlier because four different callers, independently, had mentioned missing the start of it on their drive home.

None of this was in my internship description. I wasn't hired to build a listener-feedback pipeline out of a dead phone hour; I was hired, loosely, to learn what a radio station does, and instead I discovered that a huge and completely unaddressed need — actual human contact with the people the station serves — was sitting in an unstaffed hour every single day, waiting for someone bored enough and stubborn enough to keep answering a phone that almost never rang.

That's the habit I'd bring to a campus that wouldn't otherwise be there: the willingness to occupy an unglamorous, unscheduled hour on the theory that something real might be happening in it that nobody's paying attention to. I don't think I'm unusually talented at any one thing. What I am is the kind of person who will sit at the empty desk during the dead hour, and answer the phone anyway, and keep a log nobody asked for, for long enough that a pattern shows up that everyone else was too busy to notice. WKPR still has that late log, kept now by whichever intern comes after me, because I asked that it become a real part of the job before I left. I like to think Mr. Halvorsen still calls at 5:15, and that somebody is still writing down what he says.
