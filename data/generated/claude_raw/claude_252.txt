My mother sells other people's clothes. She has done it for nine years, since the year she stopped cleaning offices, and the business has no name, though the invoices say M. Okonkwo Trading and the customers say Auntie Mercy. It runs out of the front room, the garage, a stall at the Sunday market on Ravensbourne Road, and, since 2021, a Facebook page I built for her with a header image I have never been allowed to change.

I want to write about a thing I got wrong, which took me about four years to understand and which I still think about when I am supposed to be doing other work.

Here is the wrong thing. For most of my childhood I thought my mother's business was a stage she was passing through. Not because she said so. Because everything around me said so. School said so, in the way careers evenings never once mentioned resale or market trading. My friends said so, in the way they asked what my mum did and I said she works in retail and let them assume a shop with a till and a manager. I said it in that specific way, retail, because it was a word that sounded like it had a ceiling I could point to. And I said it to her, once, when I was fourteen and angry about something unrelated: that she should get a proper job.

I have replayed that sentence more times than I would like to admit. She did not shout. She said, in English, which she uses for arguments the way other people use a raised voice, that a proper job is one that pays for your shoes, and I was wearing shoes, and would I please pass the tape.

What I got wrong was not just rudeness. It was a whole model of what work is. I thought a proper job was one that somebody else had designed and you stepped into. I did not see that my mother had designed hers.

She has a system. She buys in bulk from a clearance broker in Erith, sacks sorted by category and grade, and she pays for grade three because grade one is a con — the margin is worse and the broker has already taken out the good pieces. Then she opens the sacks on the garage floor and does the sorting herself, and this is the part I did not understand for years. She is not sorting by size or by colour. She is sorting by customer. There is a pile for Mrs Adeyemi who wants long skirts with pockets. There is a pile for the girls who come after college and want anything that photographs well. There is a pile for church, which is separate from the pile for work, though a stranger could not tell the two apart. She holds a jacket up for four seconds and she is not looking at the jacket. She is running through about sixty people she knows.

That is not a market stall. That is a customer database held entirely in one woman's head, and it is more accurate than anything I could have built for her, and I know because I tried. When I was fifteen I made her a spreadsheet. I was very pleased with it. It had dropdowns. She used it for eleven days and then stopped, and when I asked why, she said it took longer to write down what she already knew than to know it. She was right and I was annoyed about her being right for most of a year.

The other thing I had wrong was the moral shape of the whole enterprise. I had absorbed, without noticing, the idea that second-hand is what you do when you cannot afford first-hand — that the business existed inside somebody else's failure. But I have stood behind that stall on cold Sundays and watched who buys. Women who could go to Next and don't, because a coat for eight pounds that will last four winters is not a compromise, it is arithmetic. Men buying suits for interviews. A girl last March who bought a dress for a wedding and came back a month later to say thank you, which nobody does for a shop.

I still think I owe my mother a correction I have not properly delivered. I have apologised for the sentence. I have not managed to say the larger thing, which is that I was measuring her against a picture of work I got from people who had never had to invent a livelihood out of nothing but attention and a garage.

I am applying to study economics. The honest reason is not a textbook. It is that I spent nine years standing next to an economy I could not see, calling it retail, and I would like to stop mistaking systems for their absence.
