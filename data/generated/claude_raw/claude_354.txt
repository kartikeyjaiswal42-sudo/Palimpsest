My uncle had this radio from the 1950s that he kept in his garage. It was broken, had been broken for years, sitting on a shelf next to bottles of old paint and tools he didn't use anymore. When I was fifteen, I asked him if I could try to fix it. He said sure, go ahead. He didn't think I would make any progress. I don't think he cared much whether I did or not. It was just something that lived in his garage.

What started as a one-afternoon project became something I did every Saturday for six months. I would go to his place and work on the radio. I had no formal training in electronics. I looked things up online. I watched videos of people restoring old radios. I learned what different components did. I learned how to carefully unsolder things and resolder them. I learned what a capacitor was and why they fail over time.

This routine of going to my uncle's place and working on the radio became important in a way I didn't anticipate. My relationship with my uncle was fine but distant. He worked a lot. We didn't see him as much as we saw my other relatives. But by working on the radio in his garage, I started spending time with him. He would come out and watch what I was doing. He would tell me stories about the radio, about when he had used it, about music he had listened to on it as a teenager.

Over those six months, I learned the history of the radio through him. I learned things about my uncle I had never known. He was different in the garage than he was anywhere else. More relaxed. More willing to talk about his own past. He would sit on a stool and tell me about his high school years while I worked on capacitors and tubes.

Restoring the radio also taught me something about time. Every component had failed for a reason—things degrade. But they can be renewed if you have patience and care and the right knowledge. Nothing is actually broken beyond repair. It just requires understanding the problem and taking the time to fix it.

By month four, the radio worked. It was the most satisfying moment. I plugged it in and the tubes glowed and a station came in clear. It was an AM station playing oldies, which felt right. My uncle listened to it and for a moment I saw him as a teenager, the person who had bought this radio and loved it enough to keep it all these years even after it stopped working.

What happened after that is my uncle asked me to look at other broken things in his house. A lamp that didn't turn on. A toaster that was temperamental. A clock that had stopped. Every weekend I would fix something else. Not because I had to, but because I wanted to. I had found something I was good at and a person who valued it.

My relationship with my uncle completely changed. He started calling me asking my advice about things. He told my parents to have me look at their broken devices. He gave me tools. He treated me like someone who knew how to do things, like someone with skills that mattered. This probably sounds small. But for me it was huge. It was the difference between being a distant relative and being someone he actually valued and wanted to spend time with.

The radio still works. My uncle keeps it on his nightstand now instead of in the garage. Sometimes when I visit he'll turn it on and we'll listen for a few minutes. He never says this explicitly, but I know the radio means something different to him now. It's not just a broken thing that couldn't be fixed. It's evidence that his broken things matter. That someone cares enough to fix them. That restoration is possible.

This process taught me about myself. I learned I have patience and focus. I learned I can solve complex problems if I take time to understand them. I learned that small routines can become meaning. Every Saturday in the garage was just me working on electronics, but it was also me building a relationship. It was me growing into someone my uncle respected. These big changes happen through small repeated actions.
