I started the chess club at Lindbergh with a stack of fourteen sign-up sheets and no idea that the hardest part would not be finding players but keeping them, and the thing I got wrong that fall is still the thing I think about when I'm deciding how to treat people who are worse at something than I am.

Lindbergh is a football school. There had never been a chess club, and when I taped up posters in September I expected maybe four people, all of them the kids who ate lunch in the library. Instead fourteen showed up to the first meeting, most of them curious rather than committed, a few there because a rumor had gone around that it would look good on a transcript. I remember feeling something close to triumph. I had built a thing out of nothing, and for about six weeks it worked.

Then it stopped working, and for a long time I blamed the school, or standardized testing season, or the fact that chess is not, by nature, a sport with cheerleaders. It took me almost a year to admit that I was the reason the club fell apart, and the specific moment I keep returning to involves a freshman named Denny Ochoa.

Denny came to every meeting. He was not good — he moved his queen out on move three nearly every game and lost her by move eight, and I had spent the first weeks of the club setting up a Swiss-system ladder with real ratings, because I wanted us to be a serious club, the kind that could eventually compete regionally. I corrected people's openings. I made everyone learn algebraic notation in the second week, whether they wanted to or not. And one Tuesday, watching Denny walk his queen into a fork for the third time in a row, I said, in front of six other people, something like "we usually try not to let people play like that here." I meant it as a joke. Everybody laughed, including Denny, a little.

He did not come back the next week, or the week after. By November attendance had dropped from fourteen to nine, then to five, then to two — me and my friend Aaron, playing out the string in an empty classroom because neither of us wanted to be the one to say the club was over. I told myself the numbers were about football season, about homework load, about anything other than the fact that I had built a club that measured people by how good they were instead of whether they wanted to come back.

What I got wrong was not the ladder or the notation requirement, exactly. It was deciding, without saying so out loud even to myself, that the club existed to produce strong players rather than to give people a room where they were allowed to be bad at something in public. Denny wanted a place to be bad at chess. I gave him a scoreboard instead, and then I laughed at where he landed on it.

I never apologized to him directly — by the time I understood what I'd done, he had already stopped eating lunch anywhere near the library, and it felt too late and too strange to bring up. The club survived, technically, restarted the following year with an actual beginners' table and a rule I invented for myself: no comments on anyone's moves unless they ask. It is smaller than I wanted and slower than I wanted and I like it more than I liked the version with fourteen names on a sign-up sheet. I still think about Denny's queen, and about how easy it is to build something that looks like an open door and run it like a closed one.
