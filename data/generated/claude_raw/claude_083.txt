Mrs. Alvarez keeps her old flip phone in a drawer now, next to the smartphone I set up for her, like a retired employee she still can't bring herself to fire. I go over most Sundays after church to help her figure out whatever the phone has decided to do since the last time, and almost every visit ends the same way: with her saying she liked it better when Robert used to just call the house line, and me saying that Robert's line to Ecuador costs him four dollars a minute and this doesn't cost him anything, and her saying that a call that costs something means more.

We have never resolved this. I don't think we're going to.

It started because her daughter asked me to. Mrs. Alvarez had lived alone since her husband died, three doors down from us, and her son Robert lived in Guayaquil, and for years the only way they spoke was a landline call every other Sunday, twelve minutes, timed like a hostage negotiation. I showed up with my mother's old iPhone, wiped clean, and a legal pad, because I'd read somewhere that writing the steps down helps. Step one: this is the green button. Step two: this is how you see his face.

The first time the call connected and Robert's face actually appeared on the screen, Mrs. Alvarez made a sound I hadn't heard a person make before, something between a laugh and a cough, and she touched the screen like she could touch him. I thought that was the end of the argument. I thought I'd won something.

But the next Sunday she told me she didn't like it. She said that on the landline she used to close her eyes while he talked, and she could hear the exact tiredness in his voice, whether he'd been drinking, whether he was lying about being fine. On the video call his face fills the screen and somehow that makes him harder to read, not easier, because he performs for the camera the way people do, holds his mouth in a smile a half-second too long. She said the phone gives her more of him and tells her less.

I told her that was ridiculous. I told her twelve minutes every two weeks was starvation and this was a feast, and feast is better than starvation no matter what shape the food comes in. She said I was seventeen and had never gone hungry long enough to know that isn't always true.

I still think I'm right. I think about it on the walk home some Sundays, rehearsing the argument better, the one where I finally say the thing that makes her agree with me. I haven't found it. Every version I practice sounds like I'm telling her that the way she loves her son is wrong, which isn't what I mean, except maybe it is a little.

What keeps me going back isn't that I think I'll win. It's the drawer. She won't get rid of the flip phone, and some nights I catch her holding both of them at once, the dead one and the working one, weighing them like fruit at a market, like she's still deciding. I used to think teaching her the smartphone was the project. Now I think the project was smaller and harder than that: sitting with someone you love while they hold two truths that don't fit together, and not making them choose, and not pretending you understand what you don't.

Robert called last week to thank me. He said his mother seems happier, sharper, that she asks him questions now she never used to ask. I didn't tell him she still keeps the old phone charged, just in case, or that she told me last Sunday she sometimes calls her sister on it instead, on purpose, so she can close her eyes again. I don't know how to explain to him, or to myself, that I gave his mother something good and also took something from her, and that both of those are true on the same Sunday, in the same drawer, and I still haven't figured out which one to argue for.
