The sign came down on a Tuesday in March, and it took two men with a scissor lift about forty minutes, and I watched the whole thing from across the road because my mother had asked me to be there and then had not come herself.

It was a wooden sign, painted, not one of the plastic light-boxes the other places on that street have. Green background, cream letters, ARANDA'S in a font my grandfather had chosen in 2002 from a book of fonts at a printer's in Slough. Underneath, in smaller letters, PORTUGUESE GRILL. The paint had gone chalky on the south-facing half. There was a hairline crack running down through the second A that had been there since a storm in 2016, and every year my father said he would get it seen to.

When they got it down they laid it flat on the pavement and one of the men asked if we wanted it. My mother had said yes on the phone earlier, so I said yes, and then I had to carry it three streets to the car, and it was heavier than it looked, and about halfway there I had to put it down on a low wall outside a dentist's and stand next to it for a minute like an idiot.

Twenty-two years. My grandfather opened it, my father ran it, and my mother did the books at the kitchen table on Sunday nights with the radio on. I worked there from the age of eleven, unofficially, carrying things, and from fifteen properly, on Fridays and Saturdays, taking orders. I know the menu the way other people know a national anthem.

What I want to write about is not the closing. Restaurants close. Our rent went up thirty-one percent in 2023 and the road was dug up for eleven weeks in 2024 and the arithmetic stopped working, and there is no story in that, it is just arithmetic.

What I want to write about is what I understood while I was standing on the pavement next to that sign outside the dentist's.

I had spent about four years being quietly embarrassed by the restaurant. I would not have said so. If you had asked me I would have said I loved it, and I did love it, but I had also spent four years steering conversations away from it, saying "my parents have a business" instead of "my parents have a restaurant," and never once inviting anyone from school to eat there. I told myself this was because it was a small place with fourteen tables and a laminated wine list. I told myself people would be kind about it in the way that is worse than being unkind.

Standing next to the sign, I worked out that this was not true. What I was actually embarrassed by was that my father, who is fifty-four, has burn scars on both forearms from a charcoal grill, and speaks English with an accent that people ask him to repeat, and that for twenty-two years he had got up at six to go to a market in Southall and had come home at midnight smelling of piri-piri and smoke, and that the whole of it had produced a wooden sign that two men could take down in forty minutes.

I had been embarrassed on his behalf, which is a thing I had never let myself see clearly, and which is much worse than being embarrassed by him, because it means I had already decided the effort was not worth what it bought.

The sign is in our garage now, leaning against the back wall behind the bikes. My mother wanted it on a wall inside the house and my father said no, absolutely not, and would not discuss it, and I understood that too.

I am applying to study accounting, which sounds like the least romantic possible response to this, and my friends find it funny. But I sat next to my mother at that kitchen table for six years watching her try to make a rent increase survivable with a calculator and a shoebox of receipts, and nobody ever helped her, and there were things that could have been done in 2022 that we did not know about until 2024. There are eleven other family places on that road. I would like to be the person they can ring.

I have not told my father that this is why. I think he would say the restaurant was not a lesson, it was his life, and he would be right, and I do not have a good answer to that yet.
