The place is the passenger seat of a 2011 Vauxhall Zafira, parked, engine off, outside the Aldi on Fitzwilliam Road. That is where I feel most myself, and it is a stupid answer, and I have thought about giving a different one.

My father had a stroke in the February of Year Eleven. He was fifty-three. It was an ischaemic stroke on the left side, which meant his right arm and his speech, and the word the consultant used was expressive aphasia, which I looked up in the corridor on my phone. It means the words are in there. He knows what he wants to say. The route from the knowing to the saying has been cut.

I got something wrong in the first year and I still think about it most weeks.

What I got wrong was that I helped. That is the whole confession and it takes some explaining, because everyone told me I was being wonderful. He would be trying to say a word and there would be a pause — four seconds, eight seconds, sometimes twenty — and I would supply it. He wanted the word for the thing you boil water in and I would say kettle, and his face would clear, and we would move on. I was fast at it. I got good at guessing from the shape of the half-sound he had managed, from context, from knowing him. I was proud of it, privately, and I thought of myself as the only person in the house who could do it well.

The speech therapist, Ruth, told me not to. She told me in about the third week and I heard it as advice for other people. She said the pause is the work. She said every time somebody else finishes the sentence the brain does not have to build the road, and the road is what we are trying to build. I nodded. And then I went home and did it anyway, for months, because watching him struggle was unbearable and because — this is the part I have had to be honest about — being the one who understood him made me useful, and I badly needed to be useful that year.

I was not being kind. I was being comfortable. I took an eight-second silence that belonged to him and I ended it because I could not sit in it.

The car is where I stopped. It was a Saturday, about a year in, and we had gone to Aldi because he wanted to get out of the house, and he could drive by then, sort of, and he was trying to tell me something about my grandmother. He got stuck. And for whatever reason — I was tired, I think, more than virtuous — I did not say anything. I looked out of the window at the trolleys. The heating ticked as it cooled. And after maybe fifteen seconds he got there himself, the whole sentence, and it was not the sentence I would have guessed. I would have guessed wrong. For a year I had probably been guessing wrong and he had been letting me, because agreeing was faster than correcting.

So now we sit in the car. That is a real thing we do. He parks and we do not go in for ten minutes. It is the only place where there is no one else waiting, no one else to be embarrassed in front of, no reason for either of us to hurry. He talks and I do not fill anything in. It is slow. Some sentences take a minute. Occasionally he gives up and waves a hand and we let it go.

I feel most myself there because it is the only place I have managed to be genuinely quiet. Everywhere else I am the person who explains things, who moves things along, who is helpful — and I have learned that being helpful is the thing I hide behind. In that car I am not doing anything. I am just there while somebody I love does something hard.

The Zafira has 140,000 miles on it and a crack in the wing mirror. He drives it to the shop and back and nowhere else. I would not trade those ten minutes for anything I have.
