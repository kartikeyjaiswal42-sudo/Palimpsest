My grandmother thinks I love the orchard. She says it the way you say something already agreed upon, and I have never corrected her, which is its own kind of lie. Last July, standing between the fourth and fifth rows with a pair of loppers in my hand, I understood that we were having an argument that neither of us had started and neither of us would finish.

The orchard is four hundred trees on nine acres outside Nashik, mostly custard apple, with a strip of guava along the eastern boundary that my great-grandfather planted and nobody has had the heart to remove even though it yields almost nothing now. My grandmother has run it since her husband died. I spent last summer there because my mother said I should and because there was nothing else, and I ended up staying eleven weeks.

Here is what happens in an orchard in July. You get up before it is properly light, because by ten the heat makes everything slower and more stupid. You walk the rows looking for mealybug, which arrives as a white smear in the joint where a branch leaves the trunk. You prune, which is mostly deciding what to kill. You check the drip lines for the places where a rat has chewed through and the water is going into the ground instead of the roots. Then you eat, and then you sleep through the worst of the afternoon, and then you go out again until the light fails.

I did this for eleven weeks and somewhere in the middle of it I stopped noticing that I was doing it. That is the closest I can get to describing why the orchard is the place where I feel most like myself. It is not that I feel expanded there or connected to something larger. It is that the part of me that is always narrating stops narrating. There is a specific thing that happens when you are checking the underside of leaves for two hours: the checking becomes the whole of you. I have never found that anywhere else, not in a library, not in the room where I take my exams, not in my own house.

But my grandmother thinks that is love, and I think it is something narrower, and this is where we disagree.

She wants me to come back after university. She has not said this in a sentence with a subject and a verb. She has said it by walking me to the eastern boundary and telling me which guava trees her father planted in which year. She has said it by teaching me to graft, which takes a season to learn badly and years to learn well, and which is not a thing you teach someone who is leaving. In August, on the last evening, she asked me whether I thought the drip system should be replaced entirely or patched, and she waited for my answer as though it mattered, as though I would be there for the consequences of it.

I said patched. Then I went back to school and did not think about the drip system for four months.

What I want to say to her, and have not, is that I can love a place without wanting to be responsible for it, and that these are not the same feeling wearing different clothes. What she would say back, and has almost said, is that this is what everyone thinks at seventeen, and that the orchard has survived three generations of people who felt exactly that and came back anyway. And the honest problem is that I do not know which of us is right. I am not writing this from a settled position. I think she may be correct about me in a way I cannot yet verify.

There is a version of this where I say the orchard taught me patience, or roots, or the value of slow work. I do not think it taught me any of those. What it gave me was eleven weeks of being a person who was not thinking about being a person, and the discovery that I am capable of that, which I had not known.

I will go back this July. I will check the leaves and mend the drip lines and my grandmother will not ask me again, because she already asked and got her answer, and she is patient in the way people who plant guava trees are patient. We are still disagreeing. Neither of us has moved. I have started to suspect that this is what it looks like when two people take each other seriously.
