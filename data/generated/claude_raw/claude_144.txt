For three years I have sewed on hooks and eyes for the Marsden Players. Not costumes, at first. Hooks and eyes, and press studs, and the small bar of thread you make at the top of a zip so it does not run off the tape. Wardrobe is in the old scout hut behind the theatre and I go on Wednesday evenings, and for the first year that was the entirety of my contribution: fastenings.

I want to describe how small this is, because the fact that it is small is the point. A hook and eye takes about ninety seconds. Six stitches through each hole, four holes, and you do not go through to the front of the fabric. That is it. In a full show there might be forty of them. I have sewed, at a rough count, somewhere past nine hundred.

Val, who runs wardrobe and has done since 1994, gave me a tin of hooks and a strip of calico and told me to practise until they stopped falling off. They fell off for about a fortnight.

What I did not understand for a long time is why she cared so much about them. Nobody in the audience sees a hook and eye. The costume that took her forty hours — the coat with the collar she rebuilt three times — that is what people photograph. The fastening is invisible by definition. And yet she would check mine, and if the thread was too loose she would cut it out and hand it back.

I found out why in the November of my second year, during a performance.

There is a quick change in Blithe Spirit — it is not really a quick change, it is ninety seconds in the wings in the dark with one torch — and the actress playing Ruth had to come out of one dress and into another with a stage manager and me pulling at her. In the dark I could not see anything. I found the top hook with my thumb and went down the row by feel, and my hands knew the spacing because I had put them there, and she was out of it and into the next one with maybe eight seconds to spare.

That was the moment, though it took me until the drive home to understand it. Val does not care about the hooks because of how they look. She cares because at some point somebody will be undoing them in the dark, at speed, frightened, and the hook has to be exactly where the hand expects it and it has to not come away in the fingers. The invisible thing is the thing under load. Everything the audience admires is being held together by the part they will never see, and the part they will never see is the part that has to be perfect.

It changed what I notice. I started paying attention to the load-bearing invisible things elsewhere — the person who books the hall, the fact that our chemistry technician makes up the solutions before we arrive and nobody has ever thanked her. None of that appears in the photograph either.

I do make whole costumes now. I built the tailcoat for last year's Christmas show and I am proud of it in a straightforward way. But I still do the fastenings, all of them, on every show, because Val is seventy-one and her eyes are not what they were and because I have got fast.

Nine hundred hooks. Ninety seconds each. It comes to about twenty-two hours, spread over three years, in a scout hut with a bad heater. I would not trade it for anything I have done that people can see.
