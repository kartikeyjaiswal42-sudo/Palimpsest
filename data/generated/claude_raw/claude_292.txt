By the time I was fifteen I could introduce myself in under twenty seconds. Name, last city, what my father did, a joke about the school lunch. I could do it standing up, in front of thirty strangers, without my voice shaking. My mother thought this was a kind of talent. Six schools in nine years, she used to say, and look how easily you make friends.

She was wrong, and I let her be wrong, which is the part I keep coming back to.

What I had actually learned was how to be liked quickly and how to leave before anyone found out whether the liking was worth anything. In every new school I ran the same procedure. Find the table that laughs loudest at lunch. Learn three names by Wednesday. Be funny about the teachers, never about the students. Say yes to everything for the first month. By November I would be inside, and by the following August I would be gone, and the leaving cost me nothing, because I had never allowed it to cost anything.

The failure has a name. She sat next to me in the fourth school, the one in Nagpur with the blue corridors, and she was the only person in nine years who ever asked me a second question. Not "where did you move from," which everyone asked and which I had an answer ready for, but, weeks later, "do you miss it," and then, weeks after that, "do you actually like it here or are you just being nice to everybody." I remember being annoyed. I remember thinking she had broken a rule I had never had to say out loud.

She wrote to me for a year after we moved. Real letters, in an envelope, because her family did not have a computer then and mine did. I answered the first two. The third one I read on a Tuesday, put on the corner of my desk, and did not answer. Then a fourth came, shorter, asking whether I was all right. I did not answer that one either. I was busy running the procedure at school number five. Names, table, jokes about the teachers. It worked the way it always worked.

At the time I told myself it was distance. Letters are slow. We would have run out of things to say by March. What I understand now is that answering her would have meant admitting that a place could keep some claim on me after I left it, and I had built my entire self on the idea that no place could. If leaving cost nothing, then nothing had been lost, and there had been a great deal of leaving.

I did not do anything dramatic afterwards. I did not track her down. I looked once, three years later, and found a profile with a photograph too small to be certain about, and I closed the tab. Writing then would have been about me, not about her.

What I did instead was slower and much less satisfying. At school number six I stopped running the procedure. I was worse at that school, socially, than at any of the others. I ate lunch by myself for most of the first term, because I was no longer willing to be instantly charming and being slowly honest takes a great deal longer and looks, from the outside, like having no friends. But the four people I know from that school I still know. When Deven's father was in hospital last winter he called me at eleven at night and I stayed on the phone until two, and it did not occur to me until the next morning that an earlier version of me would have found a polite reason to hang up at midnight.

I have thought about writing to her. I have drafted it twice. Both drafts turned into explanations of myself, which is exactly the problem, so I deleted them.

I am still not good at staying. I moved again last year, for the seventh time, and I caught myself on the first morning scanning the canteen for the loud table. The difference is only that I noticed. I am no longer willing to call leaving a skill.
