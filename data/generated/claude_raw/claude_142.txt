I believed that if I cared enough, I could learn how to do anything. That confidence made me volunteer to sew costumes for Romeo and Juliet when the designer was overwhelmed. I'd watched my mom sew a hundred times. How hard could it be?

Very hard. The first dress was a disaster. I measured wrong, cut wrong, and sewn the bodice so tight nobody could breathe. The second was slightly better. By the fifth dress, I understood how seams worked and why you backstitch. But I was also fourteen days behind schedule and the designer looked panicked whenever I showed up.

I'd been treating sewing like something I could just brute force through. Just keep going until it works. But sewing is precise. You can't will yourself through a quarter-inch seam allowance. You can't just decide that your stitches are good enough if they're not actually good enough. The costumes have to move with the actors or they're going to ruin the performance. There's no compromise space.

I told the designer that I thought I was making it worse, not better. That she should probably give the last few dresses to someone who actually knew what they were doing. She looked disappointed but not surprised, which somehow made it worse. She'd known this wasn't working for a while but was too kind to say it directly.

I quit about ten days before opening night. Someone else came in and finished the dresses in time. They looked beautiful, actually—professional in a way that mine never would have been. I went to opening night and watched Romeo and Juliet move across the stage in costumes that I had failed to create, and I felt this weird combination of relief and shame.

What I got wrong was thinking that confidence and effort were enough. That if I just cared hard enough, I could skip the actual learning part. I'd never sewn before. I didn't have steady hands or good spatial reasoning or years of understanding how fabric moves. I had enthusiasm and stubbornness, which turned out to be exactly the wrong tools for this particular job.

I still believe you can learn how to do almost anything. But I'm more careful now about understanding the difference between something being possible and something being possible for me in the timeframe I have available. Sometimes the bravest thing isn't saying yes. It's knowing when to say you're not the right person for the job.
