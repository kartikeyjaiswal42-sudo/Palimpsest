My grandmother stood at the stove and added salt without measuring. Not a pinch—she used her three fingers to gauge it, a gesture as precise to her as any teaspoon could have been. I watched her make dal, the same way I'd watched her make it for my entire childhood, but this time I was fifteen and I had started to believe that real cooking required precision. I had been watching cooking shows where ingredients were weighed, timed, stated in exact fractions. To me, her method looked like guessing.

I asked her to measure the salt. I said she could write it down so I could learn properly. She looked at me without seeming to understand. She didn't speak much English and I didn't speak her language fluently—another barrier I barely noticed then, just a fact of our relationship. She pointed to the stove. The dal needed attention. The salt was in the dal. This was how you learned to cook, apparently. Not by noting things down but by paying attention.

What I did wrong is I decided she didn't know what she was doing. I didn't think this consciously, but I believed it underneath—that her lack of instruments and measurements meant her method was informal, imprecise, less good than the methods I was reading about. So I started cooking at home when she wasn't there, following recipes from websites. I measured everything. I cooked three versions of dal with different salt quantities to find the "correct" one. I was pleased with myself. I was being scientific about it.

Then one day I made it for my grandmother and she tasted it and said nothing, which was worse than criticism. She went to make a pot of dal herself, using her fingers, that gesture I had started to see as careless rather than skilled. I watched her this time, actually watched instead of judgment watching. I saw that she tasted the cooking water before the dal went in. I saw that she kept the lid off for specific periods because she knew by sight when the moisture had changed. I saw that she added the salt in stages, tasting as she went, adjusting based on what the dish needed in that moment, not what a recipe from the internet had decided it should need.

Her dal was better than mine. It wasn't just salt. It was the entire thing—the depth, the texture, the way the flavors came together. My versions tasted correct, which meant they tasted like nothing. Hers tasted like home and knowledge and something that had been refined over decades.

I think a lot about what I got wrong, which is assuming that my way was more rational just because it was measurable. I didn't see that her precision was real, just not expressed in numbers. She had learned from her mother and her grandmother, refining the method through repetition until it became instinctive. When I measured the salt, I was rejecting not just her technique but her knowledge. I was saying that what she knew didn't count because she couldn't explain it in the language I was learning to speak.

I still cook with recipes sometimes, and I still measure things. But I cook much more with her now, and I've started to understand what she's doing. I watch her hands instead of looking away. I taste as I go instead of following steps. The precision is still there—it's just expressed through attention rather than instruments. She never directly told me this was wrong, what I did. But she let me watch until I figured it out myself.
