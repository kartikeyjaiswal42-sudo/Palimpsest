I sang in the church choir for five years without believing a word of it, and for most of that time I thought this made me a hypocrite. The moment I stopped thinking so happened in a rehearsal in a cold building in February, and it changed more than my opinion of myself.

The facts first. St Cuthbert's, second row, alto. I joined at twelve because my mother sang there and because they paid the choral scholars four pounds a service, which at twelve is real money. I stayed because I liked it. By fourteen I had stopped believing in God, quietly and without drama, the way you stop believing in a rumour when nobody produces evidence. I did not tell anyone. I kept turning up, and I kept singing about a resurrection I did not think had happened, and I felt like a fraud every single Sunday.

I had a private argument running in my head for about three years. It went: these words are claims. To sing a claim is to assert it. To assert something you think is false is to lie. Therefore I am lying, beautifully, in four-part harmony, for four pounds.

Then February. We were learning the Allegri Miserere, which is too hard for a parish choir, and we had been on the same eight bars for forty minutes. Sylvia, who directs, stopped us because the altos were flat on a suspension. She said something I have thought about ever since. She said: you are not holding that note for yourself, you are holding it so the sopranos have something to be dissonant against.

That is a technical instruction, about tuning. But I sat there with my folder open and understood, suddenly and physically, that I had been wrong about what I was doing.

A suspension only exists as a relationship. My note, alone, is neither right nor wrong. It becomes something when it rubs against the note above it and then resolves. Nobody in the building can hear my line as a line. What they hear is the whole chord, which is made of four people each holding something incomplete.

I had been treating singing as a series of statements I personally issued. It is not. It is a structure that four people build together, and the alto part is not a sentence I say, it is a piece I carry so that the thing can stand up. Whether I believe the words is a question about me. Whether the chord tunes is a question about us.

I am not claiming this solved theology. I still do not believe in God, and I do not think singing solves anything about that. What changed was my understanding of participation. I had assumed you could only legitimately take part in something you privately endorsed, that sincerity was the entry requirement for everything. But most of the things human beings do together are not like that. You hold a line so that someone else's line means something. That is not fraud. That is how a chord works, and, I have come to think, how quite a lot else works too.

After that I stopped apologising to myself on Sundays.

I sang my last service in December. Sylvia gave me a pencil, because I had never once brought one in five years. The new thing I understand about myself is that I do not need to agree with a room in order to be honestly of use to it, and I learned that from being flat on a suspension in a freezing church.
