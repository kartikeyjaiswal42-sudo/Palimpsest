Mrs. Okafor's phone had a cracked screen protector shaped almost exactly like a spiderweb, radiating out from a point near the top left corner where she'd dropped it on her kitchen tile the week her son bought it for her. She never replaced the protector. By the time I met her, eight months later, the crack pattern had become, in a strange way, the thing I used to navigate the phone with her — I'd say "tap just above the crack" or "swipe from the corner where the web starts," because those landmarks meant more to her than any menu name I could offer.

I started coming over on Tuesdays because my mother asked me to, initially, in the vague way parents ask you to be nice to a neighbor, and I expected one visit, maybe two. Mrs. Okafor was eighty-one and had moved from Lagos to our street eleven years earlier to be near her son, and her son had, with good intentions, bought her a smartphone and then moved to Seattle for work eight months later, leaving her with a device she said made her feel, in her words, "stupid in my own hand."

The first Tuesday, we spent forty minutes just learning to unlock it. Not because she couldn't understand the concept — she ran a shop for thirty years, she understands systems fine — but because nobody had ever explained it to her the way you'd explain something to a person, rather than a problem. The people at the phone store had shown her once, fast, using words like "swipe up" and "long-press," and she'd nodded because that's what you do, and then gone home and been unable to reproduce any of it alone.

I stopped using their words. I used the crack instead. "See where the web starts, near the top? Put your finger there and drag it down toward you, slow." She liked that I was patient about repeating things, and she told me so more than once, which embarrassed me a little because I didn't think I was being especially patient — I was just going at the speed that actually worked, which happened to be much slower than I was used to going for anything.

By the fourth Tuesday she could video call her son without help. I remember standing in her kitchen doorway, trying to give them privacy, and hearing her laugh at something he said, and hearing in her voice the specific brightness of someone talking to a person they miss and can suddenly, finally, actually see. I hadn't done anything sophisticated. I hadn't taught her calculus or fixed anything broken in a technical sense. I had just kept showing up on Tuesdays and using a cracked screen as a map instead of assuming she should already speak the language everyone else spoke.

We kept meeting on Tuesdays for almost two years, past the point where she needed the lessons, because by then it wasn't really about the phone. She told me stories about Lagos, about a market stall her mother ran, about a boat trip she took as a young woman that she'd never told her own son about because he "asks a hundred questions and I only want to tell it once." I got the whole story, slowly, over many Tuesdays, partly, I think, because I'd earned it by being someone who didn't rush her.

I think about what I'd bring to a campus in terms of that cracked screen protector, honestly. Not because I'm particularly good with technology — I'm not, not especially. It's that I learned, sitting in that kitchen, that the gap between someone feeling stupid and someone feeling capable is usually not a gap in intelligence. It's a gap in how patiently someone is willing to meet you where you actually are, landmarks and all, instead of where they assume you should already be. I'd bring that same patience into a lab, a dorm, a group project with someone struggling to keep up — the willingness to find the spiderweb crack instead of insisting on the menu name.
