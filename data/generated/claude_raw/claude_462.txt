There's this thing that happens when someone lives in your house for a year. They stop being a guest and they become a person. That's what I learned from having an exchange student stay with us.

Her name was Akiko, and she was from Japan, and my parents thought it would be great for our family to host an exchange student. They thought it would expose us to different cultures, that she'd learn English, that we'd all benefit from the experience. I thought it would be fine. Probably boring. Definitely an intrusion on my space.

I was very wrong about most of this.

The first few months were awkward, which was expected. Akiko's English was okay but not great. There were communication gaps. We didn't have much in common. I showed her around school and introduced her to my friends, but I kept a lot of distance. This was still a guest situation. This wasn't real life.

By month four or five, something shifted.

We started actually talking to each other. Not about school or Japanese culture or anything performative like that, but about the actual things we cared about. She was interested in chemistry. I was interested in music. We'd stay up late and talk about our families and our worries and our hopes for the future. She'd make me tea the way she liked it. I'd help her with homework. We developed inside jokes. We had an actual relationship that went beyond the surface-level exchange student experience.

By month ten, I had completely forgotten that she was temporary.

I think that's the thing that got me wrong. I'd thought of hosting an exchange student as this discrete experience—a year-long project where we'd learn about each other and then she'd go home and life would return to normal. But human relationships don't work that way. You can't spend a year living with someone, eating meals with them, supporting them through difficult times, and then just return to normal when they leave.

When she left, it was one of the hardest things I've experienced.

Her last week, I was in this constant state of low-level sadness that I couldn't quite manage. We did all these farewell activities, saw her favorite places one more time, took a million photos. But nothing really prepared me for the fact that she was actually leaving and I wouldn't see her for years, if ever.

The morning she left, I didn't cry until she was actually gone. Then I cried for probably three hours straight. My parents didn't really know what to do with how devastated I was. They tried to make it better by pointing out that we could stay in touch, that it was just a year and she'd go on to her next experience and her life would move forward. All of that was true. None of it made me feel better.

What I got wrong was thinking I could protect myself from missing her by not getting too close in the first place. By the time I realized I'd failed to protect myself, it was because I'd already fallen into a real friendship with her. So the loss was genuine.

I still talk to Akiko, though less frequently now. She's in university and her life is busy. We're still important to each other, but we're also living completely separate lives. Every time we do talk, there's this moment where I remember how much I missed her after she left and how much she meant to me.

I think about what that year taught me about connection and loss and the fact that you can't actually protect yourself from caring about people by staying distant. The caring happens whether you're trying for it or not.
