Our robot's arm dropped off. Not metaphorically — the actual servo-mounted arm, the one we'd spent four months getting to grip and lift a foam block, detached at the shoulder joint and clattered onto the stage floor in front of maybe two hundred people, forty seconds into our qualifying match at the regional competition, and I remember the specific sound it made, a flat plastic clack that the microphones definitely picked up, because someone on another team laughed, not meanly, just startled, and I heard that laugh and felt my whole face go hot.

I was the one who'd mounted that arm the night before. Late, rushed, everyone else had gone to find food and I'd said I'd finish it, and I used two screws where the design called for four because we were out of the right length and I told myself it would hold for one competition, I'd fix it properly after. It didn't hold. We scored eleven points that round out of a possible sixty-something. We didn't advance.

Here's the part I actually want to talk about, though, because the arm falling off isn't really the story.

My teammate Priya didn't say anything to me about it in the moment. We packed up the robot, drove home in near silence, and I assumed — genuinely assumed — that she was furious, because I would have been, and I spent the whole ride composing the apology I was going to give her once we got out of the car.

I never got to give it, because she got there first, and what she said wasn't what I expected. She said thank you.

I actually laughed, out loud, thinking she was being sarcastic. She wasn't. She said thank you for staying late and finishing the mount instead of leaving it half-built, because half-built was worse than badly-built, half-built meant the whole thing wouldn't have even made it onto the stage, and she said she knew I'd been tired and could have just gone home like everyone else and let the team show up with an incomplete robot to forfeit the round entirely. She said the arm falling off in front of everyone was humiliating, sure, but it was a specific, fixable kind of humiliating, the kind you learn from, versus the shapeless humiliating of not showing up ready at all.

I did not think of it that way. I still don't fully think of it that way, if I'm being honest, because some nights I still replay that clack sound and feel my ears go hot all over again, still angry at fifteen-year-old me for the two screws instead of four. But her thank-you rearranged something in how I hold the memory. I had built the whole event, in my head, as a story about my failure. She was telling me it was also, somehow, a story about my effort, and that those were allowed to sit in the same place at the same time without cancelling each other out.

I think about this a lot more than the actual competition, honestly. I think about how easy it would have been for her to just be angry, and how much more useful it was that she wasn't, and how I still don't fully understand why she chose gratitude over blame when blame was clearly sitting right there, available, deserved even. I've asked her since. She just shrugs and says something like, you did the work, the work just wasn't finished, that's different from not doing it.

I've tried to carry that distinction into other things since — the difference between an effort that fails and an effort that never happens — but I'm not sure I've earned the right to say I've mastered it. Mostly what I've carried is smaller and stranger than a lesson. It's just the memory of being thanked for something I was braced to be blamed for, and not knowing, even now, exactly what to do with that.
