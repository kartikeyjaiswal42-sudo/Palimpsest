There's a pair of work gloves in my closet that I haven't used in two years. They're stained and worn and they don't mean anything to anyone but me. They're the physical object that reminds me of the only volunteer job I was truly terrible at.

I started volunteering at the animal shelter when I was fifteen because I thought I loved animals. That seemed like sufficient reasoning at the time. The shelter was clean and organized and the director was kind. They trained me to handle the animals, to clean the cages, to socialize the dogs. The work gloves came with the volunteer kit, thick leather things with reinforced palms.

Within three weeks, I understood that I was not good at this. Not because I didn't care—I cared a lot, probably too much. But I was nervous around the animals in a way that made them nervous around me. Dogs could sense my anxiety. I would hold a leash too tight. I would talk to them in a tense, uncertain voice. They responded to that uncertainty by pulling, by jumping, by not listening to me.

There was one dog, a rescue named Bear, who had some behavioral issues that supposedly "just needed love." Every volunteer's job was to work with him, to help socialize him, to show him that humans were kind. I was supposed to take him for walks, play with him, build his confidence. Instead, I managed to make him worse. He was already reactive to strangers, and my anxiety made me stiff. He could feel the tension in my hands. The walks became tense standoffs between a nervous teenager and a nervous dog, neither of us helping the other.

The gloves got torn up during those walks. A loose thread caught on something and the leather got pulled. I used those torn gloves for months because I felt like using the damage was admitting that I couldn't do the job right. Every time I put them on, I was reminded that I wasn't cut out for this.

What I didn't do was ask for help or admit that I was struggling. I just kept showing up every Saturday and doing a bad job while pretending everything was fine. I would clean cages—which I was okay at, the repetitive straightforward work—and avoid working with the dogs. When the director asked how things were going, I said fine. I didn't tell her that Bear would see me coming and hide under the furniture. I didn't tell her that I was making things worse.

After four months, I quit. I told my parents I was too busy with school. The truth was that I was ashamed. I was the person who was supposed to love animals and I had failed at the one job that required it. The gloves went into my closet and stayed there.

Years later, I look back and understand that I got wrong basically everything about that situation. I got wrong what it meant to be good with animals. I got wrong how to admit that I wasn't the right person for a job. I got wrong how to handle my own anxiety in a situation that required calm. Most of all, I got wrong my assumption that having a hobby meant I was automatically good at making that hobby productive in the world.

What I still think about is whether Bear ended up okay. Whether another volunteer managed to help him in ways I couldn't. Whether my four months of making him more anxious actually damaged his chances of being adopted. I think about this more often than is probably healthy for someone who just volunteered for four months three years ago.

The gloves sit in my closet as evidence that good intentions are not the same as good outcomes. That sometimes the most loving thing you can do is admit that you're not the right person for a job and step aside. That I was seventeen and trying to save animals when what I should have been doing was being honest about my own limitations.

I keep them because I'm not ready to forgive myself yet.
