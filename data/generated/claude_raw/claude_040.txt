The motorcycle was a 1978 Yamaha RD200 and it cost me eleven thousand rupees and every free hour of the summer I was sixteen, and at the end of it, it did not run.

I want to be precise about that, because there is a version of this story where it runs on the last day and everyone cheers, and I have caught myself telling that version to people who were not there. It did not run. It has never run. It is in my grandfather's shed right now with a tarp over it.

I bought it from a man in Sarita Vihar who was selling it because it had been sitting in his family's parking spot for nine years and his wife wanted the space. He was honest with me. He said it hadn't started since before his son was born. I heard that as a challenge, which was the first mistake, and I paid him in cash from my tutoring money, and my cousin and I pushed it four kilometres home because neither of us had thought about how you move a motorcycle that does not move.

Then I spent June, July, and half of August on it.

I learned a great deal. I learned to take a carburettor apart and put it back with the same number of parts. I learned that a two-stroke needs the crank seals to hold pressure or it will never idle right no matter what else you do, and I learned this the hard way, over about five weeks, after I had already replaced the plug and the points and the fuel line and the reed valves and the tank, which I lined with a kit that cost more than I told my mother it cost. I learned that YouTube videos of American men in clean garages are not made for a bike sold only in Asia in 1978.

Around the second week of August I hit the thing. Not hard. I had it on the stand and I had kicked it maybe two hundred times that afternoon and my leg was shaking, and it caught for a second and died, and I kicked the side of it with my heel and something plastic cracked. Then I sat on the floor of the shed and I was furious in a way that scared me a bit.

That was the day I changed my mind about what I was doing.

Because what I noticed, sitting there, was that I was not angry about the motorcycle. I was angry because I had told about nine people I was rebuilding a motorcycle, and August was ending, and I was going to have to tell those nine people that I had not. The bike was fine. The bike had been sitting for nine years and would sit for nine more and did not care. The whole summer had quietly stopped being about the machine somewhere around the third week and become about the announcement.

What I did next was I stopped, which sounds like giving up and I have gone back and forth on whether it was. I put the tarp on it. I did not touch it again that year. What I did instead, for the last two weeks of that summer, was write down everything I had actually learned about that engine — the sequence, the torque figures, which bolt on the left case is a different length and will bottom out and strip if you swap them — and it came to fourteen pages in a notebook.

I go and read that notebook sometimes. It is the only real thing I made that summer. I still want to finish the bike. I have not told anyone that I want to, which is different from last time, and I think that is the point.
