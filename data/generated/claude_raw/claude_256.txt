The rain gauge on our back fence is a plastic cylinder about a foot tall, graduated in millimeters, and it is the wrong color now. It came out of the box a clean translucent white. Three summers of sun have turned the upper third the yellow of an old refrigerator, and there is a hairline crack near the seventy-millimeter mark that I sealed with clear nail polish in the spring of my sophomore year because I could not afford a new one and did not want a gap in the record.

I bought it for eleven dollars with money from cutting the Kavanaghs' lawn. My idea at the time was not scientific. Our street floods at the low end, near the storm drain by the Patel house, and my father and Mr. Patel had been arguing for a year about whether it was getting worse or whether people just remembered wrong. I thought I could settle it. I would measure the rain, and then everyone would know.

The first thing I learned is that measuring rain is mostly a chore. You have to read it in the morning before the sun starts pulling water back out of the tube, which means seven o'clock, which means seven o'clock on Sundays and on the day after the winter formal and on the morning we drove to my grandmother's funeral, when I went out in dress shoes and wrote 0.0 in the notebook because zero is data too. Nobody thanks you for a zero. I wrote a lot of zeros. In August of the second year I wrote thirty-one of them in a row, and I remember standing in a wet backyard looking at an empty tube, feeling genuinely insulted.

The second thing I learned took longer. After eighteen months I had enough numbers to make a chart, and I made it, and it did not answer the question. It could not. My gauge started existing in April of one particular year, and the argument was about a decade. I had built a beautiful, careful instrument for a question that required a time machine. I remember the afternoon I understood that. I had the spreadsheet open, trying to force a trend line out of eighteen months of noise, and I realized I was making the data say what I had already decided.

That is the part I actually grew from, though it did not feel like growth. It felt like being caught. I had assumed that carefulness was the same thing as honesty, and it isn't. You can be scrupulous about your morning reading and still lie with the conclusion. So I changed what I was doing. I pulled the county's records back to 1994, which are free and which I had never bothered to look at because I wanted my own numbers to be the answer. I put my three years on top of their thirty. And the honest result is boring: total rainfall on our street has barely moved, but the number of days delivering more than forty millimeters at once has roughly doubled. It is not raining more. It is raining in bigger pieces. The drain by the Patel house was sized for a different distribution.

I showed it to my father and Mr. Patel at a barbecue, on my laptop, badly. They were both partly right and both mostly wrong, which is not a satisfying thing to tell two men holding tongs. But Mr. Patel took a photograph of the chart and sent it to someone on the township drainage committee, and in October they came and cut a second inlet.

The gauge is still on the fence. I check it before school. What changed in me over those three years is that I stopped wanting to be the person who was right and started wanting to be the person whose numbers could be checked. My notebook is dull on purpose now — date, reading, whether the sky was clear, whether I was late. If someone wants to argue with my conclusion, I would rather hand them the notebook than defend myself. That yellowed, cracked, eleven-dollar tube taught me that patience is not a virtue you feel. It is just what happens when you keep showing up for a question long enough for the question to correct you.
