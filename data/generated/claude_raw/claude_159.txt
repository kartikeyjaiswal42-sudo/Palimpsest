The route was my brother Miles's before it was mine, forty-one houses along Delford and Grantham and half of Kessler, and when he left for community college he handed it to me along with the same canvas bag he'd used for six years, the strap gone stiff with salt from a hundred winters of sweat. I was thirteen. I did not expect the route to become the thing that decided what I wanted to study, but it did, and it happened because of a habit I picked up in my first week that I never dropped.

Before I loaded the papers into the bag each morning, I'd sit on the porch steps of the distribution stop and read the "Yesterlands" column on page four of the Cutchogue Herald, a little box that ran a clipping from the paper exactly one hundred years earlier. It was supposed to take thirty seconds. I was supposed to be riding. But that column was where I first read about the fire that took the old cannery in 1924, and the fight over whether to pave Grantham Street in 1911, and a missing-persons notice for a girl named Adeline Voss who was never found, printed in 1923 in the same clipped, uncertain type as everything else that week, as if the paper had no idea it was printing a mystery that would outlive everyone involved. I started getting to the stop ten minutes early just to have time to read it before my bag got too heavy to sit comfortably.

I lost track of time on that porch more mornings than I can count, which is a strange thing to say about a job where being late means forty-one people don't get their paper before work. But something about reading a hundred-year-old fragment of my own streets, delivered to me for free every single morning, made the town feel like it had a floor underneath it I hadn't known was there. Grantham Street wasn't just the street with the pothole by the Merricks' driveway. It was a street people had once argued about hard enough to fill a column. Adeline Voss wasn't a name. She was a person whose disappearance I couldn't stop thinking about on a Tuesday in October, ninety-nine years after the fact, while pedaling past the exact intersection where she was last seen according to the clipping.

Eventually I did what anyone that obsessed does — I biked, on a non-route day, to the Cutchogue Historical Society, which turned out to be one room above the library staffed three afternoons a week by a woman named Ruth Petrosky who has been answering my questions for almost three years now. She's the person I go to when a name from the column doesn't turn up anywhere else. She showed me the actual bound volumes the "Yesterlands" clippings are pulled from, let me sit with them, and eventually let me write two of the columns myself, both about the cannery fire, both fact-checked against fire department records she keeps in a drawer that smells like the inside of an old church.

I still have the route. Miles is finishing his degree now and asks about it less than he used to, which is fair. But I've never told him that the bag he handed me turned into the reason I want to study history and archives instead of whatever I assumed I'd study at thirteen. Some mornings I still get to the stop early, sit on the same steps, and read whatever hundred-year-old fragment the paper decided to print that day, before I ever open the bag.
