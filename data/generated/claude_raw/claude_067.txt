In the semifinal round of state, a sophomore from a school I'd never heard of took my case apart in four minutes using almost nothing but questions. I was arguing that mandatory minimum sentencing should be abolished, a position I'd defended maybe thirty times that season and had genuinely stopped listening to, in the sense that I knew all its moves so well I'd stopped hearing what it actually claimed. She asked me, calmly, what I would replace it with, and I gave the answer I always gave, judicial discretion, and she asked how I'd stop that discretion from reproducing the exact racial disparities the mandatory minimums were originally written to prevent. I didn't have an answer. I had a rebuttal, which is a different thing, and it showed.

I lost that round on a two-to-one decision, and one judge's ballot said something I still have taped inside my binder: "Confident delivery of a case that never met its strongest objection." I'd been coached for three years to build cases that were hard to attack. Nobody had taught me to build a case that survived actually being right about something I'd rather not be right about, which is what she'd done to me.

What I'd bring to your campus is the version of arguing I built after that loss, which starts from the opposite instinct than the one I walked in with. Before every round now, before I write a single line of my own case, I write my opponent's best question first, the one I'm most afraid of, and I make myself answer it honestly before I'm allowed to write anything persuasive. Some of my current teammates think this is slower than it needs to be, and they're right that it is slower. I've tried to bring it into our club's practice rounds anyway, not as a rule, just as a habit I model, because I think most debate teaches people to be unbeatable and I'd rather learn to be correct, which sometimes means losing well instead of winning badly.

I still think about that sophomore's question more than I think about almost anything else from four years of tournaments. Not because losing to her hurt, though it did, but because she'd clearly built her whole case by imagining mine first and taking it seriously. I want to bring that discipline into rooms where it currently isn't the norm, classrooms, clubs, wherever people gather to disagree, because I've learned the hard way that being persuasive and being right are not the same skill, and only one of them is actually worth teaching yourself.
