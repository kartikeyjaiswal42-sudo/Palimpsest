The crate is a green plastic tray, about forty centimetres by thirty, with a stamped code on the end and a crack in one corner that somebody has repaired with a cable tie. It holds four kilos of raspberries when it is full. I filled roughly nine hundred of them over one summer at a farm outside Wisbech, and that crate is the reason I no longer believe something I used to believe quite strongly.

The belief was about effort. I would have put it, at sixteen, roughly like this: people who work hard get on, and people who do not get on have, on average, not worked hard. I thought of it as an observation, and I had a decent amount of evidence for it, all of which came from school, where it happens to be true. If you revise, you do better. The relationship is clean.

I took the job because I wanted money for a laptop. The pay was piece rate: a fixed amount per full crate, which I remember thinking was the fairest possible arrangement when it was explained to me. No favouritism, no clock-watching, no reward for standing about looking busy. Pure output.

I was terrible. First day, six hours, eleven crates. There was a woman called Marta, Romanian, in her forties, who did fifty-something in the same time and was talking to the person next to her while she did it.

I assumed I would close the gap. I thought speed was mostly a matter of trying. So I tried. I stopped taking my full break. I worked through the hot part of the afternoon when the sensible people slowed down. By the end of the third week I was up to about twenty-five crates and my back had a specific ache in it that arrived at about eleven each morning.

Marta was on sixty. And one afternoon she watched me for a minute and then came over and reorganised how I was standing.

That is the thing that broke the belief. She showed me that I was picking with one hand and holding the punnet with the other, which halves you immediately; that you clip the tray to your belt and pick with both; that you work the row from the underside up because the ripe ones hang and you can see them against the light; that you do not reach for a single berry, you take three at a time in the same hand. Ten minutes of instruction. My rate went up by half in two days and it had nothing to do with effort. I had been working harder than her all summer and producing a third of what she produced, because she had technique and I had willingness.

And she had that technique because she had been picking fruit since she was nineteen, in three countries, and because somebody had once shown her.

The piece rate did not measure how hard we worked. It measured accumulated knowledge and a functioning back, and it presented the result as a moral fact printed on a tally card at the end of the day. I had thought it was the fairest arrangement possible. It is fair only if you assume everyone arrives with the same equipment, and nobody does.

I do not think the correction I made is that effort does not matter. It clearly does; I did not get from eleven crates to forty by lying down. What changed is that I stopped reading an outcome as a report on a person's character. When I see a number now — a grade, a wage, a league table — I have started asking what else went into it besides the trying, and the honest answer is usually: nearly everything.

I bought the laptop in September. The crate lives under my bed with my football boots in it, which is a bad use of a good crate but I am not getting rid of it.
