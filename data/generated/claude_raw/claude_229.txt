Every Sunday morning since I was seven, my job has been to climb the narrow steps to our rooftop before the sun gets too high and turn the pickle jars. There are usually six or seven of them lined up against the water tank, glass jars with mango or lime or green chili packed in mustard oil and salt, covered with cloth instead of lids so the sun can reach them, and my job is simple: tilt each one, let the oil run over the fruit, set it back down facing the light. My grandfather taught me this before he taught me almost anything else, and for years I thought of it as a chore, something between watering a plant and doing dishes, a five-minute task I did half asleep in my pajamas before anyone else was awake.

Nobody in my family has ever written down how much salt goes into those jars, or how many days of sun a batch needs before it's ready, or how you're supposed to know, just by looking, that the mustard seeds have finally started working instead of just sitting there inert. My grandfather knows by smell. He knows a jar is close by the particular sourness that rises off it when you lift the cloth, a smell I couldn't identify for years except to say it was the smell of Sunday.

What I didn't expect was to become genuinely obsessed with why any of it works. Somewhere around freshman year, turning the same jars I'd turned a thousand times before, I started actually wondering what salt does to a mango at the cellular level, why the oil rises the way it does, why some batches turn out sharp and clean and others turn cloudy and have to be thrown away. I started reading about osmosis and lacto-fermentation on nights I was supposedly doing homework, watching videos from food scientists explaining brine concentration and water activity, and bringing questions to my chemistry teacher that had nothing to do with the syllabus and that she answered anyway, clearly delighted someone wanted to talk about salt gradients before first period.

I started keeping a small notebook by the jars, which my grandfather found funny and never discouraged, writing down dates and weather and how each batch smelled on day four, day eight, day fourteen, trying to find the pattern underneath what he does purely by instinct. He still can't tell me the salt percentage. He can just tell me, correctly, every single time, whether a jar needs three more days of sun or is already gone bad.

I still climb those stairs every Sunday, even now that he moves more slowly and mostly watches from a chair instead of standing over my shoulder. It is still, on paper, a five-minute task. But somewhere inside those five minutes is the reason I want to study food chemistry, the reason an entire Saturday can disappear while I read about fermentation kinetics, the reason I know exactly what a jar of achaar sounds like when you shake it right. It started as something I did without thinking. It didn't stay that way.
