I started the recycling programme in Year 11 because I had counted the bins. There were nine bins in our school and every one of them was general waste. I wrote a proposal, got sixty-two signatures, and by October we had blue bins in the science block, the library, and the sixth form common room, with a rota of volunteers who emptied them on Fridays into the big container behind the kitchens.

In February the head of site services cancelled it. I got a two-line email. Contamination rates were too high, the contractor had begun charging us as if the blue container were general waste, and the arrangement was costing the school money it did not have.

I was furious for about three weeks. I told anyone who would listen that this was exactly the problem — that adults said they cared and then folded the moment it cost forty pounds. I drafted a letter to the governors. I had a paragraph in it about moral seriousness that I thought was very good.

Then I actually went and looked in the bins.

That is the moment. It was a Tuesday, before first period, and I lifted the lid of the blue bin in the library expecting to build my case, and what was in it was a half-eaten pasta pot, three crisp packets, and a can with about an inch of energy drink still in it, sitting on top of the paper. I checked the science block one. Same. I had been emptying these for four months on Fridays and I had never once looked at what I was tipping in. I had been carrying contamination to the container with my own hands and then blaming a man in the site office for noticing.

I had designed the whole thing around access. Put a bin near people and they will use it. I had never designed anything around what happens after, which is the only part that costs money, and I had never checked, because checking would have made the number of bins I could report go down.

I asked for a meeting and I did not bring the letter. I asked what the contractor's threshold actually was. It is five percent. I asked what a single-stream paper-only bin would cost and it turns out nothing, because paper alone has a buyer. So we came back in September with four bins instead of nine, paper only, a slot instead of an open lid so a pasta pot physically does not fit, and a photograph taped to the wall above each one showing what it is for.

It is smaller than what I started. It has run for a year. I check them on Fridays now, before I empty them, and I write down what I find.
