For three years I have spent four hours a day on a bus. Two hours out in the morning, two hours back at night, and if you add it up over three school years it comes to something like two thousand four hundred hours, which is more time than I have spent in classrooms in the same period. I have done that arithmetic more than once, usually late, usually to make myself angry.

The school is in the city. We live in a village forty-one kilometres out, which does not sound far and is far, because the road goes through three towns and the bus stops at all of them and there is a level crossing at Bhatpura where I have sat for twenty-five minutes without moving. I leave the house at twenty past five. It is dark. My mother gets up to boil water and neither of us talks, because there is nothing to say at twenty past five, and I walk the eleven minutes to the stand.

For the first two years I hated it with a completeness I have not felt about anything else. I hated it specifically and in detail. I hated the man who sits near the front and clears his throat. I hated that I could not do anything on the bus because of the road — you cannot read on that road, the potholes take the page away from your eyes — and I hated that my classmates who lived in the city talked about staying after for practice or going to somebody's house, and I could not, ever, because there is one bus at four-fifteen and then there is one at six-forty and the six-forty gets me home at nine.

I want to be honest that this is still all true. None of it stopped being true.

The change happened in November of my second year and it was not a good moment. My father had been ill and there was money trouble and my aunt in the city offered to have me stay with her on weekdays, which would have ended the bus entirely. My mother wanted me to take it. My father wanted me to take it. It was the obvious thing and everyone in the room could see it was the obvious thing.

And I said no, and I did not know why I was saying it while I was saying it, and I had to go and sit outside afterwards to work out what had just come out of my mouth.

What I worked out, sitting on the step, was that the bus was the only part of my day that belonged to me.

That sounds like something you would write in an essay to make a bad thing sound good. I have worried about that. But I think it is actually true, and here is the evidence: the bus is the only place where nobody can ask me for anything. At home I am the eldest and there is my sister's homework and the water and the goats. At school I am being measured from the first bell. On the bus, for two hours, there is no version of me that anyone requires. I look out the window at the same eleven kilometres of cane and the brick kiln with the crooked chimney and the dog that sleeps outside the tyre shop at Rewari junction, and my head does whatever it wants.

Most of what I know about what I actually think, I found out on that bus. Not by reading. By staring. Ideas that have nowhere to go will eventually start talking to each other, and the only condition is that you must be bored for long enough, and nobody in my generation is ever bored for long enough. I was, four hours a day, by force.

I said no. My parents thought I was being stubborn about the village and about them, and I did not have the words in November to explain it, and I am not sure I have them now.

I do not want to be romantic about this. Two thousand four hundred hours is a real cost and it came out of my sleep and my friendships and probably my marks. If someone offered me a school across the road I would take it. I am not going to pretend the bus was a gift.

But I would not be the same person, and I know that with more certainty than I know most things about myself, and any account of me that leaves out four hours a day of looking out a window is not an account of me.
