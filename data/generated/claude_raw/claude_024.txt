The waiting room at the Eastfield Health Centre has eleven chairs, four of them with a wobble, and a poster about shingles that has been there since I was nine. I know it better than most rooms in my life. It is where I feel most like myself, which is an embarrassing thing to admit, because nobody chooses a GP surgery as their favourite place.

It started when I was ten and my mother's shoulder stopped working properly. My father was on shift, my brother was too small, so I went. I remember the receptionist speaking to my mother slowly and loudly, which is what people do when they think the problem is volume. I said, quietly, that she understood English fine, she just didn't have the words for tendons. Then I sat in the room and turned the doctor's sentences into Bengali and my mother's sentences into English, and we all got through it, and afterwards my mother bought me a Twix from the machine by the door.

After that it was just something we did. Every appointment, I went. It became as ordinary as carrying the shopping. I stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own handwriting.

Somewhere around thirteen I got good at it in ways nobody taught me. I learned that "we'll monitor it" needs a follow-up question, always, because it can mean anything from "this is nothing" to "I am worried but not yet enough to say so." I learned that my mother's word for pain, byatha, covers a range that English splits into aching and burning and stabbing, and that doctors decide things based on which one you pick, so I had to ask her extra questions she found irritating. I learned to say "she is asking whether the medicine can be taken with food" instead of "she wants to know if she can eat," because one of those gets a proper answer and the other gets a shrug. I learned to keep my own face still when the news was bad, because my mother reads my face before she hears my words.

That last one is the hardest and I am still not good at it.

What I did not expect is that the room made me. In school I am quiet — not shy exactly, just slow to speak, the person who has the answer forty seconds after the moment has passed. But in that waiting room, with the wobbling chairs and the shingles poster, I am the one who knows what happens next. I know which forms need a date of birth and which need an NHS number. I know that the phlebotomy clinic runs Tuesday mornings. I know how to say, politely, "sorry, could you go back, I want to make sure she's understood the dosage," to a man with twenty years of training and four minutes left in his appointment. I have interrupted consultants. I am seventeen.

I used to think this was a chore I had inherited. Now I think it is the closest thing I have to a home skill — the thing I would still be doing if nothing else about my life had gone the way it went. Standing between two people who both mean well and cannot reach each other, and building, sentence by sentence, a floor they can both stand on.

My mother's shoulder is fine now. She still asks me to come. I still go.
