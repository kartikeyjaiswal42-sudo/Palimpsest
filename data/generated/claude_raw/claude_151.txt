I keep a cracked yellow lid from a peanut butter jar on my key ring, and it has gotten me more questions at airport security than anything else I carry, mostly because there is no obvious reason a seventeen-year-old should be sentimental about a piece of plastic with a hairline fracture running through the middle of it. The reason is a dog named Ranger, who three trainers and two shelters had already given up on by the time he came to live in our garage.

Ranger was a shepherd mix who'd been surrendered twice and returned once, and the intake notes said he was hand-shy, food-aggressive, and unsuitable for a first-time foster, which is exactly the kind of warning my mother, who runs a small rescue out of our house, tends to ignore. He would not take a treat from anyone's fingers. He'd flinch and sometimes snap, not out of meanness but because somewhere before us a hand reaching toward his mouth had meant something bad, and every trainer who'd tried the standard approach — lure with a treat, mark the behavior, reward — had run headfirst into that flinch and eventually written him off as untrainable.

I didn't have a better method. I had a peanut butter jar that had just gone empty, and a dog who wouldn't come near my hand, and an afternoon with nothing else to do. I smeared the inside of the lid, set it on the garage floor a few feet from him, and waited. It took him four days to walk over and lick it without bolting. It took another week before he'd touch the lid with his nose, dry, no peanut butter at all, just because I clicked a sound he'd started to associate with something good happening a few feet away from my hands rather than in them. That lid became the whole hinge of his training. I could ask for a nose-touch to the lid from any distance, any direction, and build everything else — sit, stay, walking calmly past another dog — off a behavior that never once required him to trust a hand near his mouth. The trainers hadn't been wrong about the method. They'd just assumed the method was the only door into the room, when Ranger needed a completely different one.

He was adopted eleven months later by a retired postal worker who still sends my mother photos, and in every one of them, taped above the guy's desk, is that same lid, kept as a kind of relic in his house too. I asked to keep the original, and he mailed me a plastic sandwich bag with it wrapped up like something precious, which by then, to both of us, it was.

What I'd bring to a campus is the instinct that lid represents, which is not patience exactly — patience makes it sound passive, like waiting something out — but a working belief that when a standard method keeps failing on someone, the answer is rarely more of the same method delivered more forcefully. It's usually the wrong door. I've carried that instinct into tutoring a freshman who couldn't sit through a normal reading assignment but could absorb an entire chapter listened to at double speed while pacing the hallway, and into a robotics team where our best engineer thinks in sketches, not spec sheets, and needed the whole group to stop insisting on written documentation before her ideas got taken seriously. I notice, fairly quickly, when a system is failing someone not because the person is failing but because the system only has one door built into it. Then I go looking for the lid — whatever the equivalent turns out to be — because I've watched what happens when you find it. A dog nobody could place ends up asleep on a postal worker's couch, and a piece of cracked yellow plastic ends up meaning more than anything I've ever been formally taught.
