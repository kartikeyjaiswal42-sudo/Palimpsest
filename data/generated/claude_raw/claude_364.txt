There is a spot on the wall above my desk where the paint has gone slightly grey, and it is my fault. For about two years now I have been standing in front of it with a mug of tea, not drinking the tea, and touching the wall with two fingers while I think. My mother has painted over it twice. It comes back.

The problem I am usually thinking about is not a hard one. That is the embarrassing part. In my second year of physics we were given a question about a ladder leaning against a frictionless wall, and we were told to find the angle at which it slips. I solved it. I got the mark. Then, walking home, I realised I did not understand why the wall being frictionless mattered so much when the wall was doing almost nothing anyway, and that I had produced a correct answer by a route I could not have defended if anyone had stopped me in the street and asked. I had matched the problem to a shape I recognised. The shape had been right. That is not the same as knowing.

So that evening I stood at the wall for the first time, and I have been doing some version of it ever since.

The habit is small enough to sound like nothing. Every night before I sleep, I take one thing I supposedly understand and try to say it out loud, to the wall, in ordinary words, with no equations and no jargon, until either I can or I discover that I cannot. That is it. Eight minutes, maybe ten. I do not write it down. I do not tell anyone I am doing it, mostly because "I explain physics to a wall" sounds like the beginning of a bad joke and my sister already has enough material.

What I found, very quickly, was how much of my knowledge was decoration. I could not explain, that first month, why a pushed object stops. I knew the answer — friction, of course, everybody knows friction — but when I tried to say what friction actually was, in words, I ran out after about eleven seconds and stood there holding a cold mug. Same with pressure. Same with why the moon does not fall down, which I had "known" since I was nine and could not defend at sixteen. Same, humiliatingly, with the ladder.

The ladder took me four months. Not four months of work; four months of eight-minute nights, most of which went nowhere. I would get halfway through and hit the same wall — not the painted one — where I could describe the forces but not say why they had to balance in that particular way, and then I would notice it was midnight and go to bed slightly annoyed. Then one night in February I was explaining something completely different, about a book falling off a shelf, and I heard myself say a sentence about how the floor pushes back exactly as hard as it needs to and not one newton more, because it is not deciding anything, it is just being squashed. And I stopped. Because that was the ladder. The whole thing had been sitting inside a sentence I already believed and had never bothered to actually listen to.

I want to be careful here, because I know how this story is supposed to go. I am meant to say that I felt a great light and became a physicist. What actually happened is that I stood in my room at half past eleven feeling extremely stupid for four months' worth of reasons, and then I went and made toast. The good feeling came later and it was quieter than triumph. It was something more like relief — the relief of a thing that had been slightly the wrong shape in my head finally sitting flat.

The habit has changed what I am like. I am slower now in arguments, which my friends find irritating. When someone says something confident about economics or about a person we both know, I have started noticing the difference between a claim I could explain and a claim I have simply heard often enough to repeat. Most of what I believe, it turns out, is in the second category, and I do not think that is a personal failing so much as how people work. But I do think there is a difference between not knowing something and not knowing that you do not know it, and the eight minutes are the only reliable method I have found for moving things from the second state to the first.

My physics teacher, Mr Aduba, once told the class that the purpose of a problem set is not the answers. I nodded along with everyone else. I did not understand what he meant for another year and a half, and I only understood it because I had been standing in front of a wall failing to explain a ladder.

I do not know yet what I will study, exactly. I know the shape of the thing I want to keep doing: taking something I am sure of, saying it plainly, and finding out. My mother says she will paint the wall again before I leave. I have told her not to bother. I would only put it back.
