Mrs. Novak was the first adult outside my family who ever seemed to really see me, which is probably why I believed her so completely when she sat my parents down in the fall of fourth grade and told them that if they wanted my reading scores to catch up, we should speak English at home from now on. She was my ESL teacher, and she had a way of crouching down to eye level when she talked to you that made you feel like the only person in the building. My parents were skeptical. I was not. I remember sitting between them at that conference table, ten years old, translating her advice into Vietnamese for my own mother because I already understood English better than she did, and feeling, for the first time, like I was the expert in the room.

I pushed for it harder than my parents did. My bà ngoại, who lived with us and had opinions about everything, told me flatly that this was a mistake, that a language is not a subject you drop when it becomes inconvenient, that I would regret it. I respected her more than almost anyone, but I was ten and certain, and I told her that Mrs. Novak knew about school in a way she didn't. We went English-only within the month. My Vietnamese, which had been clumsy but alive, started going quiet almost immediately — first the complicated words, then the ordinary ones, until by seventh grade I could understand my grandmother perfectly and answer her only in English, a strange lopsided conversation we had every single day without either of us commenting on it.

For years I didn't think about the disagreement again. My reading scores did climb, and I told myself that settled it, that Mrs. Novak had been right and my grandmother's objection was sentimental in a way that didn't hold up against evidence. The turn happened on a Tuesday in March of last year, in a hospital hallway, when a nurse asked if anyone could help translate for my grandmother, who'd had a stroke that had taken most of the English she'd picked up over twenty years and left her only the Vietnamese she'd started with. I said I could. I walked into that room and opened my mouth and nothing came out that was worth anything — I had the grammar of a small child and none of the vocabulary for what a stroke does to a person, what a doctor needed to know, what she needed to hear from me. My mother had to take over. I stood in the corner of that room and watched my own mother comfort my grandmother in a language I had once decided, on a stranger's advice, that I didn't need.

That's the moment I stopped agreeing with Mrs. Novak, four years too late to do anything but notice it. I don't think she was malicious, or even wrong about the reading scores. But I understand now that she was giving advice about a child's test performance and calling it advice about a child's life, and I was too young and too flattered by her attention to hear the difference. I've spent the past year relearning Vietnamese from my grandmother in the hours she has energy for it, badly, slowly, writing words on index cards like I'm ten again but without the confidence I had back then. She corrects my tones and doesn't bring up the argument we had, though I think about it every time I get a word wrong. I still respect Mrs. Novak. I just don't think I'll ever again mistake someone's confidence for their being right, especially not about something as permanent as what a ten-year-old is allowed to keep.
