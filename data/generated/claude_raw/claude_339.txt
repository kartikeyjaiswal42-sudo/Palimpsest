Freshman year, my English teacher handed back a short story I'd written about a lighthouse keeper who talks to the fog, and at the bottom, in red pen, under a grade I don't even remember anymore, he had written: "You are not a writer. You think in facts. Consider a field that rewards that." His name was Mr. Dunmore, he taught AP Chemistry as well as ninth-grade English, and he had a reputation for being right about almost everything, which is probably why the sentence sat in my stomach for weeks instead of rolling off.

I did not confront him. I did not even tell my parents, mostly out of embarrassment, as if the comment were a grade I'd have to explain rather than an opinion one man had formed on one Tuesday. What I did instead, without deciding to make it a project, was buy a marble composition notebook and start writing one page a night before bed, no matter what. Some nights it was a real attempt at a story. Most nights it was closer to a diary entry about nothing — what happened at practice, an argument with my sister, a description of the exact color the sky turned at six-fifteen in February. The only rule was that I couldn't skip a night, and if I had nothing, I had to write "I have nothing" until the page filled up anyway.

I kept that habit for four years. I know because I still have the notebooks — eleven of them now, stacked on the shelf above my desk, and the eleventh one isn't full yet. It became so ordinary that I stopped thinking of it as proving Mr. Dunmore wrong and started thinking of it as just the last thing I do before I turn off the light, the way some people stretch or read. By junior year I had a piece accepted into the school's literary magazine. By senior year I was editing that same magazine, and a college counselor had flagged my writing sample as the strongest part of my application file.

None of that is the surprising part. The surprising part happened in March, in the school parking lot, where I ran into Mr. Dunmore loading boxes into his car — he was retiring at the end of the year, something I hadn't fully registered until I saw the boxes. I told him, mostly on impulse, that I was the lighthouse-keeper kid, that I remembered what he'd written on that story, and that I'd actually kept writing anyway. I expected him to look blank, or embarrassed, or to make some polite noise and go back to his boxes.

Instead he stopped, set the box down on the trunk, and told me he remembered the story better than he remembered me — said he wrote some version of that comment to at least one student a year, on purpose, because he'd noticed that the students who came back from a comment like that, who kept writing out of spite or stubbornness or whatever it was, ended up better writers than the ones he simply praised. He said praise made people comfortable, and comfortable people stop working at the thing they're already good at. He asked if I minded that he'd done it to me on purpose. I told him I didn't, though I'm still not entirely sure that's true.

What I feel now is not quite forgiveness and not quite the straightforward gratitude you'd expect for a kind gesture, because it wasn't a kind gesture — it was closer to a dare, made carelessly, by a man who couldn't have known which students would take it personally enough to spend four years answering it. But I am thankful for it, in the strange sideways way you're thankful for a hard coach or a cold winter that made you tougher. It gave me a habit before it gave me any confidence, and the habit turned out to matter more than the confidence would have on its own, because confidence can evaporate under one bad critique and a habit just makes you show up again the next night regardless.

I mailed him a copy of the literary magazine before he left, the one with my name on the masthead, with a note that just said thank you, not for what he wrote, but for making me need to prove something to a page every single night for four years. I never heard back. I don't think I needed to.
