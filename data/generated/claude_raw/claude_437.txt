My dad decided we were building a canoe the same summer his father died, using plans my grandfather had kept folded in a drawer for probably thirty years and never gotten around to using himself. I didn't want to do it. I was fifteen, most of my friends were spending June at the lake or working actual jobs that paid actual money, and instead I was in our garage six days a week steaming strips of western red cedar and gluing them to a mold my dad had built out of plywood forms, following instructions written in handwriting that wasn't even his.

I wasn't good at hiding how I felt about it. I complained, dragged my feet getting out to the garage most mornings, checked my phone constantly while we worked, and generally made it clear I thought this whole project was something my dad needed and I was just being made to sit through. About five weeks in, I made an actual mistake — not an attitude problem, a real one. I was supposed to bevel the edge of a cedar strip before gluing it into place, and instead of checking the angle against the form the way we'd been doing for weeks, I eyeballed it because I wanted to get through the afternoon faster, and glued in a strip that didn't match the curve of the hull at all. By the time my dad noticed, the glue had already set. We had to cut the strip back out, which meant damaging two of the strips around it too, wasting a piece of cedar that had cost real money and that we didn't have an easy replacement for, since the lumberyard that stocked it was ninety minutes away.

I expected him to be angry, or at least visibly disappointed, the way I'd been bracing for since the moment I realized what I'd done. He wasn't. He looked at the strip for a long minute, ran his thumb along the bad bevel, and then he just said thank you for being out here every day even though I clearly didn't want to be, and that a wasted piece of wood mattered a lot less to him than the fact that I kept showing up anyway. Then he went and got the replacement wood, and we cut a new strip together that afternoon, and he taught me to check the bevel against the form every single time after that, no shortcuts, but he never brought the mistake up again.

I didn't expect gratitude. I'd been dragging myself out to that garage for weeks feeling sorry for myself, and it turned out my dad had been noticing something completely different — not my attitude, which I knew wasn't great, but the plain fact of my presence, day after day, in a summer he was clearly using to grieve his own father in the only way he knew how. Being thanked for something I thought I'd been doing badly, right after I'd actually done something badly in the most literal, wood-and-glue sense, rearranged how I understood the whole project. It wasn't about the canoe turning out perfectly. It was about him not wanting to do that garage alone.

We finished the canoe in August, and it still has a faint mismatch in the hull line where that strip was replaced, visible if you know to look for it, though nobody else ever has. I think about that afternoon more than almost anything else from that summer — not the mistake itself, which I've made peace with, but the strange gift of being thanked in the exact moment I expected to be scolded. It changed how I show up to things now, less out of guilt about disappointing someone and more because I understand, in a way I didn't before that summer, that just being present for someone during something hard is its own kind of contribution, even when you're getting the actual work wrong.
