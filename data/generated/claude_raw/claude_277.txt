My cousin Priya asked me to be in charge of her wedding day the way you'd ask someone who has never actually run anything before, which is to say she trusted me with far more than I understood I was agreeing to. I was sixteen. She was getting married in June, and because both of our mothers were consumed with a hundred other things that morning, I became the person responsible for making sure my grandmother's jewelry — a set that had come down through three generations, a necklace and earrings that had been worn by Priya's great-grandmother at her own wedding in Amritsar — got from our house to the venue safely, along with about six other small logistical tasks that all landed on me at once because I was the oldest cousin around and everyone assumed, correctly in most cases, that I could handle it.

I put the jewelry box in a cloth bag and carried it around with me all morning while I also tried to coordinate which car the photographer needed to ride in, whether my uncle had picked up the extra folding chairs, and where the priest's assistant was supposed to park. At some point, in the middle of moving between three different conversations at once, I set the bag down in the back seat of my uncle Rajan's car while I helped carry something else, and he left. He was running to pick up ice and extra chairs from a store fifteen minutes away, had no idea what was sitting behind him, and his phone, I found out afterward, had died sometime that morning without him noticing.

For almost two hours, nobody could reach him. My aunt discovered the jewelry was missing around ten, when she went to get Priya ready, and the next ninety minutes are still, honestly, some of the worst of my life so far — my mother's face when I admitted I'd set the bag down and walked away from it, my aunt calling every relative's number she had for Rajan, Priya herself, still in her robe, asking quietly what was going on while trying not to let it show in front of the makeup artist. I remember standing in the driveway convinced I had ruined the single day my cousin had been planning for over a year, over jewelry that wasn't even replaceable, that had a history in our family longer than any of us had been alive.

Rajan came back a little after eleven, jewelry untouched in the back seat exactly where I'd left it, utterly unaware anything had happened. The relief in that driveway was enormous and short-lived, because underneath it I still had to sit with the fact that nothing bad happening didn't mean I hadn't done something genuinely careless. Priya got married on time. The necklace is in photographs from that day that our family will keep for decades. And I still think, more than I'd like to admit, about how easily that outcome could have gone the other way, and how the difference between a story we laugh about now and a family heirloom lost forever came down to nothing I actually did — just luck, just a fifteen-minute errand that happened to end where it started.

What I took from it wasn't really about jewelry. It was about what it means to be handed real responsibility, the kind with consequences that don't undo themselves, and to learn in one morning that being trusted and being careful are not the same thing, that I'd assumed I was the second because people kept treating me like I was. I think what I'd bring to a campus is exactly that lesson, still raw enough that I don't get to forget it: I no longer set important things down and walk away, not literally and not in the larger sense either. I check twice now, on things that matter and things that don't seem to yet. Nobody has to ask me to.
