The moment I decided to learn sign language, I thought I was doing it for Noah, my deaf classmate. I was being kind and inclusive and thoughtful. I was the kind of person who learned sign language for other people. By the time I actually got good at it, I realized I had been completely wrong about why I was doing it and what I was learning.

Noah joined our school in tenth grade, and within a week, I decided that I should learn sign language so that we could communicate. This decision was motivated entirely by how it would make me look and feel—like a good person, like someone who took action to include others. I imagined having conversations with him, and I imagined how that would reflect on me. I was sixteen and self-absorbed in the way that sixteen-year-olds are, and I didn't even realize it.

I started with an online course. YouTube tutorials and a textbook from the library. It was harder than I expected. Sign language isn't just English translated into hand movements. It has its own grammar, its own logic. Words move through three-dimensional space. A single sign carries multiple meanings depending on the direction of movement or the facial expression that accompanies it. I would spend hours trying to get the positioning right, watching videos and rewinding and watching again, frustrated by how much effort this was taking.

After three weeks, I had learned maybe thirty signs. Discouraged, I almost quit. But then Noah caught me practicing in the hallway one day, and instead of being impressed, he looked at my hands and laughed. Not meanly—kindly, but genuinely amused. And then he took my hands and showed me the right way to do it, and his hands were patient but firm, and I suddenly understood that I had been doing this all wrong.

He took over my education. We would meet for twenty minutes after school sometimes, and he would teach me real signs, not the sanitized textbook versions but the way that actually worked, the signs that Deaf people actually used. I stopped learning sign language as a charitable act and started learning it as a language. There was no nobility in it anymore. It was just me, being bad at something, and someone else helping me get better.

Six months in, I realized I had switched motivations so completely that I couldn't recognize my earlier self. I wasn't learning this for Noah or for any other external reason. I was learning because I genuinely wanted to communicate in his language, in his world, on his terms. Not to bring him into my world by translating everything into English. To actually enter his.

It's not that I was a bad person before. I was just a person with mixed motives doing a good thing for reasons that weren't entirely good. And the thing that changed me wasn't any grand moral realization. It was just that I kept showing up, and I got worse before I got better, and someone I respected didn't judge me for either of those things.

Now, two years later, I'm fluent in a way that still surprises me. And when I think about who I was when I started—this person trying to be a hero—I'm grateful that I failed. If I had been good at sign language from the beginning, I never would have learned what it actually means to understand someone else's world. I would have just been performing kindness. Instead, I had to be patient, and humble, and willing to be wrong over and over again. I had to learn the language of failing better.
