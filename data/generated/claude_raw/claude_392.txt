I left St. Brigid's after Year 10. I had been there since I was four.

The way I told it that summer, to anyone who asked, was that I had outgrown it. I said the science was thin, which was partly true, and I said I wanted a school where you could take philosophy, which was also true and which I then did not take. But mostly I said it in a tone. There was a tone I used. Sort of amused. Like I had walked out of a room where everyone else was still sitting down.

My grandmother had gone there. My mother had gone there. Sister Ann-Marie taught my mother chemistry in 1991 and then taught me the same unit out of a folder with my mother's handwriting still in the margins, which she showed me once, and which I told people about later as an example of how nothing there ever changed.

That's the thing I got wrong and I still think about it.

Because what I actually meant, at fifteen, was that I did not believe in God anymore and I did not know how to say that in my house. So I built a case. I collected complaints the way you collect evidence when you have already decided the verdict — the timetable, the uniform, the fact that we did a rosary in October. All of it real. None of it the reason. And I walked out with a story about intellectual freedom that let me avoid ever sitting down with my mother and saying the true sentence.

I have never said the true sentence. I'm seventeen. She probably knows. We don't do that.

The part I keep turning over is the tone. Because those people were not stupid and they were not sitting down. Sister Ann-Marie has a chemistry degree from Galway. Mr. Dolan ran the debate team for twenty years out of his own free periods and taught me how to lose an argument without getting louder, which I use more than anything else I learned anywhere. When I was eleven and my father was ill, the school fed us for a month. Nobody made a production of it. There was just food.

And I repaid that by being clever about them at parties.

I don't think leaving was wrong. I'd do it again. The new school did have better labs, and I needed to be somewhere I could ask a question without it landing as an insult, and I was suffocating in a way that was real even if my reasons were dressed up. Fine. What was wrong was the shape I put around it afterwards. I made it a story about escape, and escape stories need somewhere small and dim to escape from, so I made St. Brigid's small and dim, and it isn't.

So the thing that would make my application incomplete without it is not the leaving. It's that I now don't fully trust my own reasons for anything, in a way I think is useful. When I'm certain about something and the certainty feels good — feels clean, feels like I'm the only one who's noticed — that's exactly when I go looking for what I'm actually avoiding underneath it. It's usually a conversation.

I went back in December for the carol service. I sat at the back. Sister Ann-Marie found me on the way out and said she'd heard I was doing well and that she'd always assumed I'd end up doing something with chemistry.

I said maybe. I don't know if I meant it.
