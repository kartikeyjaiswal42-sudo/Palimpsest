The leash is red nylon, six feet, bought for four dollars at a rescue thrift sale, and about eight inches from the clip there's a lump where I repaired it myself with a fisherman's knot after Otis chewed clean through it in April. I didn't replace the leash. I could have — we have three others in the closet — but I use the mended one on purpose, most days, because I like being reminded that something can break almost all the way through and still hold weight.

Otis is a cattle-dog mix we pulled from a county shelter fourteen months ago, found tied to a guardrail off Route 9 with no collar and a look in his eyes that the shelter worker described to us, honestly, as "a project." We were told this. We adopted him anyway. He is smart in a way that makes training him harder, not easier, because he learns exactly how far he can push a boundary before anyone notices, and he came to us with a level of fear around strangers that six months of consistent work only partly wore down. He is, by any normal measure, a difficult dog. I love him more than almost anything I've ever loved.

My mother did not want to keep him. Not from the start, exactly — she agreed to the adoption, she signed the paperwork — but by month three, after he'd bitten through two leashes, destroyed a couch cushion, and nipped a delivery driver hard enough to break skin, she started saying the word "shelter" out loud, at dinner, like it was a reasonable next sentence in an ordinary conversation. I understood her fear. I did not agree with her conclusion. We had this argument, in different forms, for months.

The worst version of it happened in June, after Otis got loose in the yard and lunged at our neighbor's son, a seven-year-old named Miles who wasn't hurt, not physically, but who cried, while his mother stood on our porch with her arms crossed and my mom apologized on Otis's behalf like he owed somebody something. That night my mom told me, flatly, that we were done, that loving a dog doesn't override the fact that other people's kids have to be able to walk past our yard. I told her she was giving up on him the second things got genuinely hard — a cruel thing to say to a woman who has never once given up on me, and I knew it while I was saying it.

We didn't return him. I want to be honest about why: it wasn't because I won the argument. It was because I begged, and because my dad sided with me by staying quiet in a way that counted as a vote, and because my mom, in the end, could not make herself do it. But she has never agreed that keeping him was right. She still flinches when the doorbell rings. She still keeps the yard gate double-latched in a way she didn't used to, and every time Otis growls at something ordinary — the mail carrier, a bike, a shadow — her face does a specific thing, like she's watching the thing she predicted come true all over again.

We have not resolved this. Otis is better now, genuinely — he hasn't bitten anyone in eight months, he walks fine on that mended red leash, he sleeps against my door most nights. But my mother and I have simply stopped discussing whether keeping him was the right call, which is not the same as agreeing that it was. I love her. She is one of the most careful, protective people I know, and that instinct is exactly what made her want to give him back. I still think I was right. I don't think she's wrong to have wanted otherwise.

The knot in that leash holds. It's not invisible — you can feel the lump if you run your hand down the nylon, and I'm sure it's weaker there than the rest of the line. But it has held for fourteen months, through every walk, every lunge, every moment I was sure it would finally give. I think that's most of what I know about my mother and me right now. Not fixed. Still holding.

Some nights I catch her watching him sleep by my door, and her face isn't afraid then, it's something else, something closer to reluctant. I've never asked her what that look means, because I think some disagreements are better left as they are — not settled, just carried, the way you carry a leash you've already decided not to throw away.
