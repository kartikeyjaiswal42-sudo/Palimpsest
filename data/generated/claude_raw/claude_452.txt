My cousin Nkechi was standing at the window in her socks at six in the morning saying my name in a voice I had never heard her use, and I said what, and she said look, and I did not get up.

I want to be exact about the reason, because I have had four years to be dishonest about it and I have taken most of them. The reason was that I was cold and the room was warm and I had decided, three days into that visit, that I was going to be the one member of the family who was not going to perform amazement about Ontario. My aunt had been telling us for a week that we would love the snow. There is a way that a relative who has emigrated talks to the relatives who have not, and I was fifteen and I had catalogued every instance of it, and I had promised myself I would not give her the reaction.

So I lay there and I said, is it a lot.

Nkechi said, it's everything, and I said, okay, in a minute.

I got up forty minutes later. It was still snowing, so I did see snow, and I want to be clear that nothing was actually lost in the material sense. It was white outside. The cars had shapes I did not recognise. There was a sound missing from the street that I did not have the vocabulary for at the time and now know is the sound of tyres on dry road.

But Nkechi had gone downstairs by then, and my aunt had gone to work, and my mother was on the phone to Lagos in the kitchen, and I stood at the glass door on my own with a cup of tea, and it was fine. It was completely fine. That is the whole thing. It was fine.

Nkechi is nineteen now and lives in Calgary and we are not particularly close, and I have never told her that I think about that morning. I have thought about telling her. There is no version of the sentence that does not sound enormous for what it is — you called me and I did not come, that is the entire content — and she almost certainly does not remember it, because for her it was a Tuesday and her cousin was slow getting out of bed.

What I remember most is not the snow. It is her voice saying my name. She used my full name, which nobody in the family does except when there is something wrong, and she used it because there was nothing wrong, because she had gone up two floors in her socks to get me specifically.

I have been back twice. It snowed both times. The second time I was ready with a whole apparatus of appreciation, phone out, and my aunt laughed at me and said, you people.

There is a photograph from that first morning that Nkechi took from the upstairs window, before she came to get me, and I have it on my phone because she sent it to the family group at 6:04 a.m. In the bottom left there is a strip of the roof and the light is the colour of the inside of a shell. I was awake when she took it. I was eleven feet away.
