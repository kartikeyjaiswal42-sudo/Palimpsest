The key is brass, worn smooth on one face, with a paper tag attached by a loop of green garden wire. The tag says PREP 2 in biro and underneath, in different ink and a different hand, it says LOCK IT. Dr Ferris gave it to me in the March of Year Twelve and I gave it back to her on the last day of Year Thirteen, and in between it lived in the small zip pocket of my bag and I checked for it about four times a day.

I want to explain what that key actually was, because on the face of it a science teacher lending a student access to a prep room after hours is a story about opportunity, and the thing I learned from it was not about opportunity.

The circumstances were unremarkable. I had a coursework investigation on enzyme activity that needed readings taken at intervals over about six hours, which does not fit inside a school day, and I had asked whether I could come in early. Dr Ferris looked at me for slightly too long and then said that early was no good either, and went and got the key. She said three things. Sign the book. Nothing goes on the Bunsen unless I am in the building. Lock it.

For the first fortnight I was extremely careful and extremely useless. I set up beautifully. I labelled everything twice. And my results were nonsense, because I had not realised that the water bath in Prep 2 overshoots by two degrees when you set it from cold and then settles, so my first three runs were at a temperature I had assumed rather than measured. I threw away eleven hours of work.

I nearly did not tell her. I had a version prepared where I said the data was "a bit noisy." What made me tell the truth was, embarrassingly, the tag on the key — LOCK IT, in her handwriting, which was a small instruction from a person who had decided to trust me with a room full of things that could burn a building down. It seemed absurd to be trustworthy about the door and not about the numbers.

She was not disappointed, which I had braced for. She said: good, now you know something about that water bath that I did not tell you, and asked what I was going to do about it. Then she went home. She did not stay and help. I have thought about that a lot. She could have solved it in five minutes and instead she left me in a room with a thermometer and a problem, and I spent the next Tuesday characterising the overshoot, plotting it, and building a warm-up protocol into my method.

That is the thing the key gave me, and it was not access to equipment. It was the experience of being the only person responsible for whether something was true. In a lesson, the answer already exists — it is in the mark scheme, in the teacher's head, in the back of the book. In Prep 2 at half past six on a Thursday, with the corridor lights on the motion sensor going off every twenty minutes so that I had to wave an arm at the door, there was no back of the book. If my figures were wrong, they were simply wrong, and the only defence against that was my own care.

I became a different kind of worker in that room. I started writing down what I actually did rather than what I intended to do. I started recording the failures in the same book as the successes, on the same pages, because separating them is how you fool yourself. I began to find the checking more satisfying than the result, which I am aware sounds like something written to impress an admissions tutor, but is genuinely the change that happened and the reason I want to do this for a living.

I handed the key back in June. Dr Ferris put it in the drawer where the keys live and said she would probably give it to someone else in September. I hope she has. But somebody trusted me with a room, and the room contained no answers, and that turned out to be the entire education.
