# Failures

The brief asks for three essays the detector gets confidently wrong and a theory about why.
Below are those three, plus the larger mistakes made building it — which were more
instructive.

> **The biggest one is not in either list, because it was found by a user rather than by us.**
> An essay written by Gemini was pasted into the interface and the tool returned **0.0%
> machine share, 0 of 18 sentences flagged, median sentence probability 0.019**. See
> [#0 below](#0-it-scored-096-and-then-missed-a-real-essay-completely). Every measurement on
> this page other than that entry was re-run after the fix, so the specific sentences quoted
> in Part 1 are current but the surrounding numbers changed; where a figure moved, the new
> one is given.

Failures are selected mechanically by `scripts/find_failures.py`: highest-scoring human
documents and lowest-scoring machine documents across every held-out set. Nothing was
hand-picked to flatter the tool.

# Part 1 — three essays it gets confidently wrong

Regenerated by `scripts/find_failures.py` after the modern-generator retrain. The three
essays written up here before the retrain are no longer among the confident failures; these
are, and the shape of the failure changed with them.

## 1. A one-sentence ESL essay, called machine with P = 0.977

`ellipse:00109` — ELLIPSE corpus, an English-language learner. The essay contains almost no
sentence-ending punctuation, so the segmenter returns it as a **single "sentence"**.

> "In respond to everything i do think that having a longer summer vacation would be good
> enough to catch up…"

| top contributions |
|---|
| vocabulary richness **+3.67**, doesn't sound like the rest **+1.07**, length vs the author's baseline **+1.05** |

**Theory.** `root_ttr` is distinct words divided by the square root of the word count — a
per-*sentence* measure. Applied to a whole essay masquerading as one sentence, the count is a
hundred times what the feature was designed for and the ratio is inflated by construction.
The essay is not unusually rich in vocabulary; it is unusually long for the thing being
measured.

This is the residue of a harm that was worse before it was found. Three essays of this shape
were being flagged at P > 0.90 driven by `n_words` at **50 standard deviations** above the
training mean. Two guards were added — standardised values clipped at ±5
(`Z_CLIP`), and spans over `MAX_SENTENCE_WORDS` excluded from the document verdict entirely —
and those three now score neutral. This one still gets through because it sits just inside
the ceiling. **The guard reduced the harm; it did not eliminate it,** and the failure lands
where it always lands: on the least fluent writers in the corpus, who are the least able to
contest an accusation.

## 2. A letter to a teacher, called machine with P = 0.976

`persuade:2246059bfc70` — PERSUADE corpus. A student's letter, again segmenting to one span.

> "Dear TEACHER_NAME, Hi my name is STUDENT_NAME, one of your students here at SCHOOL_NAME…"

| top contributions |
|---|
| probability curvature **+2.15**, average predictability **+1.96**, fluent but atypical **+1.21** |

**Theory.** Salutations and sign-offs are the most formulaic text a student writes, because
the form dictates them exactly. "Dear TEACHER_NAME" is maximally predictable to the observer
model, and predictability is our strongest single signal. We are reading a letter's furniture
as evidence of authorship. Anonymisation makes it worse: `STUDENT_NAME` and `SCHOOL_NAME` are
placeholder tokens inserted by the corpus, not words the student wrote, and they are exactly
the kind of token GPT-2 finds unsurprising in that position.

## 3. A TOEFL essay on leadership, called machine with P = 0.953

`liang_toefl:0051` — 5 sentences, non-native writer, 88% of its words flagged.

> "In my opinion, a good leader should have the following qualities: confident and kind."

| sentence | top contributions |
|---|---|
| "Every time he walks in the working group, he should bring a strong feeling of authority…" | **curvature vs the author's baseline +11.26**, length vs baseline −2.22 |
| "In my opinion, a good leader should have the following qualities: confident and kind." | probability curvature **+2.99**, observer uncertainty +0.94 |

**Theory.** One contribution of **+11.26** on a five-sentence essay is the whole verdict, and
it comes from a context feature: how far this sentence's curvature sits from the rest of its
own essay. With four other sentences, "the rest of the essay" is an estimate from four
points, and one unusual sentence makes every other sentence look unusual by comparison. Short
documents are where the in-document features are least trustworthy and we still score them
confidently, which remains wrong. The tool declines to score spans that are too *long*
(`MAX_SENTENCE_WORDS`) and passages that are too short at the sentence level (`MIN_TOKENS`),
but it has no equivalent guard for a *document* with too few sentences to establish a
baseline. That is the obvious next fix and it is not done.

**All three are ESL essays.** That is not a coincidence and it is not an improvement on the
previous set, which was also dominated by them. The aggregate ESL document false-positive
rate fell from 7.3% to 5.8% with the retrain, but the essays that remain wrong are wrong for
structural reasons — segmentation, formulaic furniture, and too few sentences to compare
against — rather than because the writing resembles a machine's.

# Part 2 — four larger mistakes, and what they cost

## 0. It scored 0.96 and then missed a real essay completely

The detector reported an out-of-fold sentence AUROC of 0.960 and a document AUROC of 0.959.
Someone pasted in an essay written by Gemini — an ordinary personal statement about founding
a company — and it returned:

| | |
|---|---|
| machine share | **0.0%** |
| sentences flagged | **0 of 18** |
| median sentence probability | **0.019** |
| highest-scoring sentence | 0.595, against a 0.613 threshold |

**Why this was not a threshold problem.** One sentence sat just under the line, which invites
the fix of nudging the threshold down. Fifteen of the eighteen scored below 0.09. Catching
this document meant moving the threshold into the noise floor, where it would flag human
essays too. There was no operating point that caught it and kept the 0.0% out-of-domain
false-positive rate.

**The actual cause.** The observer is GPT-2 (2019) and every real machine essay in the
training pool was GPT-3.5 output from 2022. The 43 features therefore encoded a 2022 model's
habits, and two of the learned priors were pointing the wrong way for 2026 prose:

- `specificity_rate` carried weight **−0.50 toward human**: concrete detail meant a person
  wrote it. That was true of GPT-3.5, which wrote generically. The Gemini essay named a
  company, a transit station, CAD models, a trade show and a prize.
- On the opening sentence, *distance from applicant prose* scored **−0.86** — it read as
  *more* typical of real admissions essays than real admissions essays do.

This is the same finding the sibling project `truepen` reached independently: good modern
prose has **high** perplexity, so every likelihood-based detector loses its grip on it.

**The fix, and what it cost.** 567 essays were generated from five Gemini-3 checkpoints, 135
of them added to training (see [06-decisions.md](06-decisions.md) for why only 135). The same
essay now scores **72% machine share with 13 of 18 sentences flagged**, and recall on modern
text went from 0% to 71.6% on a checkpoint withheld from training. In-domain AUROC *fell*,
0.960 → 0.924, because the task got harder rather than because anything regressed.
Localisation regressed for real: seam-within-two-sentences 70% → 39%, undiagnosed.

**What it should have taken to find this.** Nothing clever. The README had said for months
that performance against a newer generator was unmeasured and was probably the largest gap in
the project — and then never measured it. Writing the limitation down felt like having
addressed it. A held-out set from a current model would have caught this on day one, and
`modern_unseen_family` exists now so the same thing cannot happen silently again.

## 4. We wrote the machine corpus ourselves. It was worthless.

The first machine corpus was eleven essays composed by hand in three prompt conditions to
read like model output. Trained on that, sentence AUROC was **0.793**, document AUROC was
**0.500 — chance.**

The diagnostic that found it: per-feature AUROC, split by which prompt condition produced the
text.

| feature | our "plain" | our "evasive" | **real GPT-3.5** |
|---|---|---|---|
| `mean_logprob` | 0.532 | 0.425 | **0.724** |
| `frac_rank_top10` | 0.496 | — | **0.683** |
| `mean_entropy` | 0.499 | 0.639 | **0.293** |
| `local_len_burstiness` | 0.505 | **0.840** | **0.279** |

The strongest feature in the whole model was `local_len_burstiness` at 0.840 — on the
`evasive` condition only, where the instruction had literally been *"vary your sentence
lengths sharply"*. It was measuring our own prompt. Against real GPT-3.5 the same feature
lands at 0.279, i.e. on the **opposite side of chance**.

**The lesson, which we did not expect.** Prose *composed* to sound machine-generated does not
carry the statistical signature of prose *sampled* from a model — even when the author is
itself a language model. The detectable signal lives in autoregressive sampling, not in
stylistic choices. A writer imitating machine prose makes conscious decisions about
vocabulary and rhythm; those decisions produce token statistics that look nothing like
temperature sampling from a distribution.

Fix: real GPT-3.5 output for training, hand-written essays demoted to a held-out adversarial
set. Sentence AUROC 0.793 → 0.958, document AUROC 0.500 → 0.961. The hand-written set is now
one of our reported failure modes: **0 of 11 caught.**

## 5. A 0.988 AUROC that was partly a smart-quote detector

The result looked too good, so we went looking for the artifact and found it in the
typography:

| character | human docs | GPT-3.5 docs |
|---|---|---|
| curly apostrophe `’` | 88.1% | 9.7% |
| curly quotes `“ ”` | 72.3% | **0.0%** |
| em dash `—` | 43.6% | **0.0%** |
| digits 0–9 | ≥10% | ≤1% |

The human essays were scraped from college web pages, which render smart quotes; the machine
essays came through an API as ASCII. Worse than a surface feature, this reaches the
likelihood features too — GPT-2 assigns a different token, and therefore a different
surprisal, to U+2019 than to `'`.

Fix: typography normalised at ingest for both classes. AUROC 0.988 → 0.988. **The artifact
was real and it was not what was carrying the result** — but we could not have known that
without removing it, and shipping the number without checking would have been indefensible.

Digits are deliberately left alone: their absence from machine text is a property of the
prose, not the pipeline.

## 6. A length artifact that landed on exactly the wrong people

The document model was given `log_sentences`. It learned a large negative weight — fewer
sentences means machine — because the machine essays in our corpus happen to be shorter
(median 261 words against 642). That is a fact about how Liang et al. generated their data,
not about machine writing.

Its consequence was not abstract. TOEFL essays are short, so the artifact landed hardest on
precisely the group this class of tool is already documented to harm.

**And then the interesting part happened.** We first measured this early, reported that
removing the feature took TOEFL false positives from 40.7% to 19.8% at no cost, and wrote
that down. Later changes — mixed documents added to training, the operating point moved onto
the at-risk population — made that measurement unreproducible, and the write-up went on
asserting it. We caught it by turning the ablation into a script
([`scripts/ablate_length.py`](../scripts/ablate_length.py)) and re-running it. The current
result is **substantially less flattering than what we had written**:

| | with `log_sentences` | without (shipped) |
|---|---|---|
| TOEFL document FPR | **6.7% (3/45)** | 17.8% (8/45) |
| ESL overall document FPR | 7.1% (28/395) | **6.6% (26/395)** |
| domain-shift document FPR | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| In-domain document AUROC | **0.975** | 0.909 |
| weight the fit gave it | **−2.77** | — |

*(Re-run after the modern corpus was added. Every row moved, and two of them changed sign
between runs during this work — which is itself the finding. The feature's value depends
entirely on the length distribution of whichever machine corpus is in the pool, and that
distribution is a property of how the corpus was collected, not of machine writing. A feature
whose sign flips when you change generators is not measuring authorship.)*

Three things are true here and the first two argue *against* the decision we made:

1. **The TOEFL result runs against us.** McNemar's exact test on the same 45 essays:
   **0 fixed by the removal, 5 broken, p = 0.062**. Removing the feature does not help TOEFL
   essays; on this evidence it hurts them, just short of significance.
2. **Aggregate ESL false positives are the one row that favours the removal,** 7.1% → 6.6%,
   and that is 2 documents out of 395. It is noise.
3. **It cost real in-domain accuracy**, document AUROC 0.975 → 0.909.

We removed it anyway, and the reason is not in the table. A weight of −3.09 on document
length means the model's single strongest document-level input was *"short essays are
machine-written"* — which is true of our corpus (Liang's machine essays run to a median 261
words against 642) and true of nothing else. It is a fact about a data-collection procedure,
being used as though it were a fact about writing. The population it misclassifies is not
random: it is exam-condition writing, which is short because the exam is timed, by students
least able to contest an accusation.

So the honest statement is: **the case for removing it is principled, and the measurements
now disagree with it on every row.** We would rather ship 0.909 that generalises than 0.974
that is partly a word-count detector — and a reader who finds that indefensible has a fair
argument, which is why the numbers are printed here rather than summarised. Anyone who thinks that trade is wrong can re-run the script and take the
other arm — which is precisely why the script exists.

## 7. Every caller repaired the library on the way past, so nobody noticed it was broken

`Analyzer.analyze()` — the library's own entry point, the one a reader of this repository
would call — built its document verdict with `aggregate(verdicts)`. Both of that call's
interesting arguments have defaults, and both defaults are wrong for serving:

* `doc_model=None` makes `aggregate` fall back to `DocumentDetector()`, whose `predict`
  returns `stats["max_p"]` — **the single strongest sentence in the essay**.
* `threshold=FLAG_THRESHOLD` is the `0.65` constant used to draw highlight passages, not the
  `0.3004` cut-off that the document model's `share` input was standardised at when it was
  fitted.

So the field named `any_machine_probability` stopped being a document probability and became
a sentence maximum. On one real essay it read **70.9%** where the fitted model reads
**14.9%** — opposite sides of the "likely machine" line, from the same artifacts, in the same
process.

**Nothing caught it, and the reason is the interesting part.** The value is bounded in
[0, 1], it rises when the essay looks more machine-written, and it is fed to the band
thresholds without complaint. It never looked wrong. But more than that: *every caller that
reported a number had privately worked around it.*

* `api/app.py` rebuilt the verdict itself, with a comment explaining that it had to because
  "Analyzer.analyze uses the unfitted fallback".
* `edge/scripts/export_parity.py` did the same, passing the model and threshold by hand.
* `fit_bands.py`, `evaluate.py` and `report_product.py` never touched `Analyzer` at all; they
  compose `document_statistics` and `DocumentDetector` directly.

Which means the published accuracy figures were never affected — they came from the correct
path — but **the broken path was the only one nobody exercised.** And the parity harness,
whose entire job is to prove the JavaScript port reproduces the Python one, was comparing the
port against a Python result the library did not produce. It reported exact agreement, over
359,852 comparisons, while the reference implementation it named was returning a different
statistic.

A reference implementation that each caller quietly repairs cannot be checked by comparing
against it. The fix was therefore not just to pass the arguments:

1. `Analyzer.from_artifacts` loads the document model, once, in construction.
2. Both call-site workarounds were **deleted**, so there is exactly one place the verdict is
   computed.
3. `export_parity.py` now reads `result.verdict` straight off `analyze()` and refuses to run
   if the document model is unfitted — the harness can no longer paper over its own reference.
4. `tests/test_analyzer_wiring.py` asserts the wiring end to end through `analyze()` with only
   the observer stubbed, including a guard-the-guard test proving the fitted and unfitted
   paths genuinely disagree on the test essay. Reintroducing the one-line bug turns three of
   its four tests red.

The lesson is not "pass your arguments". It is that a convenience default on a function whose
whole purpose is to be correct is a trap, and that **a workaround at a call site is a bug
report nobody filed.** Both comments explaining the workaround were accurate descriptions of
a defect, sitting in the codebase, for as long as the defect existed.

## What we would do next

1. **A third vendor.** Training data is now GPT-3.5 and Gemini 3. OpenAI's current models,
   Claude, Llama and Mistral remain entirely unmeasured, and `modern_unseen_family` (36.4%
   on 22 documents) suggests transfer across families is poor. This is still the largest gap;
   it has moved, not closed.
2. **Refuse to score short documents.** Below roughly ten sentences the in-document features
   are too noisy, and that is where the worst false positives cluster.
3. **Down-weight genre scaffolding.** Failures 1 and 2 share a cause: we read the required
   architecture of a school essay as a change of author. Explicitly modelling essay position
   (opening / body / conclusion) would let the model expect a formulaic conclusion instead of
   being surprised by one.
